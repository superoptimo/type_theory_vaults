# Formal Presentation and Metatheory

## 1. Why the book ends with a formal appendix

For ten chapters the book does mathematics "in" homotopy type theory the way a working mathematician uses ZFC: without ever pointing at the axioms. Appendix A admits that this is not enough for three jobs (p. 425):

- **stating and proving metatheory**, starting with logical consistency (no term $a : \mathbf{0}$ in the empty context);
- **building models** (simplicial sets, model categories, higher toposes);
- **implementing** the theory in a proof assistant such as Coq or Agda.

The book gives a concrete reason why consistency is not obvious. If the definition of equivalence were wrong in a way that gave $\mathbf{0} \simeq \mathbf{1}$, univalence would turn that into an element of $\mathbf{0}$. The same worry applies to [[Higher-Inductive-Types|higher inductive types]]: it is not obvious that $\mathbb{S}^1$ as defined behaves like a circle.

Two things must be pinned down before any such question is meaningful (p. 425):

1. the **syntax of terms**, which are the objects $a, A, \dots$ that judgments relate;
2. the **judgments and their rules of inference**, which are the ways one judgment is derived from others.

This is the *checker* view: a proof is a derivation tree, and "being a proof" should be a decidable property. That view is what the rest of this note follows.

## 2. Preliminaries: judgments, contexts, substitution, binding

### 2.1 Three judgment forms

Chapter 1 used two judgments, $a : A$ and $a \equiv b : A$. The appendix makes the ambient context explicit, so the forms become (pp. 426, 431):

$$\Gamma \vdash a : A \qquad\qquad \Gamma \vdash a \equiv a' : A \qquad\qquad \Gamma\ \mathsf{ctx}$$

A **context** is a list $x_1 : A_1, \dots, x_n : A_n$ of *distinct* variables. The judgment $(x_1 : A_1, \dots, x_n : A_n)\ \mathsf{ctx}$ says that each $A_i$ is a type in the context $x_1 : A_1, \dots, x_{i-1} : A_{i-1}$. So a context is a *telescope*, where later types may depend on earlier variables.

Note the book's own observation: informal mathematics leaves the context implicit ("let $x$ be a real number"), and the formal system makes this bookkeeping explicit.

### 2.2 Substitution and binding

$B[a/x]$ is capture-avoiding substitution. The simultaneous form $B[a_1, \dots, a_n / x_1, \dots, x_n]$ substitutes all variables at once. Binding is real: $\alpha$-conversion does not change an expression, so strictly an expression is an *equivalence class of syntactic forms modulo renaming of bound variables* (p. 427). Each variable $x_i$ in a judgment is also bound over the rest of the telescope, namely $A_{i+1}, \dots, A_n, a, A$.

## 3. The first presentation: untyped $\lambda$-calculus plus constants (A.1)

### 3.1 Syntax

$$t ::= x \mid \lambda x.\, t \mid t(t') \mid c \mid f$$

with $c$ ranging over **primitive constants** and $f$ over **defined constants**. Types themselves are terms (for example $\prod_{(x:A)} B$ is $c_\Pi(A, \lambda x.\, B)$).

There are two kinds of defined constant (p. 427–428):

- **explicit**: a single equation $f(x_1, \dots, x_n) :\equiv t$, with $f$ not occurring in $t$, for example $\circ(x,y)(z) :\equiv x(y(z))$;
- **recursive**: equations $f(x_1, \dots, x_n, c(y_1, \dots, y_m)) :\equiv t$, one per primitive constructor $c$, where $f$ may occur in $t$ only in a way that makes $f$ a total function (structural recursion).

### 3.2 Equality is convertibility

$t \downarrow t'$ is the equivalence relation generated by the defining equations, by $(\lambda x.\, t)(u) :\equiv t[u/x]$, and by congruence under application and $\lambda$. There is then a *single* rule for the equality judgment:

$$\frac{t : A \quad u : A \quad t \downarrow u}{t \equiv u : A}$$

Judgmental equality is therefore *type-independent* here. The book states the consequence: this presentation **omits the judgmental $\eta$-rule** $f \equiv (\lambda x.\, f(x))$, because $\eta$ only makes sense once $f$ is known to be a function, which a type-independent relation cannot see. The second presentation restores it.

### 3.3 Type formers as constants plus defined recursors

Each type is introduced by primitive constants (formation and introduction) together with *defined* constants that supply the eliminator, whose defining equations are the computation rules. For $\Sigma$ (p. 429–430): given $C : (\sum_{x:A} B(x)) \to \mathcal{U}_m$ and $d : \prod_{x:A}\prod_{y:B(x)} C((x,y))$, we may introduce $f : \prod_{p : \sum_{x:A} B(x)} C(p)$ with $f((x,y)) :\equiv d(x,y)$. The same schema covers $+$, $\mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{1}$, $\mathbb{N}$, $W$-types and identity types. For identity types the schema is path induction: $f(a, \mathsf{refl}_a) :\equiv d$.

Universes (A.1.1) form a cumulative hierarchy $\mathcal{U}_0, \mathcal{U}_1, \dots$ with $\mathcal{U}_m : \mathcal{U}_n$ for $m < n$ and $A : \mathcal{U}_m \Rightarrow A : \mathcal{U}_n$ for $m \le n$. A **conversion rule** lets us replace a type by a convertible one: $a : A$ and $A \equiv B$ give $a : B$.

## 4. The second presentation: natural deduction with explicit contexts (A.2)

This is the presentation that looks like the rule sets in the Lean or Coq kernel documentation.

**Correction to `book-guidelines.md`.** The guidelines describe the second presentation as a "Tarski-style variant with explicit universe codes". That is wrong. The source says the second presentation is the *inductive natural-deduction-style* presentation, with three judgment forms ($\Gamma\ \mathsf{ctx}$, $\Gamma \vdash a : A$, $\Gamma \vdash a \equiv a' : A$) and universes that stay *à la Russell* ($A : \mathcal{U}_i$ directly, as the Notes on p. 441 confirm). The guidelines' A.4 summary is also off, as section 6 below shows.

### 4.1 Derivations are trees

A rule $\dfrac{J_1 \cdots J_k}{J}\ \textsc{Name}$ lets us derive $J$ once all $J_i$ are derived. These hypotheses are *judgments in the metatheory*, not types internal to the theory. The book's example derivation of $\cdot \vdash \lambda x.\, x : \mathbf{1} \to \mathbf{1}$ uses the rules $\textsc{ctx-Emp}$, $\mathbf{1}\text{-}\textsc{Form}$, $\textsc{ctx-Ext}$, $\textsc{Vble}$ and $\Pi\text{-}\textsc{Intro}$, in that order.

### 4.2 Contexts and variables

$$\frac{}{\cdot\ \mathsf{ctx}}\ \textsc{ctx-Emp} \qquad \frac{x_1:A_1, \dots, x_{n-1}:A_{n-1} \vdash A_n : \mathcal{U}_i}{(x_1:A_1, \dots, x_n:A_n)\ \mathsf{ctx}}\ \textsc{ctx-Ext}$$

with the side condition that $x_n$ is distinct from the earlier variables, and

$$\frac{(x_1:A_1, \dots, x_n:A_n)\ \mathsf{ctx}}{x_1:A_1, \dots, x_n:A_n \vdash x_i : A_i}\ \textsc{Vble}$$

Two details the book flags and that matter for an implementation:

- the premises of every rule contain "just enough" well-formedness hypotheses so that the metatheorem *"if $\Gamma \vdash a : A$ or $\Gamma \vdash a \equiv a' : A$ is derivable, then $\Gamma\ \mathsf{ctx}$ is derivable"* holds, and no more;
- $\textsc{Vble}$ must hypothesize well-formedness of its context, while $\textsc{ctx-Ext}$ need not.

### 4.3 Substitution and weakening are admissible, not assumed

The book states (p. 433) that these need not be assumed. By induction on derivations, whenever the premises are derivable, so is the conclusion:

$$\frac{\Gamma \vdash a : A \quad \Gamma, x:A, \Delta \vdash b : B}{\Gamma, \Delta[a/x] \vdash b[a/x] : B[a/x]}\ \textsc{Subst}_1 \qquad \frac{\Gamma \vdash A : \mathcal{U}_i \quad \Gamma, \Delta \vdash b : B}{\Gamma, x:A, \Delta \vdash b : B}\ \textsc{Wkg}_1$$

with analogous $\textsc{Subst}_2$, $\textsc{Subst}_3$, $\textsc{Wkg}_2$ for judgmental equality. Rules of this kind are called **admissible**.

By contrast, the book *does* assume that judgmental equality is an equivalence relation respected by typing (reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and transport along $A \equiv B$), and that every type and term former is a **congruence**. For $\Pi$ these are the rules $\Pi\text{-}\textsc{Form-Eq}$, $\Pi\text{-}\textsc{Intro-Eq}$ and $\Pi\text{-}\textsc{Elim-Eq}$. The local congruence rules together imply the global $\textsc{Subst}_2$ and $\textsc{Subst}_3$.

### 4.4 Universes

$$\frac{\Gamma\ \mathsf{ctx}}{\Gamma \vdash \mathcal{U}_i : \mathcal{U}_{i+1}}\ \mathcal{U}\text{-}\textsc{Intro} \qquad \frac{\Gamma \vdash A : \mathcal{U}_i}{\Gamma \vdash A : \mathcal{U}_{i+1}}\ \mathcal{U}\text{-}\textsc{Cumul}$$

The system is set up so that $\Gamma \vdash a : A$ implies $\Gamma \vdash A : \mathcal{U}_i$ for some $i$, and $\Gamma \vdash a \equiv b : A$ implies $a : A$ and $b : A$.

### 4.5 The five-part rule schema for every type former

From A.2.4 on, each type former is introduced by (p. 434):

| Rule kind | Role |
|---|---|
| formation | when the type former applies |
| introduction | how to inhabit the type |
| elimination (induction $\mathsf{ind}$) | how to use an element |
| computation | judgmental equality: eliminator applied to an introduction form |
| uniqueness (optional) | judgmental equality: every element is determined by its eliminations |

The $\Pi$-type rules are:

$$\frac{\Gamma \vdash A : \mathcal{U}_i \quad \Gamma, x:A \vdash B : \mathcal{U}_i}{\Gamma \vdash \prod_{x:A} B : \mathcal{U}_i}\ \Pi\text{-}\textsc{Form} \qquad \frac{\Gamma, x:A \vdash b : B}{\Gamma \vdash \lambda(x:A).\, b : \prod_{x:A} B}\ \Pi\text{-}\textsc{Intro}$$

$$\frac{\Gamma \vdash f : \prod_{x:A} B \quad \Gamma \vdash a : A}{\Gamma \vdash f(a) : B[a/x]}\ \Pi\text{-}\textsc{Elim} \qquad \frac{\Gamma, x:A \vdash b : B \quad \Gamma \vdash a : A}{\Gamma \vdash (\lambda(x:A).\, b)(a) \equiv b[a/x] : B[a/x]}\ \Pi\text{-}\textsc{Comp}$$

$$\frac{\Gamma \vdash f : \prod_{x:A} B}{\Gamma \vdash f \equiv (\lambda x.\, f(x)) : \prod_{x:A} B}\ \Pi\text{-}\textsc{Uniq}$$

Note that explicit contexts let the book define $\Pi$ directly from the judgment $x : A \vdash B : \mathcal{U}_i$, with no appeal to $A \to \mathcal{U}$, and $A \to B :\equiv \prod_{x:A} B$ is then just a definition.

The eliminators $\mathsf{ind}_\Sigma$, $\mathsf{ind}_+$, $\mathsf{ind}_{\mathbf{0}}$, $\mathsf{ind}_{\mathbf{1}}$, $\mathsf{ind}_{\mathbb{N}}$ are *primitives with binders in their arguments*. For example, in $\mathsf{ind}_{\sum_{x:A} B}(z.C,\ x.y.g,\ p)$ the variable $z$ is bound in $C$, and $x, y$ are bound in $g$. The book notes this is "superficially similar to, but different from" taking functions as arguments. The $\Sigma$ rule is:

$$\frac{\Gamma, z:\sum_{x:A} B \vdash C : \mathcal{U}_i \quad \Gamma, x:A, y:B \vdash g : C[(x,y)/z] \quad \Gamma \vdash p : \sum_{x:A} B}{\Gamma \vdash \mathsf{ind}_{\sum_{x:A} B}(z.C,\ x.y.g,\ p) : C[p/z]}\ \Sigma\text{-}\textsc{Elim}$$

No judgmental uniqueness is postulated for $\Sigma$ or $\mathbf{1}$ (it holds only propositionally, Cor. 2.7.3 and §1.5). Identity types use *unbased* path induction (A.2.10): the motive $C$ lives in context $x, y : A,\ p : x =_A y$, and the computation rule is $\mathsf{ind}_{=_A}(x.y.p.C,\ z.c,\ a, a, \mathsf{refl}_a) \equiv c[a/z]$.

### 4.6 Definitions and elaboration

The book points out that named constants such as $\mathsf{isequiv}$ are *not* mere abbreviations (A.2.11, p. 437–438). Function composition formally must take its types as arguments:

$$\circ :\equiv \lambda(A:\mathcal{U}_i).\, \lambda(B:\mathcal{U}_i).\, \lambda(C:\mathcal{U}_i).\, \lambda(g : B \to C).\, \lambda(f : A \to B).\, \lambda(x:A).\, g(f(x))$$

Writing $g \circ f$ means *suppressing implicit arguments*. The book names this: "inference of implicit arguments, typical ambiguity, ensuring that symbols are only defined once, etc., are collectively called **elaboration**". Elaboration happens *before* checking a derivation and is not part of the core theory, yet "it is essentially impossible to use any implementation of type theory which does not perform elaboration".

## 5. Adding the homotopical axioms (A.3)

The two axioms that add no new syntax or judgmental equalities are introduced as constants (p. 438):

$$\frac{\Gamma \vdash f, g : \prod_{x:A} B}{\Gamma \vdash \mathsf{funext}(f,g) : \mathsf{isequiv}(\mathsf{happly}_{f,g})}\ \Pi\text{-}\textsc{Ext} \qquad \frac{\Gamma \vdash A, B : \mathcal{U}_i}{\Gamma \vdash \mathsf{univalence}(A,B) : \mathsf{isequiv}(\mathsf{idtoeqv}_{A,B})}\ \mathcal{U}_i\text{-}\textsc{Univ}$$

The book chose to add a constant instead of hypothesizing a variable in each theorem, because it regards the axioms as part of the core theory.

The circle is the worked higher inductive type. It has $\mathsf{base} : \mathbb{S}^1$ and $\mathsf{loop} : \mathsf{base} =_{\mathbb{S}^1} \mathsf{base}$, with an eliminator $\mathsf{ind}_{\mathbb{S}^1}(x.C, b, \ell, p)$ where $\ell : b =^C_{\mathsf{loop}} b$ is a *dependent path*. The first computation rule is judgmental, $\mathsf{ind}_{\mathbb{S}^1}(x.C, b, \ell, \mathsf{base}) \equiv b$. The second is **propositional**: $\mathsf{apd}_{\lambda y.\, \mathsf{ind}_{\mathbb{S}^1}(x.C,b,\ell,y)}(\mathsf{loop}) = \ell$. This asymmetry between point constructors and path constructors is what breaks the uniform inductive-type pattern (§6.2).

## 6. Basic metatheory (A.4)

All of the results below are proved for the *first* presentation (A.1), with similar results claimed for A.2.

**Computation.** $(\lambda x.\, t)(u) \to t[u/x]$ together with the defining equations form a rewriting system. It is **confluent**, so $t \downarrow u$ just means "$t$ and $u$ reduce to a common term". For A.2 the computation rules are written as undirected $\equiv$, but they can be read left-to-right (eliminator applied to introduction form simplifies) to give an operational semantics.

| Result | Statement |
|---|---|
| Thm A.4.1 (subject reduction) | $A : \mathcal{U}$ and $A \downarrow A'$ give $A' : \mathcal{U}$; $t : A$ and $t \downarrow t'$ give $t' : A$ |
| Thm A.4.2 (strong normalization) | If $A : \mathcal{U}$ then $A$ is strongly normalizing; if $t : A$ then $A$ and $t$ are |
| Lem A.4.3 (normal forms) | $v ::= k \mid \lambda x.\, v \mid c(\vec v) \mid f(\vec v)$ and $k ::= x \mid k(v) \mid f(\vec v)(k)$ |
| Thm A.4.4 | For $A$ in normal form, $A : \mathcal{U}$ is decidable; for $A : \mathcal{U}$ and $t$ in normal form, $t : A$ is decidable |
| Cor A.4.5 (consistency) | No closed normal $a : \mathbf{0}$ exists by A.4.3, hence none exists at all |
| Cor A.4.6 (canonicity) | A closed $a : \mathbb{N}$ reduces to $\mathsf{succ}^k(0)$ |
| Cor A.4.7 | "Being a proof" is decidable: "we can always recognize a correct proof when we see one" |

**What fails with univalence and HITs.** None of the above is claimed for the extended system, because "occurrences of the univalence axiom and constructors of higher [[Inductive-Types|inductive types]] never simplify, breaking Lemma A.4.3". The book lists the open points explicitly:

- whether applications of these constants can be given reduction rules that restore canonicity (open);
- there is no schema describing all permissible HITs;
- it is unclear whether computation rules on higher constructors should be judgmental.

Consistency of the extended theory is currently known only through **semantic models**: Voevodsky's model in Kan complexes for univalence, and a model of Lumsdaine and Shulman for higher inductive types.

**Correction to `book-guidelines.md`.** The guidelines said that A.4 covers "admissibility of substitution, canonicity for the base theory, decidability of type checking". In the source, admissibility of substitution and weakening belongs to A.2.2, not A.4. A.4 proves subject reduction, strong normalization, a normal-form lemma, consistency, canonicity and decidability, all for presentation A.1. The guidelines' question about "which structural rules are derivable and which are primitive" is answered by A.2.2: $\textsc{Vble}$ is primitive, while $\textsc{Subst}$ and $\textsc{Wkg}$ are admissible.

## 7. Grounding: what this looks like in code

### Rust: the judgment forms as a checker's core types

The appendix is, in effect, a specification for the data structures and function signatures of a kernel.

```rust
use std::rc::Rc;

#[derive(Clone, PartialEq)]
enum Term {
    Var(usize),                 // de Bruijn index: alpha-equivalence is structural equality
    Univ(u32),                  // U_i
    Pi(Rc<Term>, Rc<Term>),     // body binds one variable
    Lam(Rc<Term>, Rc<Term>),    // λ(x:A). b
    App(Rc<Term>, Rc<Term>),
    // Σ, +, 0, 1, N, =_A, with eliminators carrying binders in their arguments
}

/// Γ ctx: a telescope, where each type is checked in the context before it.
struct Ctx(Vec<Term>);

impl Ctx {
    fn extend(&self, a: Term) -> Result<Ctx, Err> {
        self.infer_universe(&a)?;              // ctx-Ext: Γ ⊢ A : U_i
        let mut v = self.0.clone();
        v.push(a);
        Ok(Ctx(v))
    }
    fn infer(&self, t: &Term) -> Result<Term, Err> { /* Vble, Π-Elim, ... */ todo!() }
    fn def_eq(&self, a: &Term, b: &Term) -> bool { /* Γ ⊢ a ≡ b : A */ todo!() }
}
```

Choices the appendix forces on this code:

- the first presentation has *type-independent* convertibility, so `def_eq` can be a purely syntactic normalize-and-compare, but then it cannot decide $\eta$;
- the second presentation includes $\Pi\text{-}\textsc{Uniq}$, so `def_eq` has to know types to apply $\eta$, and that is the design that real kernels follow;
- using de Bruijn indices turns the book's "expression is an equivalence class modulo $\alpha$" into plain structural equality.

### Lean: the same five-part schema, for an inductive type

```lean
-- formation:   Nat : Type
-- introduction: Nat.zero, Nat.succ
-- elimination:  Nat.rec  (the book's ind_N)
-- computation:  Nat.rec z s Nat.zero ≡ z, and Nat.rec z s (Nat.succ n) ≡ s n (Nat.rec z s n)
#check @Nat.rec
-- Lean's kernel also has definitional η for functions (the book's Π-Uniq) and for structures,
-- while Σ-uniqueness in this book's theory is only propositional.
```

The book's elaboration paragraph (A.2.11) corresponds to Lean's elaborator, which turns `g ∘ f` into `Function.comp (α := A) (β := B) (γ := C) g f` before the kernel ever sees it. The book describes this division, and Lean implements it.

## 8. Synthesis

**What this topic depends on.** Everything in Chapters 1–6 is informal shorthand for derivations in this system. The identity-type rules of A.2.10 are the formal form of §1.12, and the $\mathbb{S}^1$ rules of A.3.2 are the formal form of §6.1–6.2.

**What depends on it.** Any model construction (simplicial sets) and any implementation proof checker.

**Structure of the formal story:**

```
syntax of terms ──► judgments: Γ ctx, Γ ⊢ a : A, Γ ⊢ a ≡ a' : A
                        │
        ┌───────────────┴────────────────┐
  structural rules (Vble)           per-type rules:
  admissible: Subst, Wkg            Form / Intro / Elim / Comp / (Uniq)
        │                                │
        └───────── derivation trees ─────┘
                        │
        A.4 metatheory (presentation A.1 only):
        confluence → subject reduction → strong normalization
        → normal forms → decidable checking → consistency, canonicity
        ✗ lost once univalence / HITs are added (they never reduce)
```

### Focus Areas: `type-theory` and `automated-reasoning`

These are the two tags this topic carries in the book's `.learning-goals.md`. This is the most directly load-bearing topic in the book for the target compiler.

- **Judgments** (`type-theory`, `automated-reasoning`). The three judgment forms are the shared ancestor of a type checker and a proof checker. A derivation tree *is* a proof certificate, and Cor. A.4.7 ("we can always recognize a correct proof") is the proof-checking-is-decidable property that makes a trusted kernel feasible.
- **Contexts, substitution and variable capture.** Telescopic contexts, the side condition of distinct variables, capture-avoiding $B[a/x]$ and admissible $\textsc{Subst}$/$\textsc{Wkg}$ are the plumbing that a Hoare-logic soundness proof and an elaborator both reuse. The book's discussion of which well-formedness premises to include in each rule is also a direct engineering guide for what the kernel must check.
- **Definitional equality** (`type-theory`). The difference between A.1 (type-independent convertibility, no $\eta$) and A.2 ($\Pi\text{-}\textsc{Uniq}$) is exactly the design decision in the kernel's `isDefEq`. The book's observation that $\eta$ needs types is the reason real kernels implement type-directed conversion.
- **Elaboration** (`type-theory`). A.2.11 states the boundary: elaboration (implicit arguments, universe levels) is *outside* the core theory and runs *before* checking. For the compiler this licenses the standard architecture, a trusted small kernel plus an untrusted elaborator that must produce fully explicit terms. The mapping to Lean's kernel versus elaborator is direct. The book does *not* describe metavariables or unification, so that part (Miller pattern unification) has to come from other sources.
- **Trusted kernels** (`automated-reasoning`, `type-theory`). Subject reduction, strong normalization and consistency are the soundness argument for the kernel. The negative result is also useful: adding axioms that never reduce (univalence) forfeits canonicity, so a kernel that wants computation must treat such axioms deliberately.
- **Operational semantics.** Section 6's reading of computation rules as directed rewrites ($\textsc{Comp}$ rules left to right) is the evaluator a kernel runs.

The book has no content on SAT/SMT/CSP or abstract interpretation, so this note does not claim a connection to the `sat-smt-csp` or `static-analysis` areas.
