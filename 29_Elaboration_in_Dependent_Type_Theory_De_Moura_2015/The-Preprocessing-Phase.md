## Why the elaborator needs a translation pass before it can solve anything

By the time you reach Section 3, the paper has already told you what the elaborator must eventually *solve*: a soup of [[Constraints-and-Justifications|unification and choice constraints]] over [[Term-Representation-and-Core-Data-Structures|terms containing metavariables]], processed by the [[The-Constraint-Solving-Procedure|constraint-solving procedure]]. But that soup doesn't exist yet when the user hands the elaborator a *preterm* — the raw, quasi-formal expression the user actually typed, full of placeholders (`_`), implicit-argument sites, ad hoc overloaded notation, and applications that may or may not need a coercion inserted. Something has to walk that preterm and turn it into an actual term-with-metavariables plus a matching set of constraints. That something is the **preprocessor**, described in Section 3.5.

It helps to think of the preprocessor as a compiler's "desugaring" pass, if you're used to that framing: just as a compiler turns syntactic sugar (`for` loops, `?.` optional chaining, list comprehensions) into a smaller, more uniform core language before running type inference on it, the preprocessor turns Lean's rich surface syntax into the paper's core term grammar ($t, s ::= \ell \mid x \mid f \mid {?m} \mid \mathrm{Type}_u \mid t\,s \mid \lambda x{:}s,\, t \mid \Pi x{:}s,\, t$) plus a residual set of constraints that record everything the desugaring pass couldn't decide on its own. The key difference from ordinary desugaring is that the preprocessor's output isn't fully determined — it's allowed to leave holes (metavariables) and defer decisions (constraints) to a later phase, because deciding an implicit argument's value or which overload was meant often requires information that isn't available yet, locally, at the site where the sugar appears.

## The shape of the procedure

The paper describes the preprocessor as "a recursive procedure that, given a preterm and a context, returns a term $t$ (potentially containing metavariables) and a set of unification and choice constraints." Two structural facts about it are worth internalizing before the individual cases:

- **It's the only place that carries a context around.** Everywhere else in the algorithm — the [[The-Constraint-Simplification-Procedure|simp procedure]], the solver's `visit`/`resolve`/`process` machinery — operates on already-closed terms and constraints; contexts have already been folded into the metavariables' types by the time those procedures run. The preprocessor is where a fresh metavariable ${?m}$ first gets created and immediately abstracted over the ambient context $\ell$ (the list of free variables in scope), producing an application $({?m}\ \ell)$. This is the same "apply a fresh metavariable to the whole context" trick from the closed-term-only assignment discipline in [[Term-Representation-and-Core-Data-Structures|term representation]] — every metavariable the preprocessor mints is created with its dependency on the surrounding context baked in from the start.
- **It only creates asserted justifications.** Recall from [[Constraints-and-Justifications|constraints and justifications]] that a justification records *why* a constraint or assignment exists — asserted (from user-provided or preprocessor-generated information), assumption (from a case split), or join (from combining others). Because the preprocessor is translating what the user actually wrote, every constraint it emits is, by construction, asserted directly by that translation — there's no case-split or combination involved yet; that only happens once the solver starts working.

With that scaffolding in place, the paper walks through four concrete jobs the preprocessor does, one clause of [[Term-Representation-and-Core-Data-Structures#The term grammar|the term grammar]] at a time.

## Job 1: applications — the main source of unification constraints

Consider an application preterm that elaborates into $(r\ s)$. The preprocessor has two things to check before this can be a well-typed application: that $r$ actually has a function type, and that $s$'s type actually matches the domain. Concretely:

1. It calls `ensurefun` on $r$'s type to force it into the shape $\Pi x{:}A,\ B$ — coercing or constraint-generating as needed if $r$'s type isn't already visibly a $\Pi$-type. (This is the same `ensurefun` introduced as a support function alongside `whnf`/`unfold` in [[Computational-Behavior-and-Reduction|computational behavior]].)
2. It calls `simp` to try to enforce that $s$'s type $C$ is convertible to $A$.

If $C$ and $A$ are already convertible, that's the end of it — an ordinary [[Higher-Order-Unification|unification]] constraint (or an immediate success) falls out of `simp`, and this is genuinely "the main source of unification constraints" the paper is talking about: nearly every function application in a program generates one of these domain-matching checks.

The interesting cases are when $C$ and $A$ *aren't* directly convertible:

- **If $A$ is known (not stuck) and there's a coercion $c$ from $C$ to $A$** in the coercion database, the preprocessor just rewrites the application outright: $(r\ s)$ becomes $(r\ (c\ s))$. No constraint needed — the fix is decided immediately, at preprocessing time, because there was enough information to decide it.
- **If $A$ is stuck** (its head is a metavariable or otherwise not yet resolved) but there are candidate coercions $c_1, \ldots, c_n$ out of $C$, the preprocessor can't yet know which coercion (if any) will be needed, so it defers: it creates a fresh metavariable ${?m} : \mathrm{abstract}_\Pi\ \ell\ A$, rewrites the application to $(r\ ({?m}\ \ell))$, and emits an **ondemand choice constraint** $\langle {?m}\ \ell : A \text{ in } f,\ j\rangle$, where the choice function $f$ enumerates the alternatives $s,\ c_1\,s,\ \ldots,\ c_n\,s$. The solver will wait to invoke $f$ until $A$'s metavariables are pinned down — at which point, ideally, only one alternative type-checks and no real search is needed. (The symmetric situation, $C$ stuck with coercions available, is handled the same way; the paper notes that the doubly-stuck case — both $A$ and $C$ unresolved — isn't attempted at preprocessing time at all, since there's simply not enough information yet to even enumerate candidates.)

This is the mechanism underneath the three coercion kinds surveyed at the outward-facing level in [[Overloading-and-Coercions|overloading and coercions]] — that article shows *what* a coercion insertion looks like from the user's side; this is *how* the preprocessor decides, case by case, whether to insert one immediately or defer the decision as a choice constraint.

## Job 2: overloading, via the same choice-constraint machinery

Ad hoc overloading is handled by exactly the same apparatus, just with a different flavor of choice constraint. Where a coercion-insertion choice constraint is *ondemand* (wait for $A$ to be determined before even trying alternatives), an overloaded-symbol choice constraint is **regular**: the choice function $f$ produces the different interpretations of the overloaded symbol directly, without necessarily waiting on anything first. This is worth sitting with, because it's a nice small illustration of how much of the paper's design is about *classifying* choice constraints by "when is it safe/cheap to try alternatives" rather than inventing separate machinery per source-language feature — overloading and stuck-coercion resolution look completely different to the user, but to the constraint solver they're the same kind of object with a different scheduling hint attached.

## Job 3: placeholders

The simplest case: a bare underscore `_` written in context $\ell$ is just replaced by $({?m}\ \ell)$ for a fresh metavariable ${?m}$ — no constraint at all, at least not yet. The hole gets filled in indirectly, as a side effect of whatever *other* constraint eventually pins down ${?m}$ (e.g. because it appears as an argument whose type is now known from unifying the surrounding application). This is the mechanism behind the underscore-inferred arguments introduced informally back in [[The-Elaboration-Task|the elaboration task]] — by the time you reach Section 3, "the elaborator infers `_`" has cashed out to nothing more exotic than "a metavariable was created here, and some other constraint happened to determine it."

## Job 4: implicit arguments, split into two flavors

When inferring the type $t$ of a term $r$, if $t$ has the shape $\Pi\{x : A\},\ B$ — a $\Pi$-type whose argument is marked implicit with curly braces — the preprocessor inserts the argument automatically: it creates a fresh metavariable ${?m} : \mathrm{abstract}_\Pi\ \ell\ A$ and rewrites $r$ to the application $(r\ ({?m}\ \ell))$. This is structurally identical to the placeholder case above; an implicit argument site is, mechanically, just a `_` the user didn't have to type.

But there's a second sub-case, and it's the one that connects this section to [[Type-Classes-and-Class-Inference|type class inference]]: if the implicit argument is marked with *square* brackets — signaling that it should be synthesized by the type class mechanism rather than left to ordinary unification — the preprocessor additionally emits an ondemand choice constraint $\langle {?m}\ \ell : A \text{ in } f,\ j\rangle$, where $f$ now invokes the type class resolution procedure itself. The paper is refreshingly direct about what that procedure actually is: "essentially a simple λ-Prolog interpreter, where the Horn clauses are the user-declared instances." That's a precise, almost deflationary characterization of the backward-chaining instance search described informally in Section 2.4 — at the level of the preprocessing/solving machinery, "find a `monoid` instance for `nat`" isn't a bespoke search algorithm bolted onto the elaborator; it's one more choice function, invoked from one more ondemand choice constraint, running a general-purpose Horn-clause resolution loop over the database of declared instances.

## What this buys you: one constraint-search substrate for four different surface features

Step back and the shape of the design becomes clear. Coercion insertion, ad hoc overloading, placeholder inference, and type-class-directed implicit arguments are four features that look, from the outside, like they'd need four different resolution strategies — and Section 2 does present them that way, as separate outward-facing tasks. But Section 3.5 shows that the preprocessor reduces every one of them to the same two primitives: *create a metavariable*, and *optionally attach a choice constraint with a small, well-scoped set of candidate resolutions*. The only real degrees of freedom are (a) whether the decision can be made immediately (a coercion when $A$ is already known) or must be deferred (ondemand, waiting on $A$; or regular, tried right away), and (b) what the choice function $f$ actually enumerates (coercion candidates, overload candidates, or the results of a Horn-clause search). Everything downstream — the priority ordering of choice-constraint categories, the backtracking machinery — is described once, generically, in [[The-Constraint-Solving-Procedure|the constraint solving procedure]], and every one of these four surface features rides on it for free.

That's also precisely why the paper calls the preprocessing/solving split "slightly too simplistic," a caveat the [[The-Constraint-Solving-Procedure|constraint-solving article]] discusses further: choice constraints created here during preprocessing don't just sit inertly waiting to be solved after preprocessing finishes — the solver may interleave visiting them with still-ongoing preprocessing elsewhere in the term (e.g. via [[Tactics-and-Proof-Structuring|tactic blocks]] invoked mid-elaboration), so "preprocess everything, then solve everything" is a useful mental model but not quite the literal control flow.
