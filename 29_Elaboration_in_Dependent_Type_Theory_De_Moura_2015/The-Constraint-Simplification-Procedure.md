## Why elaboration needs a "reduce this constraint" step at all

By the time the elaborator has [[Constraints-and-Justifications|constraints]] on the table — say, a unification constraint asserting that two terms must be definitionally equal — it faces two very different kinds of work. Some constraints are almost self-evidently true or false the moment you look at their syntactic shape: `f a b ≈ f a b` needs nothing further, and `Nat ≈ Bool` is simply wrong, full stop, no metavariable in the world will fix it. Other constraints are irreducibly hard: `?m a b ≈ f (g a) b` might have many solutions for the metavariable `?m`, and deciding which one is correct requires search — the [[Higher-Order-Unification|higher-order unification]] machinery discussed elsewhere in this book.

If the solver treated every constraint as belonging to the hard category, it would be searching blindly through cases that were never actually ambiguous. The engineering answer is to interpose a *simplification* pass between "a constraint exists" and "the solver decides what to do with it": chew through everything that has an obvious, mechanical decomposition — matching applications piece by piece, unfolding definitions, reducing redexes — and only pass along what's left once no further mechanical progress is possible. This is the `simp` procedure. It's the workhorse that keeps the solver's genuinely hard case-split logic from being swamped by trivial structural matching.

Practically, `simp` is called from two places: the [[The-Preprocessing-Phase|preprocessing phase]], which uses it to check that argument types match parameter types as it builds up the term, and the constraint-solving loop itself, which uses it as the first move whenever it picks up a new or updated constraint. It is not a solver in its own right — it never *chooses* between multiple valid solutions. It only ever decomposes, unfolds, or fails outright. Anything left over that still admits choice is handed onward, tagged with a category name that tells the solver what kind of hard problem it's looking at.

## The mechanics: decompose or unfold, never guess

`simp` takes a unification constraint $\langle t \approx s, j\rangle$ — two terms plus a [[Constraints-and-Justifications|justification]] $j$ recording why they're supposed to be equal — and returns either a (possibly empty) set of simpler unification constraints, or throws an error. The empty set means "solved, no further work needed"; a thrown error means the two terms are provably *not* unifiable, full stop — no metavariable assignment could rescue them. The paper is explicit about a sanity property here: if the input constraint contains no metavariables at all, `simp` is a decision procedure — it *must* return either `{}` or an error, since there is nothing left to search over.

The procedure is defined by cases on the syntactic shape of the two terms. It's worth walking through the shape of the pseudocode rather than just listing it, because the ordering embodies a strategy: try the free, structural moves first, and only fall back to unfolding constants (which costs real computation) when nothing structural applies.

- **Syntactic identity.** $\text{simp}\langle t \approx t, j\rangle = \{\}$ — if the two terms are already the same term, there's nothing to do.
- **Reduce, then retry.** If $s$ is $\beta/\iota$-reducible, `simp` first reduces it and recurses: $\text{simp}\langle s \approx t, j\rangle = \text{simp}\langle \text{reduce}_{\beta\iota}\, s \approx t, j\rangle$. This is why `simp` is where [[Computational-Behavior-and-Reduction|definitional equality]] actually gets *used*, rather than merely defined — every redex sitting in a constraint gets flattened out before anything else happens.
- **Matching free-variable-headed applications.** $\text{simp}\langle \ell\, s_1 \dots s_n \approx \ell\, t_1 \dots t_n, j\rangle$ decomposes pointwise into $\bigcup_i \text{simp}\langle s_i \approx t_i, j\rangle$ — if both sides are applications of the *same* free variable to the same number of arguments, the only way they can be equal is argument-by-argument, so `simp` just recurses into each argument pair. No search needed: the head match is forced.
- **Matching same-constant applications.** $\text{simp}\langle f\, s_1 \dots s_n \approx f\, t_1 \dots t_n, j\rangle$ is the interesting case, because a constant $f$ might be a *definition* that could be unfolded, and unfolding is expensive, so `simp` tries hard to avoid it. If none of the arguments on either side contain metavariables, it just unfolds both sides and recurses (safe, because there's no combinatorial search hiding in metavariable-free terms — this is definitional equality's normal recursive-descent check). If $f$ is not [[Computational-Behavior-and-Reduction|reducible]], it decomposes argument-by-argument, same as the free-variable case, since an irreducible constant is treated structurally, like a free variable, once its arguments *do* contain metavariables. Only if neither shortcut applies — $f$ is reducible and metavariables are present — does `simp` give up on decomposing and hand the whole thing back as an opaque constraint (the **delta** category, below).
- **Matching different constants by definition depth.** $\text{simp}\langle f\, s \approx g\, t, j\rangle$, where $f \neq g$, uses the **definition depth** heuristic: $\text{depth}\, f$ is $0$ for a non-definition and $1 + \max\{\text{depth}\, g \mid g \text{ appears in the definition of } f\}$ otherwise — essentially, how many layers of unfolding separate $f$ from primitive constants. `simp` unfolds whichever side has *greater* depth (on the reasonable heuristic that the more deeply-defined constant is "further" from primitive form and more likely to reduce toward the other side), or unfolds both simultaneously if the depths tie. If either side is marked irreducible, this whole branch is skipped, and the constraint falls through to the catch-all case below — it cannot ever be resolved by unfolding, since irreducible means exactly "don't unfold this to prove equality."
- **Matching binders.** $\lambda$- and $\Pi$-terms decompose by introducing a fresh free variable $\ell$ for the bound variable (via `mklocal`) and recursing on the domain types and the bodies with that variable substituted in: $\text{simp}\langle A \approx B, j\rangle \cup \text{simp}\langle s[x{:=}\ell] \approx t[y{:=}\ell], j\rangle$. This is ordinary alpha-aware structural comparison of binders — compare the domains, then compare the bodies under a shared fresh name so bound-variable naming can't cause a spurious mismatch.
- **Eta-like matching.** $\text{simp}\langle s \approx (\lambda x{:}B, t), j\rangle$, where $s$ isn't already a $\lambda$, uses `ensurefun` to confirm $s$'s type has the shape $\Pi x{:}A, C$, then rewrites $s$ as $\lambda x{:}A, s\, x$ and recurses — an eta-expansion move so that both sides of the constraint are lambdas before the binder case above applies.
- **The catch-all.** $\text{simp}\langle s \approx t, j\rangle = \{\langle s \approx t, j\rangle\}$ if either $s$ or $t$ is *stuck* (its head is a metavariable, or it's a term whose reduction is blocked pending some other piece of information — see [[Computational-Behavior-and-Reduction]] for what "stuck" means precisely), and otherwise `error j` — the two terms have definite, mismatched head shapes with nothing left to try, so the constraint is simply unsatisfiable.

One detail worth flagging because it shows the paper's practical, not just theoretical, orientation: the "same constant" case above has an implementation-only optimization. When $f$'s arguments contain no metavariables and $f$ isn't a projection, the real Lean implementation tries recursing into each argument pair *first*, and only falls back to unfolding $f$ if that fails. Unfolding is the expensive move; matching structurally is cheap; try cheap first, use expensive only as a fallback. This mirrors a broader theme in the paper — algorithmic *fidelity* to the formal procedure matters, but so does not paying for work you don't need.

## Not being able to decompose further is information, not failure

The genuinely important design point is what happens to a constraint that `simp` can't fully reduce to `{}` or an error: it lands in the catch-all case, gets tagged with *why* it's stuck, and is handed back as one of a small fixed set of **categories**. This categorization is `simp`'s real deliverable to the rest of the elaborator — it's how the [[The-Constraint-Solving-Procedure|constraint-solving procedure]] knows, without re-inspecting the term, roughly how expensive and how ambiguous a given leftover constraint is going to be to resolve.

- **delta** — $\langle f\, s \approx f\, t, j\rangle$, where (by construction, since `simp` only produces this case when nothing else applied) $f$ must be a reducible definition. This is "you'll have to unfold $f$ to make more progress, and we're deferring that."
- **pattern** — $\langle ?m\, \ell_1 \dots \ell_n \approx t, j\rangle$, where the $\ell_i$ are *pairwise distinct* free variables, $t$ only mentions variables from that same set, and $?m$ doesn't occur in $t$ (the occurs check). This is a **Miller pattern** — the best-behaved shape a flex constraint can have, because it admits a unique solution by straightforward abstraction: $?m := \lambda \ell_1 \dots \ell_n, t$. See [[Higher-Order-Unification]] for why this shape is special and how Miller patterns make an otherwise-undecidable problem tractable.
- **quasi-pattern** — same shape as pattern, but the $\ell_i$ are *not* pairwise distinct (a variable repeats among the metavariable's arguments). Close to a pattern, but the repeated variable breaks the uniqueness guarantee, so it needs its own, more careful handling.
- **flex-rigid** — $\langle ?m\, s_1 \dots s_n \approx t, j\rangle$, where at least one $s_i$ is *not* a free variable (e.g. it's itself a compound term). This is the general case that Huet's algorithm's imitation/projection case-splitting exists to handle — see [[Higher-Order-Unification]].
- **flex-flex** — $\langle ?m_1\, s \approx ?m_2\, t, j\rangle$: both sides have a metavariable head. The paper is blunt that these are "badly underconstrained" — with two holes and no rigid structure to anchor against, there are usually many solutions, so the solver typically just holds these and hopes other constraints pin the metavariables down first.
- **recursor** — $\langle t \approx s, j\rangle$ where $t$ or $s$ is a stuck recursor (an eliminator application blocked because its scrutinee isn't yet a constructor application). This category exists because recursors need approximate, heuristic treatment beyond classical unification — again, elaborated further under [[Higher-Order-Unification]] and [[The-Constraint-Solving-Procedure]].

The paper notes that the wider unification literature usually lumps *pattern*, *quasi-pattern*, and *flex-rigid* together under one umbrella term, "flex-rigid" — Lean's finer three-way split exists precisely because the solver wants to route each shape differently: patterns get an immediate, unambiguous solution; quasi-patterns get special-cased handling; general flex-rigid constraints go through full case-split search. Coarser categorization would just push that same distinction-making into the solver's dispatch logic instead of `simp`'s.

## How this feeds the rest of the pipeline

The moment a constraint is categorized, the pipeline downstream — the priority queue in [[The-Constraint-Solving-Procedure]] — has a total order to sort by: pattern constraints (essentially free) are handled before quasi-patterns, which are handled before flex-rigid, and so on, roughly increasing in cost and ambiguity. `simp` is also the tool the [[The-Preprocessing-Phase|preprocessing phase]] reaches for directly, every time it needs to check that an argument's type is convertible to a parameter's expected type — that's a unification constraint like any other, and it goes through exactly this same procedure.

The broader shape worth carrying forward: `simp` is a *normalizing*, deterministic pass, entirely free of backtracking or choice. All the genuinely hard combinatorial work — deciding *which* imitation or projection to try for a flex-rigid constraint, deciding what order to explore case splits in — is deliberately kept out of `simp` and pushed into the solving procedure that consumes its output. Splitting the "obviously mechanical" work from the "genuinely requires search" work this cleanly is what lets the solver's search space stay as small as the problem actually demands, rather than growing to include cases `simp` could have ruled out for free.
