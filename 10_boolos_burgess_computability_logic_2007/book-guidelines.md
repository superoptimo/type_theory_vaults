# Computability and Logic — Guidelines

## Header

**Title:** Computability and Logic
**Author(s):** George S. Boolos, John P. Burgess, Richard C. Jeffrey
**Publication:** Cambridge University Press, 5th edition, 2007

**Brief Summary:**
The book presents, with full rigor, the central theoretical results about computability and logic: the equivalence of Turing, abacus, and recursive-function computability; the syntax, semantics, and proof theory of first-order logic culminating in the Gödel completeness theorem; and the limitative metatheorems — the undecidability of logic and arithmetic and Gödel's two incompleteness theorems — together with a wide-ranging selection of further topics (interpolation, definability, decidable and undecidable fragments, second-order logic, nonstandard models, Ramsey's theorem, and the modal logic of provability). Its organizing idea is that computability theory and metalogic are two faces of a single subject, joined through the arithmetization of syntax.

**Intent of the Author:**
Originally written by Boolos and Jeffrey to give students of philosophy, mathematics, and related fields a rigorous grounding in the principal theorems of logic (especially Gödel's theorems) beyond what an introductory course supplies, using the most elegant available proofs and standard notation. In this fifth edition, Burgess restructured the book for pedagogical flexibility — adding end-of-chapter problems and making chapters more independent of one another — while preserving the original aim of complete, readable proofs rather than a mere survey of results.

## Chapter Summaries

### Chapter 1: Enumerability (pp. 3–15)

**Summary:** This chapter lays the groundwork for the whole book by distinguishing enumerable (countable) from nonenumerable sets, and shows through a long series of examples how surprisingly many infinite sets — pairs, tuples, rationals, finite sequences, finite sets, finite strings over an alphabet — turn out to be enumerable via explicit coding/decoding functions. : [[Enumerability-and-the-Infinite|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **1.1 Enumerability** — enumerable/countable set (members can be arranged in a list, i.e. associated with the positive integers $1,2,3,\ldots$), the empty set counted as enumerable by courtesy, denumerable (enumerably infinite), acceptable vs. unacceptable lists (every member must appear at some finite position $n$, not "after" all others), list determines a function $f$ from positive integers to set members and conversely, total function vs. partial function of positive integers (domain = all of $P$ vs. a proper subset), redundant/gappy lists still count as valid enumerations, domain and range of a function, a set is enumerable iff it is the range of some (total or partial) function of positive integers. : [[Enumerability-and-the-Infinite|Link]]
- **1.2 Enumerable Sets** — Cantor's zig-zag enumeration of ordered pairs of positive integers, code number / decoding / encoding (functions $J$, $j$ mapping pairs to positions and back), coding of $k$-tuples via iterated pairing, coding finite sequences via positional (base-$b$) notation $a_0+ba_1+b^2a_2+\cdots$, coding via prime decomposition $2^{a_0}3^{a_1}5^{a_2}\cdots$, closure results: subsets of enumerable sets are enumerable, unions of enumerable sets are enumerable, finite strings over a finite/enumerable alphabet are enumerable; correspondence (one-to-one onto function) and equinumerosity between sets. : [[Enumerability-and-the-Infinite|Link1]], [[Uncomputability|Link2]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the definition of enumerability permit both redundant and gappy lists, yet rule out concatenating an infinite list of odds followed by an infinite list of evens?
2. Why is it useful to allow enumerating functions to be *partial* rather than requiring them to be total, and how does this matter for enumerating sets like the even integers or arbitrary subsets of $P$?
3. How does Cantor's pairing argument generalize from pairs to $k$-tuples and then to arbitrary finite sequences, and why does this show the set of finite sequences of positive integers is enumerable even though sequences can be of unbounded length?

### Chapter 2: Diagonalization (pp. 16–22)

**Key Definitions & Concepts (no natural subsections):**
- Cantor's Theorem: the set $P^*$ of all sets of positive integers is not enumerable.
- Diagonalization method: given a list $L = S_1, S_2, S_3,\ldots$ of sets of positive integers, define $\Delta(L)$ by $n \in \Delta(L) \leftrightarrow n \notin S_n$; $\Delta(L)$ cannot equal any $S_m$ in the list.
- Diagonal sequence / diagonal set — the sequence $s_1(1), s_2(2), s_3(3),\ldots$ read off the "diagonal" of the array representing $L$ as 0/1 rows.
- Antidiagonal sequence / antidiagonal set — obtained by flipping each diagonal entry ($1-s_n(n)$); guaranteed to differ from every row of the array, hence not in $L$.
- Reductio ad absurdum structure of the proof: supposing $\Delta(L)$ appears as some $S_m$ yields a direct contradiction ($m \in \Delta(L) \leftrightarrow m \notin S_m$, but also $\leftrightarrow m \in S_m$).
- Terminology conventions: theorem, lemma, corollary, proposition, example.
- Corollary 2.2: the set of real numbers is not enumerable (proved by coding sets of positive integers as decimal expansions).
- Richard's paradox (problem) — illustrates the danger of conflating a rigorously defined enumeration (e.g. of strings satisfying a formal coding scheme) with an informal notion like "definitions in English."

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the diagonal argument need the *antidiagonal* set specifically, rather than the diagonal set itself, to guarantee a set absent from any given list $L$?
2. In what sense is the impossibility of enumerating $P^*$ "absolute" — holding even for an idealized enumerator (Zeus) who can complete infinitely many steps in finite time — and what does this reveal about the difference between enumerability and mere infinite extent?
3. What exactly goes wrong in Richard's paradox, and how does it differ from Cantor's legitimate diagonal argument?

### Chapter 3: Turing Computability (pp. 23–34)

**Key Definitions & Concepts (no natural subsections):**
- Effectively computable function — one for which definite, explicit rules exist that in principle determine the value for any argument, requiring no external information or ingenuity.
- Turing's thesis — every effectively computable function is Turing computable (not provable, since "effectively computable" is an intuitive notion).
- Turing machine — an idealized device with an unbounded two-way tape divided into squares (blank $S_0$/B or stroke $S_1$/1), a finite set of internal states $q_1,\ldots,q_m$, and instructions describing one of five actions per state/symbol pair: erase, print, move right, move left, halt.
- Machine table, flow chart (flow graph), and quadruple representation ($q_iS_jS_kq_l$ or $q_iS_jRq_l$/$q_iS_jLq_l$) — three equivalent ways to specify a Turing-machine program.
- Configuration — snapshot of tape contents, state, and scanned square at a stage of computation (e.g. $1_2100111$).
- Monadic (tally) notation — representing a positive integer $n$ by a block of $n$ strokes.
- Standard initial/final configuration (or position) — official convention for representing arguments and values of a $k$-place function on the tape, scanning the leftmost stroke.
- A numerical function is Turing computable if some Turing machine computes it per this convention.
- Worked examples: writing $n$ strokes, doubling a block of strokes, parity of a block, addition and multiplication in monadic notation (the "leapfrog" routine).

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the choice of numeral system (decimal, Roman, monadic) not affect which functions count as effectively computable, even though it drastically affects practical ease of computation?
2. What is the status of Turing's thesis — why can it be given only heuristic support (e.g. "writing/erasing can be done stroke by stroke") and accumulated evidence, rather than a formal proof?
3. How does the "standard position" convention for initial/final configurations let one read off, from a single Turing machine, an entire family of functions (one for each number of arguments $k$, including the $k=0$ case)?

### Chapter 4: Uncomputability (pp. 35–44)

**Summary:** Having enumerated the Turing machines (via quadruple/prime-power coding), this chapter exhibits explicit Turing-uncomputable functions — the diagonal function, the halting function, and (optionally) the productivity/busy-beaver functions — providing the first concrete evidence for inherent limits on computation. : [[Uncomputability|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **4.1 The Halting Problem** — coding of Turing machines as finite sequences of positive integers (and hence by single numbers via prime decomposition), giving an enumeration $M_1,M_2,M_3,\ldots$ of all Turing machines and a corresponding list $f_1,f_2,f_3,\ldots$ of Turing-computable functions; diagonal function $d(n) = 2$ if $f_n(n)$ is defined and $=1$, else $1$; Theorem 4.1 ($d$ is not Turing computable, proved by diagonalization/reductio); halting function $h(m,n)$ (does machine $m$ halt on input $n$); Theorem 4.2 (the halting function $h$ is not Turing computable), proved via a copying machine $C$ and a dithering machine $D$ combined to derive a contradiction; the halting problem — no Turing machine (hence, by Turing's thesis, no effective procedure) decides whether an arbitrary machine halts on an arbitrary input. : [[Uncomputability|Link]]
- **4.2\* The Productivity Function** — score/productivity of a $k$-state machine; the scoring function $s(k)$ and its uncomputability (Proposition 4.3); the Rado/busy-beaver function $p(n)$ and its uncomputability (Theorem 4.7), proved via a growth-rate argument.

**Key Questions:**
1. How does the proof that $d$ is not Turing computable use a reductio ad absurdum turning on the case $n = m$, and how does this mirror the diagonalization argument of Chapter 2?
2. Why does the unsolvability of the halting problem block the "obvious" procedure for computing the productivity/busy-beaver function (run all $k$-state machines and wait for them to halt)?
3. What is the logical relationship between the halting function $h$ and the diagonal function $d$ — why would Turing-computability of $h$ imply Turing-computability of $d$?

### Chapter 5: Abacus Computability (pp. 45–62)

**Summary:** This chapter introduces the abacus (register) machine, an idealized computer with unlimited random-access registers, as a more flexible-seeming but ultimately equivalent alternative to the Turing machine, and uses it to establish that a broad class of functions built up by composition, primitive recursion, and minimization are computable. : [[Recursive-Function-Theory|Link1]], [[Turing-and-Abacus-Computability|Link2]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **5.1 Abacus Machines** — registers $R_0, R_1, R_2, \ldots$ each holding a natural number $[n]$; elementary instructions $n+$ (add one to box $n$) and $n-$ (conditional: subtract one from box $n$ if nonempty, else branch); flow-chart programs; example programs for emptying, copying, addition, multiplication, and exponentiation of registers. : [[Turing-and-Abacus-Computability|Link]]
- **5.2 Simulating Abacus Machines by Turing Machines** — standard format for abacus computation of a function (arguments in first $r$ registers, value read from a designated register); construction converting any abacus flow chart into an equivalent Turing-machine flow chart (via $s+$/$s-$ subcharts and a "mop-up" routine), showing every abacus-computable function is Turing computable.
- **5.3 The Scope of Abacus Computability** — composition (Cn) and (primitive) recursion (Pr) as function-building operations, each shown to preserve abacus computability; minimization (Mn); Theorem 5.8: all recursive functions are abacus computable (hence Turing computable). : [[Turing-and-Abacus-Computability|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the apparent extra power of an abacus machine (random-access registers of unbounded size vs. a linear tape) turn out not to increase the class of computable functions?
2. How does the abacus program for primitive recursion use a counter register to implement the two recursion equations $h(x,0) = f(x)$, $h(x, y') = g(x,y,h(x,y))$?
3. Why is the "mop-up" step needed when translating an abacus flow chart into a Turing-machine flow chart, given the different ways the two kinds of machine represent halting and represent empty vs. occupied registers?

### Chapter 6: Recursive Functions (pp. 63–72)

**Summary:** Independently of the machine-based approach, this chapter defines the recursive functions as those built from basic functions (zero, successor, identity/projection) by composition, primitive recursion, and minimization, and states Church's thesis that these coincide with the effectively computable functions. : [[Arithmetization-of-Syntax-and-Representability|Link1]], [[Recursive-Function-Theory|Link2]], [[Recursive-Semirecursive-and-Arithmetical-Sets-and-Relations|Link3]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **6.1 Primitive Recursive Functions** — basic functions: zero function $z$, successor $s$, identity/projection functions $\mathrm{id}_i^n$; composition (Cn): $h(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = f(g_1(x_1,\ldots,x_n),\ldots,g_m(x_1,\ldots,x_n))$; (primitive) recursion (Pr): $h(x,0) = f(x)$, $h(x,y') = g(x,y,h(x,y))$; primitive recursive functions (those built from basic functions by Cn and Pr); worked derivations of sum, product, factorial, and the exponentiation/super-exponentiation hierarchy as primitive recursive. : [[Recursive-Function-Theory|Link1]], [[Recursive-Semirecursive-and-Arithmetical-Sets-and-Relations|Link2]]
- **6.2 Minimization** — minimization (Mn): $h(x) = $ the least $y$ such that $f(x,y)=0$ and $f(x,t)$ is defined and $\ne 0$ for all $t<y$ (undefined if no such $y$); regular function (one for which minimization always yields a defined total value); recursive (total or partial) functions — those built from the basic functions by Cn, Pr, and Mn; Church's thesis (and its extended version for partial functions).

**Key Questions:**
1. Why is minimization needed at all — what can recursive functions compute that primitive recursive functions provably cannot (foreshadowing Example 7.23, the Ackermann-type function)?
2. Why does minimization naturally produce partial functions, and under what condition (regularity) is the resulting function guaranteed total?
3. What is the precise relationship between Church's thesis and Turing's thesis, and why does the book treat "recursive," "Turing computable," and "abacus computable" as three independently motivated analyses of the same intuitive notion?

### Chapter 7: Recursive Sets and Relations (pp. 73–87)

### Chapter 22: Second-Order Logic (pp. 279–285)

**Summary:** This short chapter introduces second-order logic — quantification over relations and functions on the domain, in addition to individuals — and shows that essentially every major positive theorem proved for first-order logic (compactness, both Löwenheim–Skolem theorems, Gödel completeness) fails for it, using the categoricity of a second-order axiomatization of arithmetic as the central example. : [[Second-Order-Logic|Link]]

- Individual variables vs. relation variables and function variables (set variables when one-place); second-order formula/sentence (contains at least one relation or function variable); truth in an interpretation for quantified relation/function variables, defined by expanding the language with a corresponding new predicate/function symbol and checking truth of the instance for every possible denotation.

### Chapter 24: Decidability of Arithmetic without Multiplication (pp. 295–301)

**Summary:** Although (true) arithmetic as a whole is undecidable, Presburger arithmetic — the sublanguage of arithmetic omitting the multiplication symbol $\cdot$ — is decidable, and this chapter proves Presburger's theorem in full via an explicit 30-step quantifier-elimination procedure (Skolem's dual theorem, decidability of arithmetic without addition, is stated but not proved). : [[Definability-in-Arithmetic-and-Forcing|Link1]], [[Decidable-Fragments-of-Arithmetic|Link2]], [[Recursive-Semirecursive-and-Arithmetical-Sets-and-Relations|Link3]]

**Key Concepts:**
- Arithmetic without multiplication / Presburger arithmetic — the set of sentences of true arithmetic containing no $\cdot$; Presburger's theorem asserts this set is recursive.
- Arithmetic without addition (Skolem's theorem) — the sentences omitting $<, {}', +$; decidable because $+$ is definable from $\cdot$ and ${}'$ is definable from $<$.
- Reduction to the language $K$ — constants $0, 1$, unary predicates $D_2, D_3, \ldots$ (divisibility by $n$), $<$, $+$, $\dot- $ (truncated subtraction), interpreted over all integers in the model $M$; a sentence $S$ of $L$ without $\cdot$ is true in $\mathcal{N}$ iff its translate $S^*$ (relativizing quantifiers to $x=0 \lor 0<x$) is true in $M$.
- Coextensive terms/formulas — terms $r,s$ (or formulas $F,G$) that are provably/necessarily equal (or biconditional) under universal closure; the goal is to find, for any quantifier-free $F(x)$, a coextensive quantifier-free $G$ for $\exists x\, F(x)$ with no new free variables (elimination of quantifiers).
- Normal form of a term — one of $kx, -kx, kx+t, -kx+t, t$ (where $t$ omits $x$).
- Lower/upper inequalities — atomic formulas $t < kx$ (lower) vs. $kx < t$ (upper), with $k$ positive and $t$ free of $x$.
- The 30 operations — a fixed algorithmic sequence (disjunctive normal form conversion, replacing $=$ by pairs of $<$, replacing negated $<$/$D_m$, normalizing terms, reducing multiple lower/upper inequalities to at most one each via case splits, reducing $D_m(kx-t)$ atoms via prime-power decomposition and the Chinese remainder theorem, and finally Table 24-1's seven elimination cases) that reduces $\exists x\,F(x)$ to a coextensive quantifier-free sentence.
- Chinese remainder theorem (invoked, cf. Lemma 15.5) — used at step (26) to combine several divisibility conditions $D_{m_1}(x-i_1), \ldots$ into a single $D_m(x-i)$.

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **26.1 Ramsey's Theorem: Finitary and Infinitary** — partition of a set (family of nonempty, jointly exhaustive, pairwise disjoint classes); size-$k$ set; Theorem 26.1 (Ramsey's theorem, finitary): for positive integers $r,s,n$ with $n \ge r$, there is $m \ge n$ such that any partition of the size-$r$ subsets of $\{0,\ldots,m-1\}$ into $s$ classes has a size-$n$ homogeneous subset; homogeneous set (all its size-$r$ subsets lie in the same class); Theorem 26.2 (infinitary Ramsey's theorem): for $f:[\omega]^r \to \{1,\ldots,s\}$ there is an infinite $Y\subseteq \omega$ and $j$ with $f:[Y]^r \to \{j\}$; notation $[X]^r$ (all size-$r$ subsets of $X$), $\omega$ (the natural numbers); proof by induction on $r$, defining an operation $\Phi$ that outputs $(j,Y)$; the falsity of the naive "uniform for all $r$" strengthening of Theorem 26.2 (counterexample via $f(x)=1$ iff $x$ contains $|x|$). : [[Ramsey's-Theorem-and-Combinatorics|Link]]
- **26.2 König's Lemma** — tree (nodes $T$, levels $T = T_0 \cup T_1 \cup \cdots$, relation $R$ with each node in $T_{n+1}$ immediately below exactly one node in $T_n$); branch (infinite chain $b_0, b_1, \ldots$ each immediately below the next); Lemma 26.3 (König's lemma): an infinite tree with all levels finite has an infinite branch, proved via the compactness theorem (encoding branch-choice as a set $\Gamma$ of sentences in a language with predicate $B$ and one constant per node); derivation of finitary Ramsey's theorem (26.1) from the infinitary version (26.2) plus König's lemma, by building the tree of "bad" partitions and extracting an infinite branch/homogeneous set; Corollary 26.4 (Schur's theorem): under any finite coloring of $\mathbb{N}$, there are same-colored $x,y,z$ with $x+y=z$; glorious set (a set $Y$ with more than $p$ elements, where $p = \min Y$) and the glorified Ramsey's theorem (homogeneous set required to be glorious); the fact that ordinary Ramsey's theorem is provable in $P$ but glorified Ramsey's theorem is not (Paris–Harrington theorem, stated but not proved — beyond the book's scope, requiring deeper nonstandard-models analysis). : [[Ramsey's-Theorem-and-Combinatorics|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why is the "detour through the infinite" (proving the infinitary Ramsey's theorem first, then using König's lemma plus compactness) a genuinely different strategy from a direct finitary combinatorial proof, and what does this reveal about the relationship between infinitary existence proofs and finitary constructive content (note that the least witnessing $m$ is not even effectively bounded by this method)?
2. Why does the naive "one homogeneous set works for all $r$ simultaneously" strengthening of the infinitary Ramsey's theorem fail, and what does the counterexample formula $f(x) = 1$ iff $|x| \in x$ reveal about the limits of homogeneity?
3. What is the essential extra ingredient (glory) that makes the glorified Ramsey's theorem strong enough to be independent of $P$, whereas ordinary Ramsey's theorem is provable in $P$?

### Chapter 27: Modal Logic and Provability (pp. 327–340)

**Summary:** After introducing sentential modal logic (the systems K, K+(A1)–(A4), and Kripke's possible-worlds semantics with soundness/completeness theorems), the chapter shows that the specific modal system GL exactly captures provability in Peano arithmetic $P$ via the arithmetical soundness theorem (built on Löb's theorem), and then proves GL's fixed point theorem and normal form theorem, which mechanically "cash out" as decision procedures and structural results about Gödel-type self-referential sentences (e.g., the Gödel sentence is provably equivalent in $P$ to the consistency sentence). : [[Modal-Logic-and-the-Logic-of-Provability|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **27.1 Modal Logic** — box $\Box$ ("necessarily") and diamond $\Diamond$ (abbreviation for $\sim\Box\sim$, "possibly"); modal tautology (substitution instance of a nonmodal validity); system K — axioms = tautologies plus $\Box(A\to B)\to(\Box A\to\Box B)$, rules = tautologous consequence plus necessitation (from $A$ infer $\Box A$); candidate additional axioms $(A1)\ \Box A \to A$, $(A2)\ A \to \Diamond A$, $(A3)\ \Box A \to \Box\Box A$, $(A4)\ \Box(\Box A \to A) \to \Box A$; model for K = triple $\mathcal{W} = (W, >, \omega)$ (possible worlds, accessibility relation, valuation), with $\mathcal{W},w \models \Box A$ iff $\mathcal{W},v \models A$ for all $v < w$; frame conditions $(W1)$ reflexivity, $(W2)$ symmetry, $(W3)$ transitivity, $(W4)$ irreflexivity; soundness and completeness of $S$ for a class $\Sigma$ of models; Theorem 27.1 (Kripke soundness/completeness): K plus any subset of $\{(A1),(A2),(A3)\}$ is sound and complete for models satisfying the corresponding subset of $\{(W1),(W2),(W3)\}$; system $\mathrm{GL} = K + (A3) + (A4)$; Theorem 27.8 (Segerberg soundness/completeness theorem): GL is sound and complete for transitive, irreflexive models, via the notion of rank $\mathrm{rk}(w)$. : [[Modal-Logic-and-the-Logic-of-Provability|Link]]
- **27.2 The Logic of Provability** — translation $A^\varphi$ of a modal sentence into arithmetic given an assignment $\varphi$ of arithmetic sentences to sentence letters ($p^\varphi = \varphi(p)$, $\bot^\varphi = 0{=}1$, $(B\to C)^\varphi = B^\varphi \to C^\varphi$, $(\Box B)^\varphi = \mathrm{Prv}(\ulcorner B^\varphi \urcorner)$); Theorem 27.9 (arithmetical soundness theorem): $\vdash_{\mathrm{GL}} A \implies \vdash_P A^\varphi$ for all $\varphi$, proved using Löb's theorem for the $(A4)$-type axiom; Solovay completeness theorem (converse, stated but not proved: $\vdash_P A^\varphi$ for all $\varphi \implies \vdash_{\mathrm{GL}} A$); sentence of Gödel type (a self-referential sentence $\pi_\alpha$ built via the diagonal lemma from a formula $\alpha(x)$ constructed out of $\mathrm{Prv}$ by truth functions); the resulting decision procedure for sentences of Gödel type, via translating $\alpha$'s corresponding modal $p$-sentence to a normal-form fixed point; worked example: the Henkin sentence corresponds to $H = \sim\Box\bot$ ($\eta = 0{=}1$, confirming it is a theorem of $P$ — Löb's answer to Henkin's question), and the Gödel sentence corresponds to $H=\sim\Box\bot$ as well giving the (previously unknown) result that the Gödel sentence is provably equivalent in $P$ to the consistency sentence. : [[Gödel's-Second-Incompleteness-Theorem-and-the-Logic-of-Provability|Link1]], [[Modal-Logic-and-the-Logic-of-Provability|Link2]]
- **27.3 The Fixed Point and Normal Form Theorems** — modalized in $p$ (every occurrence of $p$ lies within a subsentence beginning $\Box$); $p$-sentence, letterless sentence; fixed point $H$ of $A$ with respect to $p$ ($H$ contains no $p$, only letters of $A$, and $\vdash_{\mathrm{GL}} \Box(p \leftrightarrow A) \to \Box(p \leftrightarrow H)$); iterated box notation $\Box^0 A = A$, $\Box^{n+1}A = \Box\Box^n A$; normal form for letterless sentences (truth-functional compound of the $\Box^n \bot$); Theorem 27.10 (De Jongh–Sambin fixed point theorem): every $A$ modalized in $p$ has a fixed point $H$; Theorem 27.11 (normal form theorem): every letterless sentence is GL-equivalent to one in normal form; Corollary 27.12 combines both to get letterless normal-form fixed points, effectively computable via the $A^\S$ / $B^\#$ transforms (defined by induction on the "grade" of $A$, i.e., the number of distinct maximal $\Box$-headed subsentences substituted in). : [[Modal-Logic-and-the-Logic-of-Provability|Link]]

**Key Questions:**

2. What is the significance of the fixed point theorem holding only for sentences *modalized in* $p$ (all occurrences of $p$ inside the scope of $\Box$) — what would go wrong (loss of uniqueness or existence) for arbitrary $p$-sentences?

How does the "cashing out" procedure (Example 27.13) turn a purely modal-logic fact about GL into simultaneously new information about $P$ (e.g. the equivalence of the Gödel sentence and the consistency sentence), and why is this considered a genuinely novel connection between the first and second incompleteness theorems rather than a restatement of them?
