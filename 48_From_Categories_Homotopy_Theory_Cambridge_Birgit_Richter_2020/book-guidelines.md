# From Categories to Homotopy Theory — Guidelines

## Header

**Title:** From Categories to Homotopy Theory
**Author(s):** Birgit Richter
**Publication:** Cambridge Studies in Advanced Mathematics, vol. 188, Cambridge University Press, 2020

**Brief Summary:**
This book bridges pure category theory and its applications in homotopy theory. Part I gives a thorough, example-rich introduction to category theory — from basic definitions through natural transformations, the Yoneda lemma, limits and colimits, Kan extensions, monads, abelian categories, monoidal categories, and enriched categories. Part II applies this machinery to homotopy theory: simplicial objects, the nerve and classifying space of a category, operads, classifying spaces of symmetric monoidal categories and group completion, diagram-category models of iterated loop spaces, functor homology, and homology/cohomology of small categories. Diagram categories and the idea that "many algebraic and homotopical concepts are instances of a single categorical construction" run throughout.

**Intent of the Author:**
Richter wrote the book to make category theory accessible to graduate students and researchers with a background in algebraic topology and algebra, and to make the case — through concrete, heavily diagrammatic proofs and worked examples — that functor homology and homology of small categories deserve to be much more widely known among working algebraic topologists.

## Chapter Summaries

### Chapter 1: Basic Notions in Category Theory (pp. 7–26)

**Summary:** This chapter lays the axiomatic foundations of category theory — categories, functors, and the basic zoo of special morphisms and objects (epi/mono, projective/injective, terminal/initial) — building the vocabulary that every later chapter depends on. : [[Foundations-of-Category-Theory|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **1.1 Definition of a Category and Examples** — category $\mathcal{C}$ (objects, morphism sets $\mathcal{C}(C_1,C_2)$, associative composition, identities), source/target (domain/codomain), locally small category, small category, finite category, discrete category, many standard example categories (Sets, Gr, Ab, $K$-vect, $R$-mod, Top, Top$_*$, CW, Ch, Ch$_{\geq 0}$), category of correspondences Corr, a poset as a category, the categories $[0]$, $[1]$, $[n]$ (diagrams), category of open sets $\mathcal{U}(X)$, endomorphism monoid $\mathcal{C}(C,C)$ and the category $C_M$ associated to a monoid $M$; constructions: empty category, product $\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{D}$, disjoint union $\mathcal{C}\sqcup\mathcal{D}$, join $\mathcal{C}*\mathcal{D}$, opposite/dual category $\mathcal{C}^o$. : [[Foundations-of-Category-Theory|Link1]], [[Monads-and-Comonads|Link2]]
- **1.2 EI Categories and Groupoids** — isomorphism, EI category (every endomorphism is iso), groupoid (every morphism is iso); examples: category $I$ of finite sets/injections, category $\Sigma$ of finite sets/surjections, category of isomorphisms $\mathrm{Iso}(\mathcal{C})$, one-object groupoid $C_G$ from a group $G$, fundamental groupoid $\Pi(X)$, translation category $E_G$ of a group. : [[Foundations-of-Category-Theory|Link]]
- **1.3 Epi- and Monomorphisms** — epimorphism (right-cancellable), monomorphism (left-cancellable, dual via $\mathcal{C}^o$), testing criteria via induced maps on hom-sets, retraction/section, projective object, injective object, and their preservation under retracts and splitting properties; cautionary examples where epi+mono $\neq$ iso (e.g., $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Q}$ in commutative rings).
- **1.4 Subcategories and Functors** — subcategory, full subcategory, functor (covariant), examples (inclusion, identity, abelianization, forgetful functors, singular homology, fundamental group functor $\pi_1$, free group/free abelian group functors, morphism functor $\mathcal{C}(C_0,-)$, constant functor $\Delta_D$, presheaf $F:\mathcal{U}(X)^{op}\to\mathcal{C}$); contravariant functor, dual vector space functor; isomorphism of categories, full functor, faithful functor, fully faithful functor, essentially surjective functor; the category **cat** of small categories; connected category. : [[Foundations-of-Category-Theory|Link]]
- **1.5 Terminal and Initial Objects** — terminal object, initial object, zero object, zero morphism; uniqueness up to isomorphism; examples across Sets, Sets$_*$, $R$-mod, Ab, Gr, $E_G$; inductive cone $\mathcal{C}*[0]$ and projective cone $[0]*\mathcal{C}$. : [[Foundations-of-Category-Theory|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the definition of category insist morphisms form a *set* (locally small), and what breaks (e.g., in the category **cat**) if you don't restrict to small categories?
2. Why are epimorphisms in general categories (e.g. commutative rings, $\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}$) not the same as surjections, and what does this reveal about the danger of using set-theoretic intuition for categorical notions?
3. What is the precise relationship between EI categories, groupoids, and the more familiar notion of a group — and why does every category "look like a monoid with many objects"?

### Chapter 2: Natural Transformations and the Yoneda Lemma (pp. 27–48)

**Summary:** This chapter introduces natural transformations and functor categories, culminating in the Yoneda lemma and Yoneda embedding — arguably the single most important structural tool in category theory — and uses these to define adjoint functors and equivalences of categories. : [[Natural-Transformations-and-the-Yoneda-Lemma|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **2.1 Natural Transformations** — natural transformation $\eta: F\Rightarrow G$ (components $\eta_C$, naturality square), natural isomorphism, vertical composition, horizontal composition, interchange law, functor category $\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}')$, 2-category **cat**, center of a category $Z(\mathcal{C})$; examples: connecting homomorphism in homology, abelianization as a natural transformation, double-dual map, category of presheaves as a functor category. : [[Kan-Extensions-Ends-and-Coends|Link1]], [[Natural-Transformations-and-the-Yoneda-Lemma|Link2]]
- **2.2 The Yoneda Lemma** — representable functor and representation $(C,\eta)$; Yoneda lemma (bijection $\mathrm{Nat}(\mathcal{C}(C,-),F)\cong F(C)$, natural in $C$ and $F$); uniqueness of representations up to isomorphism; Yoneda embedding $Y:\mathcal{C}^o\to\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{C},\mathrm{Sets})$ (fully faithful); Brown representability, Eilenberg–Mac Lane spaces, cohomology operations and the Steenrod algebra as an application. : [[Comma-Categories-and-the-Grothendieck-Construction|Link1]], [[Enriched-Category-Theory|Link2]], [[Natural-Transformations-and-the-Yoneda-Lemma|Link3]]
- **2.3 Equivalences of Categories** — equivalence of categories (functors $F,G$ with natural isomorphisms $FG\cong\mathrm{Id}$, $GF\cong\mathrm{Id}$), example via translation category $E_G\simeq[0]$. : [[Natural-Transformations-and-the-Yoneda-Lemma|Link]]
- **2.4 Adjoint Pairs of Functors** — adjunction $\varphi_{C,C'}:\mathcal{C}'(LC,C')\cong\mathcal{C}(C,RC')$, left/right adjoint, unit $\eta$ and counit $\varepsilon$, triangle identities; reflection of an object at a functor, reflective subcategory; free–forgetful adjunction examples; construction of adjunctions from pointwise reflections. : [[Natural-Transformations-and-the-Yoneda-Lemma|Link]]
- **2.5 Equivalences of Categories via Adjoint Functors** — equivalence of categories $\Leftrightarrow$ adjoint equivalence $\Leftrightarrow$ fully faithful and essentially surjective functor. : [[Natural-Transformations-and-the-Yoneda-Lemma|Link]]
- **2.6 Skeleta of Categories** — reduced category, skeleton of a category; for reduced categories, equivalence = isomorphism; every category has a skeleton (via choice); categories equivalent iff skeleta isomorphic. : [[Natural-Transformations-and-the-Yoneda-Lemma|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. In what precise sense does the Yoneda lemma let you "recover an object from its incoming (or outgoing) morphisms," and why does this make representable functors and their representations unique up to unique isomorphism?
2. What is the difference between an isomorphism of categories and an equivalence of categories, and why is "fully faithful + essentially surjective" the right characterization of the latter?
3. How do the unit $\eta$ and counit $\varepsilon$ of an adjunction encode fullness/faithfulness of the adjoint functors, and why does this matter for recognizing reflective subcategories?

### Chapter 3: Colimits and Limits (pp. 49–70)

**Summary:** This chapter develops (co)limits as universal cones/cocones over diagrams, catalogues their many concrete incarnations (products, coproducts, pushouts, pullbacks, equalizers, coequalizers, and more), and shows how completeness relates to adjoint functors and to interchanging limits and colimits. : [[Kan-Extensions-Ends-and-Coends|Link1]], [[Limits-and-Colimits|Link2]], [[Monads-and-Comonads|Link3]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **3.1 Diagrams and Their Colimits** — diagram $F:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}$, constant functor $\Delta$, colimit $\mathrm{colim}_\mathcal{D}F$ as a universal cocone, cone/cocone, colimit as left adjoint to $\Delta$; sequential colimits (CW complexes, stable homotopy groups); coproducts (fold map, wedge sum, free product of groups); pushouts (amalgamated products, Seifert–van Kampen); coequalizers (tensor product as coequalizer, cokernel, split coequalizer); limits and sequential limits ($p$-adic integers); products, pullbacks, equalizers (sheaf condition), fiber products, kernel pairs. : [[Comma-Categories-and-the-Grothendieck-Construction|Link1]], [[The-Nerve-and-Classifying-Spaces-of-Categories|Link2]], [[Limits-and-Colimits|Link3]]
- **3.2 Existence of Colimits and Limits** — complete/cocomplete/bicomplete categories, finitely (co)complete; complete $\Leftrightarrow$ products + equalizers (dual for cocomplete). : [[Kan-Extensions-Ends-and-Coends|Link1]], [[Limits-and-Colimits|Link2]], [[Monads-and-Comonads|Link3]]
- **3.3 Colimits and Limits in Functor Categories** — pointwise computation of (co)limits in $\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C})$; monomorphisms in functor categories detected componentwise. : [[Limits-and-Colimits|Link]]
- **3.4 Adjoint Functors and Colimits and Limits** — functor preserving colimits/limits; left adjoints preserve colimits, right adjoints preserve limits ("RAPL/LAPC"). : [[Limits-and-Colimits|Link]]
- **3.5 Exchange Rules for Colimits and Limits** — iterated (co)limits commute with each other; interchange morphism $\chi:\mathrm{colim}\,\mathrm{lim}\to\mathrm{lim}\,\mathrm{colim}$ (generally not iso); filtered category; filtered colimits commute with finite limits in Sets. : [[Limits-and-Colimits|Link1]], [[Kan-Extensions-Ends-and-Coends|Link2]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does completeness reduce to just "has products and equalizers," and how does this let you build all the special (co)limits from a small set of primitives?
2. Why do left adjoints preserve colimits and right adjoints preserve limits, and how does this single fact explain concrete phenomena like "free functors preserve coproducts"?
3. Why does the interchange morphism $\chi:\mathrm{colim}\,\mathrm{lim}\to\mathrm{lim}\,\mathrm{colim}$ fail to be an isomorphism in general, and what special condition (filteredness, finiteness) restores it?

### Chapter 4: Kan Extensions (pp. 71–90)

**Summary:** This chapter constructs left and right Kan extensions as the universal ways to extend a functor along another functor, expresses them via colimits/limits over comma categories, introduces ends/coends as their natural-transformation-flavored cousins, and closes with Mac Lane's slogan that "all concepts are Kan extensions." : [[Kan-Extensions-Ends-and-Coends|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **4.1 Left Kan Extensions** — left Kan extension $(K,\alpha)$ of $F$ along $G$ with universal property; comma category $G\downarrow D$; existence when $\mathcal{C}$ small, $\mathcal{E}$ cocomplete, via $K(D)=\mathrm{colim}_{G\downarrow D}(F\circ U)$; pointwise left Kan extension; Day convolution example; isomorphism when $G$ fully faithful; left Kan extension as left adjoint to precomposition $G^*$. : [[Kan-Extensions-Ends-and-Coends|Link]]
- **4.2 Right Kan Extensions** — right Kan extension dual to the left version, using $D\downarrow G$; existence via limits, pointwise right Kan extension. : [[Kan-Extensions-Ends-and-Coends|Link]]
- **4.3 Functors Preserving Kan Extensions** — a functor preserving a Kan extension; left adjoints preserve left Kan extensions; pointwise right Kan extension $\Leftrightarrow$ preserved by all representable functors. : [[Kan-Extensions-Ends-and-Coends|Link]]
- **4.4 Ends** — dinatural transformation, end $\int_D H(D,D)$ as universal dinatural transformation; natural transformations as an end example; coend, tensor product of functors $F\otimes_\mathcal{D}G$ as a coend.
- **4.5 Coends as Colimits and Ends as Limits** — twisted arrow category $\mathcal{D}^\tau$; end of $H$ = limit of $H\circ\chi$; ends exist when $\mathcal{E}$ complete and $\mathcal{D}$ small. : [[Kan-Extensions-Ends-and-Coends|Link]]
- **4.6 Calculus Notation** — integral notation $\int_D H$, $\int^D H$; Fubini theorem for ends.
- **4.7 "All Concepts are Kan Extensions"** — colimits/limits as Kan extensions to the terminal category $[0]$; the Yoneda lemma as a right Kan extension along the identity functor; adjoint functors expressed as Kan extensions of identity functors. : [[Kan-Extensions-Ends-and-Coends|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why is a "pointwise" left/right Kan extension not automatic from the abstract universal-property definition, and what role does preservation by representables play in distinguishing the two notions?
2. How does the comma category $G\downarrow D$ let you compute a left Kan extension explicitly as $\mathrm{colim}_{G\downarrow D}(F\circ U)$, and why does this reduce to $F(C)$ itself when $G$ is fully faithful?
3. In what precise sense are colimits, limits, the Yoneda lemma, and adjoint functors all special cases of Kan extensions, and why does this unification matter conceptually?
