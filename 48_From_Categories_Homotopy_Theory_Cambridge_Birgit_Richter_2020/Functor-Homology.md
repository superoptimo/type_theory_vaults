# Functor Homology

## Why does the book need this chapter at all?

Every working algebraic topologist and homological algebraist eventually collects a
zoo of homology theories: Hochschild homology of algebras, cyclic homology,
André–Quillen cohomology, Gamma homology, group homology, and so on. Each of
these theories usually arrives with its own hand-built chain complex — a specific
recipe of tensor powers and face/degeneracy maps that someone wrote down because
it happened to compute the right thing.

That's unsatisfying for the same reason that hand-rolled parsers are unsatisfying
once you've seen a parser generator: every one of these complexes is *doing the
same abstract operation* — deriving a coequalizer-like gluing construction — over
a different indexing category. **Functor homology** is the abstraction that makes
this explicit: instead of writing down a chain complex by hand, you exhibit your
homology theory as $\mathrm{Tor}$ of two functors on some diagram category
$\mathcal{C}$. Once you've done that, you get for free:

- comparison machinery (two homology theories agree if the underlying functors
  agree — no need to compare chain complexes directly),
- extra structure "for free" from combinatorial features of $\mathcal{C}$ (spectral
  sequences, decompositions),
- and, per Richter's own framing, a genuine explanatory payoff: "in order to get
  functor homology interpretations we have to understand what something really
  is" (p. 346).

This chapter is the payoff chapter for essentially the entire first fourteen
chapters of the book: it needs [[Enriched-Category-Theory|enriched category theory]] (Ch. 9) for the tensor
product of functors, abelian categories with enough projectives (Ch. 7) to do
homological algebra in a functor category, and the diagram categories built up
in Chapters 10 and 14 ($\Delta$, $\Gamma$, categories of operators) as the actual
indexing categories used below.

**What breaks without this abstraction:** without it, "Hochschild homology,"
"cyclic homology," and "Gamma homology" look like three unrelated inventions,
each defined by its own opaque boundary formula. You'd have no systematic way
to predict *in advance* what extra structure a new homology theory might carry,
and no uniform proof technique — you'd reprove long-exact-sequence and
excision-type results from scratch for each one.

## 1. The tensor product of functors, again

### 1.1 Recap: coends as a "gluing" operation

Section 4.4 already introduced the coend $\int^D H(D, D)$. This chapter's first
move is to specialize that construction to functor tensor products, which the
book had previewed even earlier but now nails down for its purposes.

**Definition 15.1.1.** Let $\mathcal{D}$ be small, $(\mathcal{C}, \otimes, 1)$ a
cocomplete symmetric monoidal category, and $F : \mathcal{D}^{op} \to \mathcal{C}$,
$G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ functors. Their **tensor product**
$F \otimes_{\mathcal{D}} G$ is the coend of
$H : \mathcal{D}^{op} \times \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$, $(D_1, D_2) \mapsto
F(D_1) \otimes G(D_2)$ — concretely, the coequalizer

$$
\coprod_{f \in \mathcal{D}(D_1,D_2)} F(D_2) \otimes G(D_1)
\;\rightrightarrows\;
\coprod_{D \text{ obj of } \mathcal{D}} F(D) \otimes G(D)
\;\twoheadrightarrow\; F \otimes_{\mathcal{D}} G.
$$

**Why coequalizer, and why does this deserve to be called a "tensor product"?**
Think of the analogous construction for modules: $M \otimes_R N$ is built from
$M \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} N$ by forcing $mr \otimes n = m \otimes rn$ for every
$r \in R$. Here $\mathcal{D}$ plays the role of the ring $R$ (a "ring with many
objects," in the spirit of the "category = monoid with many objects" motif from
Chapter 1), $F$ and $G$ are the modules, and the coequalizer glues together the
pieces $F(D_1) \otimes G(D_2)$ along every morphism $f : D_1 \to D_2$, exactly the
way $M \otimes_R N$ glues $M \otimes N$ along every ring action. **Example 15.1.2**
makes this literal: if $\mathcal{R}$ is the one-object category with $R$ as its
endomorphism monoid, then functors $F_M : \mathcal{R}^{op} \to \mathrm{Ab}$ and
$G_N : \mathcal{R} \to \mathrm{Ab}$ picking out a right $R$-module $M$ and left
$R$-module $N$ satisfy $F_M \otimes_{\mathcal{R}} G_N \cong M \otimes_R N$ — the
classical tensor product is the $|\mathcal{D}|=1$ special case of functor
tensor product.

**Grounding (Rust).** The coequalizer-as-gluing intuition maps cleanly onto a
"quotient by an equivalence relation generated by rewrite rules" pattern:

```rust
// Conceptually: F ⊗_D G is the free C-module on pairs (D, f, g)
// modulo the relation induced by every morphism `h: D1 -> D2` in D:
//   (D2, F(h)(x), y)  ~  (D1, x, G(h)(y))     for x: F(D2), y: G(D1)
//
// This is exactly a union-find / congruence-closure problem in disguise:
// generators are basis elements, relations are the coequalizer's parallel pair.
struct FunctorTensor<C> {
    generators: Vec<(DiagramObj, C, C)>, // (D, f-side value, g-side value)
}

impl<C: Eq + Clone> FunctorTensor<C> {
    // Building the coequalizer = repeatedly merging generators connected
    // by a morphism-induced relation, like Union-Find.merge.
    fn identify(&mut self, morphism_image: (DiagramObj, C, C), source: (DiagramObj, C, C)) {
        // union(morphism_image, source) in a congruence-closure structure
    }
}
```

The key engineering intuition to carry forward: a coend/coequalizer construction
*is* a normalization-by-quotienting procedure, the same shape as computing normal
forms modulo a rewrite system or interning terms modulo an equational theory.

### 1.2 Adjunction with the enriched end

**Theorem 15.1.3.** If $\mathcal{C}$ is closed symmetric monoidal and bicomplete,
then for $F : \mathcal{D}^{op} \to \mathcal{C}$, $G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$,
$C \in \mathcal{C}$:
$$
\mathcal{C}(F \otimes_{\mathcal{D}} G, C) \;\cong\; \mathrm{nat}_{\mathcal{C}}(F,
\mathcal{C}(G(-), C)),
$$
where $\mathrm{nat}_{\mathcal{C}}$ is the enriched end of natural transformations
(Ch. 9.3). This is the categorified Hom–tensor adjunction $\mathrm{Hom}(M \otimes N,
P) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(M, \mathrm{Hom}(N,P))$ — unsurprising once you recognize
$\otimes_{\mathcal{D}}$ as "the" tensor product for this setting, since Kan
extensions and (co)ends were built in Chapter 4 precisely to be the correct
categorical generalizations of colimit-flavored and limit-flavored constructions,
and the tensor–hom adjunction is a coend/end pair (Fubini-flavored) by
construction.

The specialization to $k\text{-mod}$ (**Remark 15.1.4**) is what actually gets
used later:
$$
k\text{-mod}(F \otimes_{\mathcal{D}} G, M) \cong \mathrm{nat}(F, k\text{-mod}(G(-),
M)). \tag{15.1.1}
$$

**Corollary 15.1.5 (the workhorse identity).** If $G = \mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}}(D,-)$
is representable, then
$$
F \otimes_{\mathcal{D}} \mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}}(D,-) \cong F(D),
$$
naturally in $F$ and $D$ — the functor-category analogue of the Yoneda lemma's
"$\mathrm{Nat}(\mathcal{C}(C,-),F) \cong F(C)$," now phrased via the tensor
product instead of the hom/nat-functor. This single identity is what will let
the chapter *compute* Hochschild homology in low degree directly from the
definition, rather than by unwinding the coend by hand every time.

## 2. Tor and Ext of functors

### 2.1 Setting up homological algebra in a functor category

To even talk about derived functors, you need projective resolutions — which
means you need to know the functor category *has* enough projectives. This is
where Chapter 7's abelian-category machinery ($\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{D},
\mathcal{A})$ is abelian if $\mathcal{A}$ is, Proposition 7.3.7) gets cashed in.

**Lemma 15.2.1.** If $\mathcal{A}$ has enough projectives and $P$ is projective
in $\mathcal{A}$, then
$$
F_D(P) := \coprod_{f \in \mathcal{D}(D,-)} P
$$
is a projective object in $\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{A})$, and
$\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{A})$ has enough projectives.

**What's actually going on:** $F_D$ is left adjoint to the evaluation functor
$\varepsilon_D : \mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{A}) \to \mathcal{A}$, $G
\mapsto G(D)$. Left adjoints of exact-ish evaluation functors send projectives to
projectives — an instance of the general "left adjoints preserve projectives
(when the right adjoint is exact)" pattern from Chapter 2/3's adjoint-functor
machinery. This is the free-object construction in functor-land: $F_D(P)$ is "the
free functor generated by a copy of $P$ sitting at $D$," exactly analogous to how
a free $R$-module on a generator is $R$ itself, or how a free monad algebra is
built from the free-forgetful adjunction of Chapter 6.

**Definitions 15.2.2–15.2.3.** For $F : \mathcal{D}^{op} \to \mathcal{A}$, $G :
\mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{A}$ (target abelian, bicomplete, closed symmetric
monoidal, with enough projectives):
$$
\mathrm{Tor}^{\mathcal{D}}_*(F,G) := H_*(P_* \otimes_{\mathcal{D}} G), \qquad
\mathrm{Ext}^*_{\mathcal{D}}(F,G) := H^*(\mathrm{nat}(P_*, G)),
$$
where $P_*$ is a projective resolution of $F$. This is *literally* the classical
derived-functor recipe — resolve, apply the (co)variant functor, take homology —
just executed inside a functor category instead of $R\text{-mod}$. If you already
know why $\mathrm{Tor}^R_*(M,N)$ is well-defined (independent of the resolution
chosen) up to canonical isomorphism, the identical argument goes through here
verbatim, because everything used (projective resolutions exist, $\otimes$
preserves epis and cokernels) has already been established for $\mathrm{Fun}
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A})$.

### 2.2 The axiomatic characterization

**Theorem 15.2.4.** If $H_*$ is a functor $\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{C}, k\text{-mod})
\to \mathrm{grMod}_k$ such that:

1. $H_0(F) \cong G \otimes_{\mathcal{C}} F$ naturally,
2. $H_*$ sends short exact sequences of functors to long exact sequences,
   naturally,
3. $H_i(F) = 0$ for $F$ projective and $i > 0$,

then $H_i(F) \cong \mathrm{Tor}^{\mathcal{C}}_i(G,F)$ for all $F$.

This is the categorical incarnation of the standard "dimension-shifting"
uniqueness argument for derived functors (à la Cartan–Eilenberg), and it is the
*load-bearing tool* for the rest of the chapter: instead of proving directly that
some hand-built complex computes $\mathrm{Tor}$, you check three much easier
conditions and get the isomorphism for free. This is the methodological
punchline that Section 15.3 will exploit repeatedly.

**What breaks without the axiomatic characterization:** you'd be stuck proving,
by an explicit chain map and chain homotopy, that two specific resolutions
compute the same homology every single time you wanted a new functor homology
description — exactly the kind of bespoke, non-reusable proof this chapter's
whole raison d'être is to eliminate.

## 3. Worked example: Hochschild homology as functor homology

This is the chapter's centerpiece, and Richter is explicit that she's not just
proving a theorem but *demonstrating a method*: "how do you guess the diagram
category, and how do you find the right functors?" (p. 350). This is worth
reading as a fully worked instance of a **reverse-engineering methodology** —
given a target invariant, systematically recover the diagram category and
functors that realize it as $\mathrm{Tor}$.

### 3.1 The target: classical Hochschild homology

**Definition 15.3.1.** For a commutative ring $k$, a $k$-algebra $A$, and an
$A$-bimodule $M$, Hochschild homology $HH^k_i(A;M)$ is the homology of the
complex with $M \otimes A^{\otimes n}$ in degree $n$ and differential
$b = \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i d_i$, where the face maps $d_i$ multiply adjacent tensor
factors (cyclically at the top, wrapping the last factor around to hit $M$
again):
$$
d_i(a_0 \otimes \cdots \otimes a_n) = \begin{cases}
a_0 \otimes \cdots \otimes a_i a_{i+1} \otimes \cdots \otimes a_n & i < n \\
a_n a_0 \otimes a_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes a_{n-1} & i = n.
\end{cases}
$$
The book's own picture is a ring of tensor factors with $M$ glued at a fixed
basepoint — that visual is the entire motivation for step 3.2 below.

### 3.2 Guessing the diagram category

The reverse-engineering has three observations feeding into the design of a
category:

1. **No commutativity assumed**, and $a_i \cdot a_{i+1}$ (not $a_{i+1}\cdot a_i$)
   matters in the face map — so morphisms must remember an **ordering**.
2. $M$ always sits at a **distinguished position** (glued to a basepoint) — so
   objects need a **basepoint**.
3. Each chain-degree-$n$ piece is $M \otimes A^{\otimes n}$, indexed by a **finite
   set** with $n+1$ elements — so objects are just **finite pointed sets**.

**Definition 15.3.2.** $(\mathrm{as})$ = category of finite pointed associative
sets: objects $[n] = \{0,\ldots,n\}$ with basepoint $0$; a morphism $f \in
(\mathrm{as})([n],[m])$ is a basepoint-preserving function together with a
**total ordering on each fiber** $f^{-1}(j)$.

This is precisely designing a data structure to match an algorithm's access
pattern — the category's morphisms are engineered to carry *exactly* the data
(pointedness + ordering) that the Hochschild differential needs and no more.
**Example 15.3.3** confirms the ordering data isn't vacuous: a morphism $[5] \to
[0]$ is a permutation in $\Sigma_6$, since the whole domain collapses to one
fiber whose total ordering is all the information left.

**Definition 15.3.4.** $L^k(A;M) : (\mathrm{as}) \to k\text{-mod}$, $[n] \mapsto
M \otimes A^{\otimes n}$; a morphism $f$ acts by multiplying, within each fiber,
the tensor factors *in the fiber's prescribed order* — $b_i =
\prod^{<}_{f(j)=i} a_j$. **Example 15.3.5** works out a concrete case where a
morphism $[4]\to[2]$ with a 3-element fiber $\{2<1<3\}$ produces the product
$a_2 \cdot a_1 \cdot a_3$ — this is the moment the abstract "total ordering on
fibers" cashes out as "the order you multiply things in."

**Grounding (Rust).** The category $(\mathrm{as})$ is a clean `enum`/trait
target — objects are just `usize`, morphisms are a function plus per-fiber
permutation data:

```rust
struct AsMorphism {
    n: usize,           // domain [n]
    m: usize,           // codomain [m]
    image: Vec<usize>,  // image[i] = f(i), basepoint-preserving: image[0] == 0
    // for each j in 0..=m, a total order on the fiber f^{-1}(j):
    fiber_order: Vec<Vec<usize>>, // fiber_order[j] = preimages of j, in order
}

fn apply_lk<R: Ring>(f: &AsMorphism, a: &[R]) -> Vec<R> {
    // a[0] plays the role of the M-coordinate; a[1..] are algebra elements
    let mut b = vec![R::one(); f.m + 1];
    for (j, fiber) in f.fiber_order.iter().enumerate() {
        b[j] = fiber.iter().fold(R::one(), |acc, &idx| acc * a[idx].clone());
    }
    b
}
```
This is worth internalizing as a pattern: **the diagram category IS the
signature of an algebraic theory**, and functors out of it are algebras for
that theory — the same idea underlying Lawvere theories and, later in this same
book, operads (Ch. 12) and diagram categories for iterated loop spaces (Ch. 14).

### 3.3 Guessing the contravariant functor

Given the covariant functor, Richter needs $\bar{b} : (\mathrm{as})^{op} \to
k\text{-mod}$ such that $\mathrm{Tor}^{(\mathrm{as})}_*(\bar{b}, L^k(A;M)) \cong
HH_*(A;M)$. The method: use Theorem 15.2.4's degree-0 axiom
$\mathrm{Tor}^{(\mathrm{as})}_0(\bar b, F) \cong \bar b \otimes_{(\mathrm{as})} F$
and match it against the known formula $HH_0(A;M) \cong M / \langle am - ma
\rangle$.

Using Corollary 15.1.5 to rewrite $L^k(A;M)[0]$ and $L^k(A;M)[1]$ as tensor
products against representable functors $k\{(\mathrm{as})(-,[0])\}$ and
$k\{(\mathrm{as})(-,[1])\}$, the only possibility left is to **define** $\bar b$
as the cokernel:
$$
k\{(\mathrm{as})(-,[1])\} \xrightarrow{\;d_{0<1} - d_{1<0}\;}
k\{(\mathrm{as})(-,[0])\} \twoheadrightarrow \bar b \to 0,
$$
where $d_{0<1}, d_{1<0}$ are the two morphisms $[1]\to[0]$ distinguished only by
their fiber ordering — because the boundary map $M \otimes A \to M$, $m\otimes a
\mapsto ma - am$, is literally the alternating sum of exactly those two maps.
**There is no choice at any step** — this is the point Richter is making about
the method: once you've correctly reverse-engineered the diagram category, the
functors that realize your homology theory are *forced*, not invented.

### 3.4 Extending to arbitrary functors, and the hard part

**Definition 15.3.6** extends $HH_*$ from algebra-bimodule pairs to *any* functor
$F : (\mathrm{as}) \to k\text{-mod}$, using the same $b = \sum (-1)^i d_i$ formula
with $d_i = F(\partial_i)$ for explicitly defined coface-like maps $\partial_i :
[n] \to [n-1]$.

Getting $\mathrm{Tor}_0 \cong HH_0$ is immediate from the construction. The
**genuinely hard step** — the one place creativity/work is required, per
Richter's own admission ("the bad news is that what you have done so far might
not work!", p. 353) — is verifying condition 3 of Theorem 15.2.4: that this
$HH_*$ vanishes on projectives in positive degree.

To make this tractable, the book introduces **projective generators**
(**Definition 15.3.7**): a family $\{P^i\}$ such that every object is a
cokernel of a sum of copies of the $P^i$. **Lemma 15.3.8** shows
$\{k\{\mathcal{D}(D,-)\}\}$ is always such a family in $\mathrm{Fun}(\mathcal{D},
k\text{-mod})$ (proved via Lemma 15.2.1's construction plus $k$ being a
projective generator of $k\text{-mod}$) — so it suffices to check acyclicity on
the representables $(\mathrm{as})_n$, not on *all* projectives.

This reduction — from "check a property for all objects in an infinite,
unwieldy class" to "check it for a small, explicit generating family, using
right-exactness/cokernel-compatibility to propagate the result" — is a proof
pattern worth recognizing on sight: it recurs constantly in homological algebra
and, more relevantly to a compiler's soundness proofs, wherever an invariant
needs to be checked only on generators of a syntax or a lattice rather than on
every derived element.

The vanishing itself (`HH_*((\mathrm{as})_n) \cong` singular homology of $n!$
disjoint standard $n$-simplices, hence concentrated in degree 0) is quoted from
[PR02] rather than reproved. The payoff: **Theorem 15.3.9**,
$$
HH_*(F) \cong \mathrm{Tor}^{(\mathrm{as})}_*(\bar b, F) \quad \text{for all } F :
(\mathrm{as}) \to k\text{-mod}.
$$

**Remark 15.3.10** notes a *second*, older functor-homology description of
Hochschild homology via the simplicial category $\Delta$ (Loday): $HH_*(A;M)
\cong \mathrm{Tor}^{\Delta^{op}}_*(k, L^k(A;M))$. The book flags that these two
descriptions were compared directly by Słomińska via a decomposition of
$(\mathrm{as})$ — a reminder that a functor-homology description is not unique,
mirroring how the same operational semantics can admit multiple equivalent
denotational encodings.

## 4. Cyclic homology as functor homology

Cyclic homology upgrades Hochschild homology by taking its $\mathbb{Z}/(n+1)$
rotation symmetry (rotating tensor factors, (15.4.1)) seriously. Since we no
longer distinguish a basepoint (any tensor factor is now "equivalent" under
rotation), the diagram category drops pointedness but *keeps* the ordering data.

**Definition 15.4.1.** $F(\mathrm{as})$ = category of finite associative sets:
objects $[n] = \{0,\ldots,n\}$, morphisms are functions with a total ordering
on each fiber (same as $(\mathrm{as})$, minus the basepoint condition). The
Loday functor $L^k(A) := L^k(A;A) : F(\mathrm{as}) \to k\text{-mod}$ sends $[n]
\mapsto A^{\otimes n+1}$.

**Remark 15.4.3** is structurally elegant: $(\mathrm{as})$ embeds in
$F(\mathrm{as})$ (pointed $\hookrightarrow$ unpointed), $\Delta$ embeds too
(order-preserving maps, standard fiber ordering), and there are forgetful maps
back down — producing a diagram
$$
\begin{array}{ccc} (\mathrm{as}) & \hookrightarrow & \\ & & F(\mathrm{as})
\twoheadrightarrow \Delta. \end{array}
$$
$F(\mathrm{as})$ is shown isomorphic to Fiedorowicz–Loday's category
$\Delta S$, via the fact that every morphism factors uniquely as
order-preserving-then-permutation — an EI-category-flavored unique
factorization reminiscent of $\Delta$'s own epi/mono factorization (Chapter
10.1).

Applying the *identical* method as Section 15.3 — analyze $HC_0$ via the
totalization of the cyclic bicomplex, identify it as a cokernel of representable
functors — produces
$$
b = \mathrm{coker}(d_{0<1}-d_{1<0} : F(\mathrm{as})_1 \to F(\mathrm{as})_0),
$$
and **Theorem 15.4.4**: $\mathrm{Tor}^{F(\mathrm{as})}_*(b, F) \cong HC_*(F)$ for
all $F : F(\mathrm{as}) \to k\text{-mod}$, in particular $HC_*(A) \cong
\mathrm{Tor}^{F(\mathrm{as})}_*(b, L^k(A))$.

The value of walking through this second example immediately after the first is
that it demonstrates the method is a **reusable algorithm**, not a one-off trick:
same three-step shape (identify what data morphisms must carry → define the
covariant functor → force the contravariant functor via the degree-0
axiom), different diagram category because the underlying symmetry differs.

## 5. Gamma homology as functor homology

Gamma homology (Robinson–Whitehouse) is Hochschild-homology's further
generalization: it treats a commutative $k$-algebra $A$ as an $E_\infty$-algebra
and measures homotopical/obstruction-theoretic information — specifically,
Robinson's obstruction theory for $E_\infty$-structures on ring spectra has
Gamma cohomology as its obstruction groups, and these coincide with André–Quillen
cohomology of simplicial $E_\infty$-algebras (a fact attributed to
[BasRi04]).

Since $A$ is now commutative, orderings on fibers become irrelevant, so the
diagram category simplifies to $\Gamma$, the category of finite pointed sets
(no ordering data at all — the third and simplest diagram category in this
progression, matching the progression $(\mathrm{as}) \to F(\mathrm{as}) \to
\Gamma$ as ordering/basepoint structure is stripped away). The Loday functor
$L^k(A;M) : \Gamma \to k\text{-mod}$ is defined exactly as before, now
well-defined without fiber orderings.

**Definition 15.5.1.** $t : \Gamma^{op} \to k\text{-mod}$, $t = \mathrm{coker}
(\chi : \Gamma_2 \to \Gamma_1)$ with $\chi = d_0 - d_1 + d_2$ — the same
"force the contravariant functor from the low-degree boundary map" recipe, one
more time.

**Theorem 15.5.2.** $H\Gamma_*(F) \cong \mathrm{Tor}_*(t,F)$ for all $F : \Gamma
\to k\text{-mod}$, in particular $H\Gamma_*(A;M) \cong \mathrm{Tor}_*(t,
L^k(A;M))$.

**Example 15.5.3** is the chapter's payoff computation: Gamma homology of
$k[x]$ with coefficients in $k$ (acting via $x \mapsto 0$) computes the stable
$k$-homology of the integer Eilenberg–Mac Lane spectrum, which for $k=\mathbb{F}_p$
recovers **half the dual Steenrod algebra** —
$\mathbb{F}_2[\xi_1^2,\xi_2,\xi_3,\ldots]$ at $p=2$. This is presented as
evidence that although $k[x]$ is free as a *commutative* algebra, it is far
from free as an $E_\infty$-algebra, and Gamma homology is exactly the invariant
that detects the difference — the functor-homology formalism isn't just
bookkeeping here; it's doing real computational work that would be very hard
to access from the original chain-complex definition alone.

## 6. Adjoint base-change for functor Tor

The chapter closes by generalizing the classical **flat base-change** theorem
for ring homomorphisms: if $f: R_1 \to R_2$ makes $R_2$ flat over $R_1$, then
$\mathrm{Tor}^{R_2}_n(M \otimes_{R_1} R_2, N) \cong \mathrm{Tor}^{R_1}_n(M, N_f)$.

**The categorical generalization needs no flatness hypothesis at all** — only
an adjoint pair. Given $L : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$, $R : \mathcal{D} \to
\mathcal{C}$ adjoint, precomposition with $L^{op}$/$R$ gives functors $L^*, R^*$
between the relevant functor categories.

**Lemma 15.6.1.** Precomposition with any functor $L$ preserves exact
sequences — because exactness in a functor category is checked *objectwise*
(Ch. 7's abelian-category machinery again), and precomposing just relabels which
objects you're evaluating at.

**Theorem 15.6.2** [FFPS03]. For an adjoint pair $L \dashv R$, $F :
\mathcal{D}^{op} \to k\text{-mod}$, $G : \mathcal{C} \to k\text{-mod}$:
$$
\mathrm{Tor}^{\mathcal{C}}_n(L^*(F), G) \cong \mathrm{Tor}^{\mathcal{D}}_n(F,
R^*(G)) \quad \text{for all } n \geq 0.
$$
The proof is another instance of the "check on projective generators, then
extend" pattern: verify the $n=0$ case for representable $F, G$ using Corollary
15.1.5 plus the triangle identities of the adjunction (Ch. 2.4) —
$$
F \otimes_{\mathcal{D}} R^*G \cong R^*G(D) = k\{\mathcal{C}(C, RD)\} \cong
k\{\mathcal{C}(-, RD)\} \otimes_{\mathcal{C}} k\{\mathcal{C}(C,-)\} \cong
k\{\mathcal{D}(L(-), D)\} \otimes_{\mathcal{C}} k\{\mathcal{C}(C,-)\} = L^*F
\otimes_{\mathcal{C}} G,
$$
— then bootstrap to all projectives and, since precomposition is exact
(Lemma 15.6.1), to all functors. **Why no flatness is needed:** flatness in the
classical statement exists precisely to guarantee exactness of $- \otimes_{R_1}
R_2$; here, exactness is supplied "for free" by the fact that precomposition
functors are *always* exact (objectwise evaluation), so the adjunction alone
does all the work that flatness used to do.

## Synthesis: where this sits in the book, and why it matters for the elaborator/CSP project

**Structurally:** this chapter is the payoff of the whole "Part I machinery"
strand of the book — enriched categories and coends (Ch. 9, 4) build the tensor
product of functors; abelian categories with enough projectives (Ch. 7) make
derived functors well-defined; adjunctions (Ch. 2) power the base-change
theorem; and diagram categories built from finite (pointed) sets and orderings
echo the diagram-category theme that Chapter 14 develops for loop-space models.
Chapter 15 in turn feeds directly into **Chapter 16**, where the
Jibladze–Pirashvili comparison theorem identifies functor $\mathrm{Tor}$ with
Baues–Wirsching homology of small categories, closing the loop between this
chapter's "homology of algebraic gadgets encoded as functors" and "homology of
categories themselves."

**For the standing project** (a Rust-based dependent-type-language compiler
with a Miller-pattern-unification elaborator and a CSP kernel for
counterexample search): this chapter doesn't carry the elaborator's core
mechanism directly — no metavariables or definitional equality here — so this
note deliberately does not force a `type-theory`/`automated-reasoning` tag onto
it (per the "don't force the connection" guidance). What *is* directly
transferable is the **methodology, independent of the specific mathematics**:

- **The "identify the required data, then let the rest be forced" pattern**
  (Section 3.2–3.3) is exactly the discipline you want when designing an AST or
  IR: figure out precisely what invariant your representation must preserve
  (here: ordering + basepoint; in a unifier, e.g., which variables are rigid vs.
  metavariables), then let the operations on that representation follow with no
  further design freedom — the same "no choice at any step" feeling Richter
  describes.
- **The "check on generators, not on all objects" reduction** (projective
  generators, Lemma 15.3.8) is structurally the same move as checking a
  soundness invariant only on a grammar's productions or a lattice's join-
  irreducible elements rather than on every derived term — worth recognizing
  when designing invariant-checking passes for the abstract interpreter.
- **Diagram categories as algebraic signatures** (Section 3.2's Rust sketch):
  a functor out of a small category is exactly "an algebra for the signature
  that category encodes." This is the same idea underlying Lawvere theories and
  is a genuinely useful lens for representing typing judgments or inference
  rules as functors out of a category of contexts, should that framing ever be
  useful for the compiler's IR design.
