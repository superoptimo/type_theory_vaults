# Rings, Fields, and Vector Spaces — Guidelines

## Header

**Title:** Rings, Fields, and Vector Spaces: An Introduction to Abstract Algebra via Geometric Constructibility
**Author(s):** B. A. Sethuraman
**Publication:** Springer-Verlag, Undergraduate Texts in Mathematics, 1997

**Brief Summary:**
A one-semester introduction to abstract algebra built around a single unifying goal: proving that certain classical straightedge-and-compass constructions (trisecting an angle, doubling the cube, squaring the circle) are impossible. Starting from divisibility in the integers, the book develops rings, fields, vector spaces, field extensions, and polynomial theory, using each new structure only as far as needed to reach the final algebraic criterion for constructibility. The central thesis is that a deep geometric problem, unsolved for two thousand years, yields completely to abstract algebraic machinery.

**Intent of the Author:**
Written for a discrete-mathematics-literate but otherwise algebra-naive undergraduate audience, the book favors exposition and worked examples over exhaustive theorem-proving, and is meant to be read "actively" — with the reader verifying every claim and example independently rather than accepting them on trust.

## Chapter Summaries

### Chapter 1: Divisibility in the Integers (pp. 9–28)

**Summary:** Establishes the Well Ordering Principle as a foundational axiom and uses it to derive the division algorithm, the greatest common divisor (characterized as the smallest positive linear combination of two integers), and ultimately the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, closing with Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes. : [[Divisibility-and-Prime-Factorization-in-the-Integers|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts:**
- **Well Ordering Principle** — every nonempty subset of the nonnegative integers has a least element; taken as an axiom.
- **Divides** ($d \mid a$) — $d$ divides $a$ if $a = db$ for some integer $b$; $d$ is a divisor/factor, $a$ a multiple.
- **Division Algorithm** — given $a, b$ with $b > 0$, there exist unique $q, r$ with $0 \le r < b$ and $a = bq + r$.
- **Greatest common divisor** $\gcd(a,b)$ — the largest common divisor of $a$ and $b$; shown to equal the smallest positive linear combination $xa + yb$.
- **Linear combination** — any integer $xa + yb$ for integers $x, y$.
- **Relatively prime** — $\gcd(a,b) = 1$.
- **Prime / composite** — an integer $p > 1$ whose only divisors are $\pm 1, \pm p$; a non-prime integer greater than 1 is composite.
- **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic** — every integer greater than 1 factors uniquely into primes, up to order.
- **Euclid's Theorem** — there are infinitely many primes (proof by contradiction using $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n + 1$).
- **Least common multiple** (Exercise 13) — the smallest positive common multiple of $a$ and $b$; related to gcd via $\mathrm{lcm}(a,b) = ab/\gcd(a,b)$.

**Key Questions:**
1. Why must the greatest common divisor of $a$ and $b$ be the *least* positive linear combination of $a$ and $b$, rather than merely *some* linear combination equal to a small number?
2. How does the Well Ordering Principle underlie both the division algorithm and the existence of $\gcd(a,b)$?
3. In the proof of unique factorization, why does the argument track degrees/factors shrinking at each stage rather than proving existence directly by strong induction?

### Chapter 2: Rings and Fields (pp. 29–62)

**Summary:** Abstracts the algebraic structure common to $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, matrix rings, and polynomial rings into the definitions of group, ring, integral domain, and field, then develops subrings, the subring/subfield generated by an element, and the crucial distinction between polynomial expressions in an algebraic element and polynomials in a formal variable. : [[Rings|Link1]], [[Integral-Domains-and-Fields|Link2]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **Groups and rings** — binary operation (Def 2.1: group — associative, identity, inverses); abelian group (Def 2.2: commutative binary operation); ring (Def 2.3: seven axioms — commutative/associative addition, additive identity and inverses, associative multiplication, multiplicative identity, left/right distributivity); "number system" as an informal synonym for ring (Remark 2.5); commutative ring (Def 2.6).
- **Subrings** — closed under addition/multiplication (Def 2.9); subring (Def 2.10: closed subset containing 1 that is itself a ring); criterion lemma (2.11) for verifying subrings; subring generated by $S$ and $a$, denoted $S[a]$ (Def 2.13), as all polynomial expressions in $a$ with coefficients in $S$ — the smallest subring containing $S$ and $a$; the crucial warning (Remark 2.16) that polynomial *expressions* in an algebraic element need not have unique degree/coefficients, unlike polynomials in a formal variable $x$.
- **Integral Domains and Fields** — zero-divisor (Def 2.17: nonzero $a$ with $ab=0$ or $ba=0$ for some nonzero $b$); integral domain (Def 2.18: commutative ring with no zero-divisors); cancellation in integral domains (Lemma 2.19); field (Def 2.20: integral domain where every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse); subfield and field extension $K/F$ (Def 2.24).

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the definition of a ring require both left and right distributivity but not commutativity of multiplication, while a "number system" in the loose everyday sense usually is commutative?
2. Why can two different polynomial *expressions* in an algebraic element $a$ (e.g. $1+\sqrt2+(\sqrt2)^2+(\sqrt2)^3$ and $3+3\sqrt2$) be equal even though they have different "degrees," whereas this can never happen for polynomials in a genuine variable $x$?
3. Why must every field automatically be an integral domain, and why does the absence of zero-divisors matter for cancellation?

### Chapter 3: Vector Spaces (pp. 63–96)

**Summary:** Generalizes the geometric notion of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$/$\mathbb{R}^3$ to an abstract $F$-vector space over any field $F$, develops linear independence, spanning sets, and bases, proves that all bases of a finite-dimensional space have the same size (hence dimension is well-defined), and introduces subspaces — laying the groundwork for measuring the "size" of a field extension in Chapter 4. : [[Vector-Spaces|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **Vector spaces (general)** — Def 3.1: an $F$-vector space is an abelian group $V$ with scalar multiplication $F \times V \to V$ satisfying distributivity over vector and scalar addition, associativity with field multiplication, and $1 \cdot v = v$; key examples include $F^n$, $F^\infty$, $M_n(F)$, $F[x]$, and any field/ring extension $K$ of $F$ (with $F$ playing a dual role as both scalars and vectors).
- **Linear Independence, Bases, Dimension** — linear combination (Def 3.4); spanning set (Def 3.5); linearly independent/dependent set; basis as a minimal spanning set / maximal independent set; the theorem that any two bases of a vector space have the same cardinality, justifying a well-defined dimension; finite-dimensional vector space; replacement/exchange argument used to prove any linearly independent set can be extended to a basis of no larger size than a given spanning set.
- **Subspaces** — a subset $W$ of $V$ closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication that is itself a vector space; criterion lemma for verifying subspaces; dimension of a subspace cannot exceed dimension of the ambient space.

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does a spanning set with "redundant" vectors (like $i, j, w$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$) fail to serve as a good notion of "coordinate axes," and what property distinguishes a basis from an arbitrary spanning set?
2. In what sense does a field extension $K/F$ automatically become an $F$-vector space, and what is the "dual role" that elements of $F$ play in this construction?
3. Why is it not obvious a priori that all bases of a vector space have the same number of elements, and why does this fact matter for defining dimension?

### Chapter 4: Field Extensions (pp. 97–118)

**Summary:** Defines the degree $[K:F]$ of a field extension as the dimension of $K$ as an $F$-vector space, proves the multiplicativity of degree in a tower $F \subseteq K \subseteq L$, and introduces algebraic and transcendental elements, showing that every element of a finite-dimensional extension is algebraic. : [[Field-Extensions-and-Degree|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts:**
- **Degree of a field extension** (Def 4.1) — $[K:F]$, the dimension of $K$ as an $F$-vector space.
- **Tower Theorem** (Theorem 4.2) — for $F \subseteq K \subseteq L$, if $[L:K]=m$ and $[K:F]=n$ are finite, then $[L:F] = mn$, with a basis for $L/F$ given by products of bases for $L/K$ and $K/F$.
- **Subfield/subring generated by $F$ and $a$** — recalled from Chapter 2, motivating the question of the corresponding "smallest subfield" $F(a)$ containing $F$ and $a$.
- **Algebraic / transcendental element** (Def 4.10) — $a \in K$ is algebraic over $F$ if it satisfies some nonzero polynomial with coefficients in $F$; otherwise transcendental over $F$.
- **Algebraic number / transcendental number** (Def 4.11) — the special case $F=\mathbb{Q}$, $K=\mathbb{C}$.
- **Theorem 4.12** — every element of a finite-dimensional extension $K/F$ is algebraic over $F$ (proved via linear dependence of $1, a, a^2, \ldots, a^n$).
- **Algebraic field extension** (Def 4.14) — $K/F$ is algebraic if every element of $K$ is algebraic over $F$; algebraic number fields are finite-dimensional extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$.

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the proof of the Tower Theorem require carefully tracking which field is playing the role of "scalars" and which is playing the role of "vectors" at each stage?
2. Why is it comparatively easy to prove a complex number is algebraic but very hard to prove one is transcendental?
3. Is the converse of Theorem 4.12 true — can a field be algebraic over $F$ without being finite-dimensional over $F$? What does this reveal about the relationship between "algebraic" and "finite-dimensional"?

### Chapter 5: Polynomials (pp. 119–154)

**Summary:** Develops $F[x]$ as an integral domain with its own division algorithm, greatest common divisor theory, and notion of irreducibility, culminating in a unique factorization theorem for polynomials that mirrors the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, and closes with results on counting roots (with multiplicity) and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. : [[Polynomial-Rings-and-Factorization|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts:**
- **Division Algorithm for polynomials** (Theorem 5.2) — for $f(x), g(x) \in F[x]$ with $g(x) \ne 0$, unique $q(x), r(x)$ exist with $f(x) = q(x)g(x)+r(x)$ and $r(x)=0$ or $\deg r < \deg g$.
- **Evaluation homomorphism** (Lemma 5.3) — substitution respects sums and products: $(fg)(a)=f(a)g(a)$.
- **Root** (Def 5.5) — $a \in F$ with $f(a)=0$; Corollary 5.4: dividing $f(x)$ by $x-a$ leaves remainder $f(a)$.
- **Divides / factor** for polynomials (Def 5.7), directly analogous to integer divisibility.
- **GCD of polynomials, relatively prime polynomials** (Def 5.9, 5.11) — defined via the "smallest positive linear combination" idea adapted to polynomials (no natural size order on polynomials, so GCD is characterized by the divisibility property instead); Corollary 5.12 (Bezout-style identity for polynomials).
- **Irreducible / reducible polynomial** (Def 5.14) — $f(x)$ is irreducible if every factorization $f=gh$ is trivial (one factor constant); monic polynomial (highest coefficient 1).
- **Unique Prime Factorization of Polynomials** (Theorem 5.19) — every nonconstant $f(x) \in F[x]$ factors into irreducibles, unique up to order and constant multiples.
- **Root of multiplicity $k$** (Def 5.20) — $(x-a)^k \mid f(x)$ but $(x-a)^{k+1} \nmid f(x)$.
- **Corollary 5.22** — number of roots (with multiplicity) of a nonconstant polynomial cannot exceed its degree.
- **Fundamental Theorem of Algebra** (Example 5.15.9, used in Theorem 5.23) — every nonconstant polynomial in $\mathbb{C}[x]$ has a root in $\mathbb{C}$; consequently the number of roots in $\mathbb{C}$, counted with multiplicity, equals the degree.
- **Eisenstein's Irreducibility Criterion** (Notes) — a criterion using a prime $p$ dividing all but the leading coefficient and $p^2$ not dividing the constant term, used to prove irreducibility of cyclotomic polynomials $x^{p-1}+\cdots+x+1$.

**Key Questions:**
1. Why can the greatest common divisor of two polynomials not be defined as "the largest" divisor, unlike the integer case, and how does the book instead characterize it?
2. What is the structural analogy between the proof of unique factorization for polynomials and the proof of the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic for integers, and where does the analogy break down (e.g. "smallest factors" versus "smallest degree")?
3. Why does the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra guarantee that a polynomial with real (or rational) coefficients has exactly $\deg f$ roots when counted in $\mathbb{C}$ with multiplicity, even though it may have no roots at all in the original field?

### Chapter 6: The Field Generated by an Element (pp. 155–168)

**Summary:** Resolves the two questions left open in Chapter 4 — whether $F[a]=F(a)$ and how to compute $[F(a):F]$ — by introducing the minimal polynomial of an algebraic element $a$ over $F$, proving it is irreducible, unique among monic least-degree elements of $I_{F,a}$, and that it determines both the equality $F[a]=F(a)$ and an explicit power basis for the extension. : [[The-Field-Generated-by-an-Element-and-Minimal-Polynomials|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts:**
- **$I_{F,a}$** (Def 6.2) — the set of all polynomials in $F[x]$ satisfied by $a$.
- **Theorem 6.1** — if $F[a]=F(a)$ then $a$ is algebraic over $F$.
- **Theorem 6.4** — the nonzero polynomial $p(x) \in I_{F,a}$ of least degree is irreducible and divides every element of $I_{F,a}$.
- **Corollary 6.5, 6.6** — any two least-degree elements of $I_{F,a}$ are constant multiples of each other; $I_{F,a}$ equals the set of all multiples of any least-degree element.
- **Minimal polynomial** $m_{F,a}$ (Def 6.8) — the unique monic polynomial in $I_{F,a}$ of least degree; irreducible and divides every polynomial satisfied by $a$.
- **Theorem 6.9** — any irreducible polynomial in $I_{F,a}$ has least degree; if also monic, it equals $m_{F,a}$.
- **Theorem 6.10 (culminating result)** — if $\deg(m_{F,a})=n$, then (1) $F[a]=F(a)$, (2) $[F(a):F]=n$, and (3) $\{1,a,a^2,\ldots,a^{n-1}\}$ is an $F$-basis for $F(a)$.

**Key Questions:**
1. Why must the minimal polynomial of $a$ over $F$ be irreducible, and how does this fact get used to prove $F[a]=F(a)$?
2. How does Theorem 6.10 let us compute the degree of a field extension $F(a)/F$ entirely from data about $a$ (its minimal polynomial), without ever leaving $F$?
3. Why do the distinct powers $1, a, \ldots, a^{n-1}$ have to be linearly independent over $F$ when $n = \deg(m_{F,a})$?

### Chapter 7: Straightedge and Compass Constructions (pp. 169–184)

**Summary:** Translates the geometric process of straightedge-and-compass construction into field theory — showing that every constructible point's coordinates lie in a tower of quadratic extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$ — and uses this criterion, together with the results of Chapters 4–6, to prove the classical impossibility of trisecting an arbitrary angle, squaring the circle, and doubling the cube. : [[Straightedge-and-Compass-Constructibility|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts:**
- **Constructible real number** (Def 7.1) — a line segment of that length can be built with straightedge and compass.
- **Plane of $F$, line of $F$, circle of $F$** (Def 7.3) — points with coordinates in $F$; lines/circles whose equations have coefficients in $F$.
- **Lemma 7.2** — the plane of $\mathbb{Q}$ is constructible.
- **Proposition 7.5** — intersecting lines/circles of $F$ yields coordinates in $F(\sqrt d)$ for some nonnegative $d \in F$.
- **Lemma 7.7** — conversely, if $F$ is constructible then $F(\sqrt d)$ is constructible for nonnegative $d \in F$.
- **Theorem 7.9 (constructibility criterion)** — $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ is constructible iff $\alpha$ lies in a field $K$ with a tower $\mathbb{Q}=K_0 \subseteq K_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq K_t = K$, each $K_i = K_{i-1}(\sqrt{d_{i-1}})$.
- **Corollary 7.10 (necessary condition)** — a constructible $\alpha$ must be algebraic over $\mathbb{Q}$ with minimal polynomial of degree a power of 2 (converse false — noted explicitly in the Notes).
- **Theorem 7.11, 7.12, 7.13** — impossibility of trisecting $60°$, squaring the circle (via transcendence of $\pi$), and doubling the cube (via irreducibility of $x^3-2$).

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does intersecting two lines, a line and a circle, or two circles of a field $F$ always land you in at worst a quadratic extension $F(\sqrt d)$, and never anything of higher degree in one step?
2. Why is Corollary 7.10 only a *necessary*, not sufficient, condition for constructibility — what extra structure (the tower of quadratic extensions, not just the power-of-2 degree) does Theorem 7.9 actually require?
3. How do the three impossibility proofs (trisection, squaring the circle, doubling the cube) each reduce to computing or bounding the degree of a specific minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$?
