# Handbook of Constraint Programming — Guidelines

## Header

**Title:** Handbook of Constraint Programming
**Author(s):** Francesca Rossi, Peter van Beek, Toby Walsh (Editors); 30+ contributing chapter authors
**Publication:** Elsevier, Foundations of Artificial Intelligence series, 2006 (ISBN-13: 978-0-444-52726-4)

**Brief Summary:**
This handbook is an encyclopedic, survey-style reference covering the full field of constraint programming: a paradigm in which a user declaratively states the constraints of a combinatorial problem and a general-purpose solver finds a satisfying (or optimal) assignment to the decision variables. Part I covers the foundations — constraint propagation, backtracking and local search, global constraints, tractability and complexity, and modelling. Part II covers languages, systems, extensions (soft, continuous, structured, temporal, distributed, uncertain constraints), and major application domains (scheduling, vehicle routing, configuration, networks, bioinformatics). Each of the 26 chapters is a self-contained survey written by leading researchers in that subtopic.

**Intent of the Author:**
The editors' explicit aim is to capture the full breadth and depth of constraint programming in one encyclopedic reference, complementing existing textbooks (which focus narrowly on core notions and techniques) by also covering extensions, applications, and languages. It is written for researchers, graduate students, upper-year undergraduates, and practitioners, requiring no prior background in the field, and is meant to enable cross-fertilization with other research areas.

## Chapter Summaries

### Chapter 1: Introduction (pp. 3–12)

**Summary:** The editors (Rossi, van Beek, Walsh) frame constraint programming as a declarative paradigm — the user states relations (constraints) among decision variables and a general-purpose solver finds a satisfying or optimal assignment — and lay out the handbook's purpose and two-part structure, previewing every subsequent chapter's contribution.

**Key Definitions & Concepts:**
- Constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) — decision variables plus constraints (relations) that must hold among them; a constraint solver finds an assignment satisfying all constraints.
- Constraint programming as "programming" in two senses — declarative ("mathematical programming," stating constraints on feasible solutions) and procedural ("computer programming," since an efficient search strategy must still be programmed, e.g. in constraint logic programming).
- Systematic search (backtracking, branch and bound) vs. local search (incomplete but often effective); constraint propagation (inference that reduces the search space by propagating information between neighboring constraints).
- The handbook's two-part organization — Part I: Foundations (history, propagation, search, global constraints, tractability/complexity, modelling); Part II: Extensions, Languages, and Applications (CLP, procedural/concurrent languages, finite-domain systems, OR integration, continuous/structured/temporal/distributed/uncertain extensions, and application domains).

**Key Questions:**
1. In what sense is constraint programming "programming" in both the mathematical-programming sense and the computer-programming sense, and why does the editors' framing suggest that a purely declarative constraint statement is rarely sufficient in practice?
2. Why do the editors treat constraint propagation and tractability/complexity results as separate but complementary responses to the same underlying difficulty (the worst-case intractability of CSPs)?

### Chapter 2: Constraint Satisfaction: An Emerging Paradigm (pp. 13–28)

**Summary:** A historical survey (1965–1985) by Freuder and Mackworth tracing the emergence of constraint satisfaction as a distinct AI/CS paradigm, following the "language stream" (constraint programming languages, from Sketchpad to Prolog and CHIP) and the "algorithm stream" (consistency algorithms and search, from Waltz filtering to Mackworth's and Freuder's work). It sets historical context for the rest of the handbook by showing how representation (CSP formulation), inference (constraint propagation/network consistency), and search (backtracking) developed together. : [[Randomness-and-Phase-Transitions|Link1]], [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link2]], [[Backtracking-Search|Link3]], [[Temporal-Constraint-Satisfaction|Link4]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **2.1 The Early Days** — constraint satisfaction (finding values for variables subject to constraints on allowed value combinations); language stream (constraint languages: Sketchpad, Absys, CONSTRAINTS, ThingLab, Alice, Prolog, CHIP, CLP($\mathcal{X}$)); algorithm stream (Waltz filtering/arc consistency, Montanari's path consistency, Mackworth's general consistency framework, Freuder's $k$-consistency).
- **2.2.1 Representation** — CSP $P = \langle X, D, C \rangle$: variables $X$, domains $D$, constraints $C = \langle C_1,\dots,C_t\rangle$ each a pair $\langle R_{S_j}, S_j\rangle$ (relation over scope); solution (n-tuple satisfying all constraints); unsatisfiable CSP; constraint graph/hypergraph representation; Constraint Optimization Problem (COP, adds a cost function to minimize).
- **2.2.2 Reasoning: Inference and Search** — search space $\Omega = \bowtie_i D_i$ (relational join of domains); inference (constraint propagation eliminates subspaces) vs. search (systematic exploration); conjunctivity of CSPs underlies both strategies. : [[Soft-Constraints-and-Preferences|Link1]], [[Tractability-and-Computational-Complexity-of-CSPs|Link2]]
- **2.2.3 Inference: Constraint Propagation Using Network Consistency** — thrashing (repeated exploration of essentially identical failing subtrees); node consistency ($D_i \subseteq R_i$); arc consistency (AC-1, AC-2/Waltz, AC-3 by Mackworth); path consistency (PC-1, PC-2); generalized arc consistency (GAC) for hyperedges/non-binary constraints; $k$-consistency (given consistent values for $k-1$ variables, a $k$th consistent value exists); $(i,j)$-consistency (generalization of $k$-consistency). : [[Constraint-Propagation-and-Local-Consistency|Link1]], [[Backtracking-Search|Link2]]
- **2.2.4 Search: Backtracking** — backtrack search (Golomb and Baumert 1965, "backtrack programming"); fail-first principle (choose the variable/domain most likely to fail first); preclusion/forward checking (lookahead pruning); dependency-directed backtracking, backjumping, intelligent backtracking (non-chronological backtracking methods); nogoods (learned failure explanations); backmarking. : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link1]], [[Backtracking-Search|Link2]], [[Foundations-and-History-of-Constraint-Satisfaction|Link3]]
- **2.2.5 Analysis** — thrashing/complexity analyses (Haralick and Elliott's statistical analysis, Nudel's expected complexities, Mackworth and Freuder's polynomial-time complexity results for arc/path consistency); structural tractability (Freuder's "width" of a constraint graph, connection to backtrack-free search).

**Key Questions:**
1. How did the "language stream" (constraint programming languages) and "algorithm stream" (consistency algorithms) diverge and why did the field need to reintegrate them into a single constraint programming community in the 1990s?
2. Why does achieving local consistency (e.g., arc or path consistency) with non-empty domains not guarantee a global solution, and what does this imply about the limits of inference alone?
3. In what sense is "the fail-first principle" (choosing the most constrained variable) and "preclusion" (forward checking) already visible in Golomb and Baumert's 1965 paper, and why did it take another 15 years for these ideas to be formalized and widely adopted?

### Chapter 3: Constraint Propagation (pp. 29–77)

**Summary:** Bessière's comprehensive survey formalizes constraint propagation via two complementary lenses — local consistencies (properties a network must satisfy) and rules iteration (properties of the propagation process) — then systematically works through arc consistency algorithms (AC-1 through AC-7, AC2001), stronger consistencies (path consistency, $k$-consistency, singleton consistencies), weaker/cheaper consistencies (forward checking, bound/range consistency), and how global constraints and solver architectures exploit constraint-specific semantics. : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link1]], [[Backtracking-Search|Link2]], [[Constraint-Propagation-and-Local-Consistency|Link3]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **3.1 Background** — constraint (relation over a scheme of variables), constraint network $N=(X,D,C)$; instantiation (locally consistent, globally consistent); normalized/binary/embedded networks.
- **3.2 Formal Viewpoint** — preorder $\preceq$ on networks (nogood-equivalence); space $\mathcal{P}_N$ of tightenings of $N$; globally consistent network (guarantees backtrack-free search but is exponential to build); domain-based tightenings $\mathcal{P}_N^D$; stability under union; $\Phi$-closure (unique smallest $\Phi$-consistent network, computable by greedy fixpoint iteration if $\Phi$ is stable under union and polynomial to check). : [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link]]
- **3.3 Arc Consistency** — (generalized) arc consistency ((G)AC): every value has a support; complexity results (GACSupport, IsItGAC, NoGACWipeOut, MaxGAC, GACDomain — all NP-hard in general, with dependency relations); AC-3/GAC-3 (coarse-grained, $O(er^3d^{r+1})$, or $O(ed^3)$ binary); AC-4 (fine-grained, optimal $O(ed^2)$ time but $O(ed^2)$ space via support counters); AC-6 (lazy support search, $O(ed)$ space); AC2001 (optimal coarse-grained algorithm using "Last" support pointers); bidirectionality (AC7, DEE, ACb); propagation-list ordering heuristics (Wallace–Freuder, constrainedness $\kappa_{ac}$). : [[Constraint-Propagation-and-Local-Consistency|Link1]], [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link2]]
- **3.4 Higher Order Consistencies** — path consistency (Montanari) and 2-path consistency (equivalent); $k$-consistency (Freuder) and strong $k$-consistency; adaptive consistency (Dechter and Pearl, guarantees backtrack-free search along a variable ordering); Montanari's decomposability/minimal network (central problem — building the minimal network is intractable, and even generating a solution from it is not backtrack-free unless $\Pi_2^P=\Sigma_2^P$); consistencies based on constraints (pairwise consistency, $k$-wise consistency, hyper $k$-consistency, relational $(i,m)$-consistency, Dechter–van Beek). : [[Constraint-Propagation-and-Local-Consistency|Link]]
- **3.5 Domain-Based Consistencies Stronger than AC** — Restricted Path Consistency (RPC); Path Inverse Consistency (PIC, $k$-inverse consistency); max-Restricted Path Consistency (maxRPC); Neighborhood Inverse Consistency (NIC); Singleton Arc Consistency (SAC — assign a value, check the resulting subproblem is not AC-inconsistent; SAC1, SAC2, SAC-Opt, SAC3). : [[Constraint-Propagation-and-Local-Consistency|Link]]
- **3.6 Domain-Based Consistencies Weaker than AC** — Directional Arc Consistency (DAC, w.r.t. a variable ordering); Forward Checking (FC, as a local consistency notion); Partial/Full Lookahead (PL, FL); bound(Z)/range/bound(D)/bound(R) consistency (using ordered-domain bounds instead of full support search — e.g., BC(Z) can be NP-hard even for a binary constraint, while it is cheap for linear/sum constraints). : [[Constraint-Propagation-and-Local-Consistency|Link]]
- **3.7 Constraint Propagation as Iteration of Reduction Rules** — reduction rule/propagator; monotonicity, idempotence, commutativity; least fixpoint (Generic-Iteration algorithm; Direct-Iteration for commuting idempotent monotonic propagators). : [[Constraint-Propagation-and-Local-Consistency|Link]]
- **3.8 Specific Constraints** — event types (RemValue, IncMin, DecMax, Instantiate) for efficient incremental propagators (e.g. AC5 schema); constraint entailment; global constraints (decomposition, GAC-polytime decomposition, hidden variable encoding); alldifferent via bipartite matching (Régin); constructive disjunction; priorities in the propagation list (CHOCO's 7-level scheme). : [[Soft-Constraints-and-Preferences|Link1]], [[Global-Constraints|Link2]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why is the distinction between "local consistency" (a declarative property of the output network) and "rules iteration" (a procedural characterization of propagation) important, and what guarantees does monotonicity of propagators buy an implementation (Proposition 3.80/3.81)?
2. Why does the intuitive equivalence between arc consistency and 2-consistency break down on non-normalized binary networks and on non-binary networks (Example 3.25), and what practical confusion has this historically caused?
3. Why is bound(Z) consistency sometimes exponentially harder to decide than arc consistency itself (Proposition 3.73), even though it appears to be a "weaker" notion — and what does this imply about when bound consistency is actually a useful compromise?

### Chapter 4: Backtracking Search Algorithms (pp. 85–129)

**Summary:** Van Beek surveys the major techniques for improving backtracking search on CSPs — branching strategies, constraint propagation, nogood recording, non-chronological backtracking (backjumping), variable/value ordering heuristics, randomization and restarts, and best-first/discrepancy-based search — and reviews both empirical and theoretical (proof-complexity and partial-order) methods for comparing the resulting algorithms. : [[Backtracking-Search|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **4.1 Preliminaries** — CSP definition; deadend (node with no consistent extension); naive backtracking (BT).
- **4.2 Branching Strategies** — enumeration/d-way branching, binary choice points/2-way branching, domain splitting; Hwang–Mitchell result that 2-way branching can be exponentially more powerful than d-way branching (for a fixed CSP model). : [[Backtracking-Search|Link]]
- **4.3 Constraint Propagation** — maintaining arc consistency (MAC) vs. forward checking (FC, arc consistency on constraints with exactly one uninstantiated variable) vs. DPLL's unit propagation (equivalent to FC on SAT); minimal domains (finding them is as hard as solving the CSP); maintaining strong $k$-consistency (MC$_k$). : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link1]], [[Backtracking-Search|Link2]], [[Constraint-Propagation-and-Local-Consistency|Link3]]
- **4.4 Nogood Recording** — nogood (a set of assignments/branching constraints inconsistent with any solution); jumpback nogood (Definition 4.5, recursively defined from failed leaves); eliminating explanation (accounts for a value's removal under constraint propagation); generalized nogoods (allow $x_i \neq a_i$ conjuncts, cf. Katsirelos & Bacchus); nogood database management — $i^{th}$-order size-bounded (Dechter) vs. relevance-bounded (Ginsberg's DBT, Bayardo–Miranker) nogood recording/deletion; watch literals (efficient large-nogood management, enabling 100–200-order recording in modern SAT solvers). : [[Backtracking-Search|Link]]
- **4.5 Non-Chronological Backtracking** — backjumping (Gaschnig's BJ, only at all-leaf deadends); Conflict-directed Backjumping (CBJ, Prosser — backjumps from any deadend using the jumpback nogood); conflict set ($O(n^2)$ data structure); Partial Order Backtracking (Bruynooghe) and Dynamic Backtracking (DBT, Ginsberg — retains nogoods across backjumps, risking exponential degradation with dynamic variable ordering); Partial order dynamic backtracking (PBT, Ginsberg & McAllester). : [[Backtracking-Search|Link]]
- **4.6 Heuristics for Backtracking Algorithms** — variable ordering by domain size: dom (Golomb & Baumert, "fail-first"), dom+deg (Brélaz), dom/deg, dom/wdeg (weighted degree from deadend-responsible constraints); structure-guided heuristics: width (Freuder, backtrack-free if strong $k$-consistency exceeds width), cycle-cutting, bandwidth, graph-separator-based divide-and-conquer; value ordering heuristics based on solution-count/promise estimates (Ginsberg et al.'s product heuristic, Geelen, Frost & Dechter's summation/"min-conflicts" heuristic). : [[Backtracking-Search|Link]]
- **4.7 Randomization and Restart Strategies** — restart strategy $S=(t_1,t_2,\dots)$; Luby's optimal fixed-cutoff strategy and universal strategy $S_u$; heavy-tailed runtime distributions; Van Moorsel & Wolter's necessary/sufficient condition $E[T] < E[T-t \mid T>t]$ for restarts to help; value mistake (Harvey) vs. backdoor mistake (Williams, Gomes, Selman) as competing explanations for heavy tails. : [[Backtracking-Search|Link]]
- **4.8 Best-First Search** — discrepancy (deviation from the value-ordering heuristic); Limited Discrepancy Search (Harvey & Ginsberg); depth-bounded discrepancy search (Walsh); interleaved depth-first search (Meseguer). : [[Backtracking-Search|Link]]
- **4.9 Optimization** — objective constraint; branch-and-bound via CSP re-solving with $c < f(S)$ added. : [[Constraint-Logic-Programming|Link1]], [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link2]]
- **4.10 Comparing Backtracking Algorithms** — proof complexity (resolution refutation proofs, tree vs. unrestricted resolution — DPLL+nogood-recording can be exponentially better than plain DPLL); partial order/hierarchy on algorithms by nodes visited (Kondrak & van Beek; Chen & van Beek's backjump-level analysis showing MC$_k$ dominates BJ$_j$ for $j \le k$). : [[Backtracking-Search|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why do CBJ and FC-CBJ (and more generally MC$_k$-CBJ and MC$_{k+1}$-CBJ) turn out to be incomparable — each exponentially better than the other on some instances — despite CBJ seeming like a strict improvement over chronological backtracking?
2. What explains the apparent contradiction between early experiments (1979–1995) finding MAC worse than forward checking and later experiments (post-1994, Sabin & Freuder) finding the opposite, and what does this reveal about the risk of generalizing from "easy" benchmarks?
3. The "value mistake" theory (Harvey) and "backdoor mistake" theory (Williams et al.) offer different accounts of why restarts eliminate heavy-tailed behavior — what evidence does each explain that the other cannot (e.g., random value ordering + restarts removing heavy tails without changing variable-ordering mistake probability)?

### Chapter 5: Local Search Methods (pp. 135–159)

**Summary:** Hoos and Tsang survey stochastic local search (SLS) methods for CSP and SAT — from the simplest iterative-improvement algorithms (Min-Conflicts, GSAT) through randomized, tabu-search, and penalty-based (dynamic local search) variants — emphasizing how each family escapes local minima and comparing their completeness guarantees and empirical performance. : [[Local-Search-Methods|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts:**
- Stochastic Local Search (SLS) algorithm — formally defined by search space, solution set, neighbourhood relation, memory states, initialisation function, step function, and termination predicate; deterministic local search is a degenerate special case.
- 1-exchange / 1-flip neighbourhood (differ in one variable's value); evaluation function (e.g., number of violated constraints); Iterative Improvement (II) / hill-climbing — Iterative Best-Improvement vs. Iterative First-Improvement.
- Min-Conflicts Heuristic (MCH) — pick a conflicted variable at random, assign the value minimizing violations; essentially incomplete (can stagnate).
- GSAT — best-improvement flip minimizing unsatisfied clauses, with static restarts; essentially incomplete.
- Randomised Iterative Improvement (RII) / random walk step — with probability $wp$ take a uniformly random neighbour; probabilistically approximately complete (PAC) if the neighbourhood graph is connected.
- WMCH (MCH + random walk) and GWSAT (GSAT + conflict-directed random walk, inspired by Papadimitriou's 2-SAT algorithm) — both PAC for $wp>0$.
- WalkSAT/SKC — two-stage selection (pick unsatisfied clause, then a variable via zero-damage/greedy/random-walk rule using scoreb); WalkSAT architecture generalizes this.
- Tabu Search — short-term memory (tabu tenure) forbidding reintroduction of recently removed values, with an aspiration criterion overriding tabu status on improvements; TMCH, TS-GH (Galinier & Hao, best-improvement + tabu, one of the strongest CSP SLS algorithms), GSAT/Tabu, WalkSAT/Tabu.
- Novelty / Novelty+ / R-Novelty(+) / Adaptive Novelty+ — use variable "age" to avoid flip-flopping; Novelty is essentially incomplete but Novelty+ (added random walk) is PAC; Adaptive Novelty+ auto-tunes noise.
- Penalty-based / Dynamic Local Search (DLS) — modify the evaluation function via penalty weights on violated constraints/clauses to escape local minima and diversify search (rather than purely to "learn" important constraints).
- GENET / Breakout Method — early penalty-based CSP algorithms (neural-network-style / per-constraint weights incremented at local minima).
- Guided Local Search (GLS) — augmented evaluation function $g'(a) = g(a) + \lambda\sum p_i I_i(a)$; utility-based penalty selection $util_i(a) = I_i(a)\cdot c(i)/(1+p_i)$; single tunable parameter $\lambda$.
- Discrete Lagrangian Method (DLM) — dynamic local search inspired by Lagrangian multipliers for continuous constrained optimisation, though rigorous continuous-optimisation guarantees do not carry over.
- ESG / SAPS / RSAPS — exponentiated-subgradient-inspired clause-weighting with scaling and (probabilistic) smoothing stages; SAPS improves on ESG's efficiency via restricted scaling and probabilistic smoothing; RSAPS adapts the smoothing probability reactively.
- Systematic Local Search — achieves completeness by recording and resolving no-goods at local minima (contrasts with the (in)completeness of most SLS methods).
- MAX-CSP / Weighted MAX-CSP / MAX-SAT / Weighted MAX-SAT — optimisation variants where local search naturally applies via the same evaluation-function machinery.

**Key Questions:**
1. Why do most of the highest-performing "penalty-based" (dynamic local search) algorithms owe their success primarily to search diversification rather than to the originally intended goal of "learning" which constraints are important?
2. What is the essential structural difference between fine-grained algorithms like Novelty/WalkSAT (variable-selection restricted to an unsatisfied clause, using age or score) and the two-stage "randomized iterative improvement" family (WMCH/GWSAT), and how does this difference explain why Novelty is provably essentially incomplete for any fixed noise setting while GWSAT is PAC?
3. In what sense can "local search" be made complete (Systematic Local Search), and why does this completeness require a potentially exponential nogood database despite otherwise resembling ordinary incomplete local search?

### Chapter 7: Tractable Structures for Constraint Satisfaction Problems (pp. 209–244)

**Summary:** Dechter surveys graph-theoretic parameters (tree-width/induced-width, hypertree width, cycle-cutset size, path-width) that control the worst-case complexity of solving a CSP by inference (variable elimination, tree/cluster clustering), by hybrids of search and inference (cutset conditioning), and by structured search (AND/OR search spaces), showing that all these algorithms have complexity bounded exponentially in some such parameter of the constraint graph. : [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link1]], [[Temporal-Constraint-Satisfaction|Link2]]

- **7.4 Structure-Based Tractability in Search** — AND/OR search tree (built from a DFS spanning tree or pseudo-tree; OR nodes = variables, AND nodes = value choices), size $O(n\cdot k^m)$ for pseudo-tree depth $m$, and $O(k^{w^*\cdot \log n})$ via tree-width; graph-based backjumping (GBJ) exploits the same graph independencies during search; AND/OR search graphs (merging unifiable/identical subtrees, using caching), minimal AND/OR graph size $O(n\cdot k^{w^*})$; path-width $pw^*$ bounds the OR-graph size, with $w^*\le m^*\le pw^*\le w^*\log n$. : [[Tractability-and-Computational-Complexity-of-CSPs|Link]]

### Chapter 8: The Complexity of Constraint Languages (pp. 245–280)

**Summary:** Cohen and Jeavons develop the algebraic theory of constraint-language complexity: the complexity of $CSP(\Gamma)$ over a finite domain depends only on the relational clone $\langle\Gamma\rangle$ generated by $\Gamma$, which is characterized by its polymorphisms $Pol(\Gamma)$, translating the tractability question into a question about finite algebras — culminating in a general dichotomy conjecture and several sufficient conditions (semilattice, near-unanimity, Mal'tsev polymorphisms) that guarantee tractability. : [[Tractability-and-Computational-Complexity-of-CSPs|Link1]], [[Constraints-in-Procedural-Concurrent-and-Rule-Based-Languages|Link2]], [[Temporal-Constraint-Satisfaction|Link3]], [[Symmetry-in-Constraint-Programming|Link4]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **8.1 Basic Definitions** — constraint language $\Gamma\subseteq R_D$; $CSP(\Gamma)$ parameterized decision problem; tractable language (every finite subset solvable in P) vs NP-complete language; the general finite-domain dichotomy is still open (known for $|D|=2,3$). : [[Randomness-and-Phase-Transitions|Link]]
- **8.2 Examples** — $\Gamma_{LIN}$ (linear equations, tractable via Gaussian elimination); Schaefer's Dichotomy Theorem for Boolean languages (six tractable cases: 0-valid, 1-valid, Horn, dual-Horn, bijunctive/2-clause, affine/linear over GF(2)); max-closed constraints; $\Gamma_{ZOA}$ (0/1/all / implicational relations); connected row-convex constraints; betweenness relation $B_D$ (tractable iff $|D|\le4$); linear Horn constraints.

- **8.5 Infinite Domains** — $\langle\Gamma\rangle\subseteq Inv(Pol(\Gamma))$ can be strict for infinite $D$, but equality holds when the relational structure is $\omega$-categorical (Theorem 8.56); Allen's Interval Algebra (13 basic relations, $2^{13}$ total) classified into 18 maximal tractable subalgebras (Theorem 8.57). : [[Tractability-and-Computational-Complexity-of-CSPs|Link]]
- **8.6 Multi-Sorted Constraint Languages** — multi-sorted relation/CSP (variables can have different domains with a signature); tractability can depend on signature alone, not just tuples (Example 8.62); conversion $\chi(R)$ to a one-sorted language preserves tractability; multi-sorted polymorphisms can combine constant/semilattice/near-unanimity/affine operations *per sort* to still guarantee tractability (Theorem 8.68). : [[Tractability-and-Computational-Complexity-of-CSPs|Link]]
- **8.7 Alternative Approaches** — CSP as the Homomorphism Problem between relational structures; connections to descriptive complexity / Datalog (Feder–Vardi); disjunctive combination $\Gamma\times_\vee\Delta$ of languages and $k$-independence conditions (Theorems 8.79, 8.81) giving tractability criteria for disjunctive combinations (e.g. linear Horn constraints, connected row-convex constraints derived this way). : [[Tractability-and-Computational-Complexity-of-CSPs|Link1]], [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link2]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does reducing the tractability question to *relational clones*, and then to *polymorphisms*, actually make the classification problem more tractable rather than just relabeling it?
2. What is the intuitive reason a near-unanimity polymorphism of arity $k$ guarantees that enforcing $k$-consistency achieves *global* consistency, while a semilattice or Mal'tsev polymorphism does not need this?
3. Why can multi-sorted constraint languages be tractable even when treating them as a single (one-sorted) domain over the union of all sorts would appear NP-complete (as in Example 8.61/8.62) — what does the signature restriction actually buy computationally?

### Chapter 10: Symmetry in Constraint Programming (pp. 329–376)

**Summary:** Gent, Petrie and Puget give a group-theoretic foundation for symmetry in CSPs and survey the three main families of symmetry-breaking techniques — problem reformulation, static symmetry-breaking constraints (lex-leader and its specializations for matrices and all-different), and dynamic methods applied during search (SBDS, SBDD, STAB, and their computational-group-theory-powered variants) — along with applications, combinations, and open problems in automatic symmetry detection. : [[Symmetry-in-Constraint-Programming|Link]]

- **10.1 Symmetries and Group Theory** — permutation, Cauchy/cyclic form, composition $f\circ g$; group axioms (closure, identity, inverse, associativity); order of a group $|G|$; generators, $G=\langle S\rangle$; subgroup; (right) coset $H\circ g$, index $|G:H|$, transversal; orbit $\delta^G$; stabiliser $G_\beta$; symmetric group $S_n$; Schreier–Sims algorithm (constructs a stabiliser chain $G_0\supseteq\dots\supseteq G_n$ and coset representatives, foundational for computational group theory in CP). : [[Symmetry-in-Constraint-Programming|Link]]

- **10.4 Adding Constraints Before Search** — lex-leader method (Crawford, Ginsberg, Luks, Roy): for each $g\in G$ add $V\preceq_{lex}V^g$, selecting the lexicographically-least representative of each symmetry class, but requires one constraint per group element (potentially exponential) and can conflict with search heuristics since it doesn't "respect" them; simplifying/pruning redundant lex-leader constraints; Puget's result that with an all-different constraint, arbitrary variable-symmetry groups can be broken with only $n-1$ binary constraints (Theorem 10.24, via $s(g),t(g)$ = first-moved point and its image); specialized ordering constraints for matrix models (double-lex on rows and columns, multiset ordering, allperm), with efficient $O(nb)$ GAC propagation for the $lex$ constraint. : [[Symmetry-in-Constraint-Programming|Link]]
- **10.5 Dynamic Symmetry Breaking** — SBDS (Symmetry Breaking During Search, Backofen & Will / Gent & Smith): on backtracking from $var=val$, post $A^g\Rightarrow(var\ne val)^g$ for each symmetry $g$, sound and complete, respects search heuristics; SBDD (Symmetry Breaking via Dominance Detection, Focacci & Milano / Fahle, Schamberger & Sellmann): prune a search node if it is dominated by (symmetric to an extension of) a completed no-good subtree, via a dominance check $\Phi$; STAB (Symmetry Breaking Using Stabilizers): posts lex-ordering constraints only for the stabiliser $G_A$ of the current partial assignment (cheaper but incomplete); GAP-SBDS/GAP-SBDD link SBDS/SBDD to the computational-group-theory package GAP so only generators (not all group elements) need be supplied, scaling symmetry-group size from thousands to billions; GE-trees (Group-Equivalence trees) as a unifying conceptual framework covering SBDS, SBDD, and lex-leader. : [[Symmetry-in-Constraint-Programming|Link]]
- **10.6–10.9 Combinations / Applications / Detection** — naively combining symmetry-breaking methods can lose solutions (shown by Smith); variable-symmetry and value-symmetry breaking can be safely combined since they act on disjoint groups; automatic symmetry detection via microstructure-graph or constraint-parse-tree automorphism (Puget; Ramani & Markov); further themes: symmetry-aware inference/propagation, symmetry and implied constraints, symmetry vs. local search (breaking symmetry can hurt local search — Prestwich), "almost symmetries" and dominance relations.

1. Why must dynamic methods like SBDS and SBDD add symmetry-related information *conditionally* (only once a symmetry is known to still hold, e.g. via $A^g$) rather than statically ruling out $(var\ne val)^g$ outright — what would go wrong otherwise?

3. Why can naively combining two independently-correct symmetry-breaking methods (e.g., lex-leader row/column ordering plus SBDD) still lose solutions, even though each method alone is sound?

- **11.2 Representing a Problem** — a CSP $M$ models a problem $P$ if every solution of $M$ maps to a solution of $P$ and every solution of $P$ is reachable from some solution of $M$ (correspondence can be many-to-many due to symmetry introduced by modelling).
- **11.3 Propagation and Search** — standard search interleaves choice points (binary branching, $k$-way branching, domain splitting) with constraint propagation to a target local-consistency level per constraint; solvers typically maintain AC on some binary constraints, BC on arithmetic constraints, and full GAC only for global constraints with efficient filtering — this trade-off (Puget's remark on ILOG Solver) drives most modelling decisions; model/algorithm/heuristic choices interact and cannot be optimized independently (Beacham et al.). : [[Backtracking-Search|Link1]], [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link2]]
- **11.4 Viewpoints** — a viewpoint $\langle X,D\rangle$ gives variables/domains a meaning in terms of $P$; different viewpoints (e.g. Nadel's nine models of $n$-queens) yield fundamentally different models; good viewpoints let constraints be expressed concisely with efficient propagation.
- **11.5 Expressing the Constraints** — combining constraints with the same/overlapping scope (conjunction) can strengthen propagation beyond the separate constraints (Katsirelos & Bacchus); eliminating variables via substitution using a linear equation strengthens bounds/domain propagation (Harvey & Stuckey); global constraints (e.g. allDifferent) let the user choose consistency level (AC-as-cliques, BC, or GAC) trading propagation strength for cost; extensional (table/case) constraints allow explicit enforcement of AC/GAC on arbitrary relations; reified constraints (0/1 variable linked to whether a constraint holds) and meta-constraints/cardinality operator express disjunctions of constraints. : [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link]]
- **11.6 Auxiliary Variables** — variables added purely to make constraints expressible or better-propagating (e.g. Boolean $o_{ij}$ variables for car-sequencing option capacities); can also serve as extra search variables (SONET ring-count example) to simplify decision order. : [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link]]
- **11.7 Implied (Redundant) Constraints** — logically entailed constraints added purely to prune search earlier; must forbid an assignment the existing propagation would otherwise allow; usefulness depends on the search/variable order (Borrett & Tsang); can be superseded by a good global constraint (Régin & Puget's sequencing constraint subsumed Dincbas et al.'s implied constraints); can come from analyzing subproblems (Van Beek & Wilken); automatic generation is only partially solved (Hnich et al.; Frisch et al.'s "eliminate" method). : [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link]]
- **11.8 Reformulations of CSPs** — non-binary-to-binary translations: hidden-variable (adds an auxiliary variable per constraint) and dual-graph transformation (a variable per constraint, values = satisfying tuples); permutation problems and their dual viewpoint (swap roles of variables and values); Boolean/direct-encoding viewpoint (generally weaker than integer/set models); reformulating from a genuinely different "angle" (open-stacks problem's many viewpoints). : [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link]]
- **11.9 Combining Viewpoints** — channelling constraints link two mutually-redundant models $\langle X_1,D_1,C_1\rangle,\langle X_2,D_2,C_2\rangle$ so propagation in one viewpoint feeds the other (Cheng, Choi, Lee, Wu); model induction (Law & Lee) merges translated constraints into one viewpoint without full combination; minimal dual model keeps only one viewpoint's constraints plus channelling when they're propagation-redundant with the other (Choi, Lee, Stuckey formalize "propagation redundant"); choice of search variables can be either viewpoint or interleaved dynamically; multiple (>2) viewpoints can be combined (quasigroup completion, three mutually dual models). : [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link]]
- **11.10 Symmetry and Modelling** — modelling can introduce spurious symmetry (e.g. labeling indistinguishable queens); set variables and identical-class counting avoid it; symmetry-breaking constraints can additionally enable new implied constraints (template design problem). : [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link]]
- **11.11 Optimization Problems** — Constraint Satisfaction Optimization Problem (CSOP) $\langle X,D,C,f\rangle$; branch-and-bound with tightening bound constraints vs. modelling the objective as a variable solved as a sequence/single CSP; dominance rules (forbid assignments provably no better than some alternative) resemble but are logically weaker than implied constraints. : [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link]]
- **11.12 Supporting Modelling and Reformulation** — folklore heuristics ("reduce variables/constraints") are unreliable rules of thumb; richer models (multiple viewpoints, auxiliary variables, implied constraints) generally outperform minimal ones provided propagation stays efficient; automated model-generation systems (Frisch et al.) and constraint modelling "patterns" are the main current routes to systematizing modelling. : [[Modelling-Constraint-Satisfaction-Problems|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why can adding *more* variables and constraints to a model (auxiliary variables, redundant viewpoints, implied constraints) often reduce search effort, directly contradicting the folk-wisdom heuristics "minimize variables" and "minimize constraints"?
2. In combined (redundant) models linked by channelling constraints, why does propagation in one viewpoint's constraints sometimes trigger useful pruning in the other viewpoint only through the channelling constraints, and under what condition (per Choi, Lee & Stuckey) does one viewpoint's own constraints become entirely propagation-redundant and safely removable?
3. Why does the usefulness of an implied constraint depend on the variable/value ordering used by the search (as in the car-sequencing example), rather than being a fixed property of the constraint itself?

### Chapter 12: Constraint Logic Programming (pp. 409–452)

**Summary:** This chapter presents Constraint Logic Programming (CLP) as the merger of constraint solving with logic programming, giving it a rigorous operational/algebraic/logical semantics (the Jaffar–Lassez CLP scheme) and showing how CLP serves simultaneously as a conceptual modeling language, a design modeling language (via constraint solvers, reification, indexicals, CHR, generalized propagation), and a language for programmer-controlled search — explaining CLP's historical role as the origin of many ideas covered elsewhere in the handbook (global constraints, hybrid solving, concurrent constraint programming). : [[Constraint-Logic-Programming|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **12.1 History of CLP** — CLP originated from Colmerauer's Prolog II/III, Jaffar & Lassez's CLP scheme and CLP(R), and the CHIP language (Dincbas, Van Hentenryck, Simonis); evolution from "black box" to "glass box" constraint solvers that programmers can extend.
- **12.2 Semantics of Constraint Logic Programs** — constraint domain $C$ (signature $\Sigma_C$, domain of computation $D_C$, constraint theory $T_C$, solver $solv_C$); a CLP(C) program is a set of rules $H \mathrel{:-} B$; operational semantics via state reductions $\langle L_1,\dots,L_m \mid c\rangle$; finite failure (Definition 12.5); logical semantics treats each rule as $\forall(A \leftarrow L_1 \land \dots \land L_n)$, with soundness/completeness Theorems 12.8–12.9; algebraic semantics via C-interpretations and the least C-model $lm(P,C)$ (Theorem 12.12–12.13); fixpoint semantics via the immediate consequence operator $T_P^C$ with $lm(P,C) = lfp(T_P^C)$ (Theorem 12.15); Clark completion $P^\star$ gives a logical semantics for finite failure; extensions cover negation (SLDNF, constructive negation), abductive/inductive CLP, answer set programming, and optimization via $\text{minimize}(G,E)$ with logical reading $\exists M(G \land E{=}M \land \neg\exists_{\{M\}}(G \land E{<}M))$. : [[Constraint-Logic-Programming|Link]]
- **12.3 CLP for Conceptual Modeling** — CLP directly models standard CSPs (domain constraints, conjunction/disjunction via multiple rules); defining constraints via built-ins, global constraints (e.g. alldifferent, cumulative), local (existentially quantified) variables, and recursive constraints (e.g. the mortgage constraint); data structures (lists/trees) enable parametric models (e.g. N-queens); optimization and soft constraints via extra penalty arguments. : [[Constraint-Logic-Programming|Link]]
- **12.4 CLP for Design Modeling** — incremental constraint solvers and backtracking (trailing, copying, semantic backtracking); solver completeness/incompleteness (a program is "solver complete" for a goal if the solver decides all its answers); modeling disjunction via multiple rules vs. finite-domain propagation vs. tabling; reified constraints $c[B]$ (Boolean-linked truth value) enabling logical combinations without choice points, though with weaker propagation than expected; redundant constraints to strengthen incomplete solvers; mechanisms for specifying constraint behavior — dynamic scheduling/freeze, attributed variables, indexicals ($X$ in $r$), Constraint Handling Rules (CHR), and generalized propagation (executing a multi-clause definition to extract common information, e.g. "infers most"/"infers ac"). : [[Constraint-Logic-Programming|Link]]
- **12.5 Search in CLP** — search elicits a disjunction of conjunctions equivalent to the original constraints, driving the solver to completeness; reflection predicates (e.g. get_min, get_domain_size) let programs query solver state to build heuristics; search control via variable/value choice and exploration method; programmable heuristics (median value selection, "label earliest", tentative-value/relaxation-based branching); local search via propagation of tentative values (pioneered by Localizer); optimization via branch-and-bound or restart-based minimize/2. : [[Constraint-Logic-Programming|Link]]
- **12.6 Impact of CLP** — CLP originated global constraints, cooperating solvers, constraint databases, concurrent constraint programming (Oz/Mozart), combinations with functional/term-rewriting/imperative paradigms, object-oriented constraint toolkits (ILOG Solver, CHARME), and modern modeling languages (OPL); widespread industrial use (container ports, scheduling, ILOG).
- **12.7 Future of CLP** — strengths (full programming language for modeling, search, hybrid solving) vs. weaknesses (Prolog-inherited awkwardness, no clean cost semantics separate from declarative semantics, need to combine backtracking with local search, runtime overhead vs. potential for parallelism).

**Key Questions:**
1. How does the CLP schema's separation of operational, algebraic, and logical semantics allow the *same* declarative meaning to be given to a "naive" disjunctive definition of a constraint (multiple rules) and to a highly optimized reified/propagator-based definition — and why does the chapter argue this is simultaneously CLP's greatest theoretical strength and a practical weakness (no way to compare models for efficiency)?

### Chapter 13: Constraints in Procedural and Concurrent Languages (pp. 453–494)

**Summary:** This chapter surveys three families of approaches for bringing constraints and search out of logic programming and into other paradigms: (1) toolkits and modeling languages embedding constraint solvers and programmable search into procedural/object-oriented languages (ILOG Solver, OPL, Comet, CLAIRE/SALSA); (2) concurrent constraint programming (CCP), which uses ask-and-tell over a shared constraint store to coordinate concurrent computation, and its generalization in Oz/AKL via first-class computation spaces for programmable search and encapsulation; and (3) Constraint Handling Rules (CHR), a rule-based "glass-box" language specifically designed for writing and reasoning about constraint solvers. : [[Constraints-in-Procedural-Concurrent-and-Rule-Based-Languages|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **13.1 Procedural and Object-Oriented Languages** — design objectives: ease of adoption, flexibility/extensibility, versatility, efficiency; distinguishes constraint *modeling* from *programming the search*; glass-box extensibility (ILOG Solver's demon-based custom constraints) vs. modeling languages (Numerica, OPL, OplScript, CLAIRE/SALSA) that raise abstraction while remaining declarative (no destructive assignment); modeling concerns include aggregation/combinators, static typing, matrix/element-constraint reformulation; search programming via goal-based and-or tree interpreters (ILOG Solver's ILCGOAL macros) versus declarative search-tree specification (OPL's `forall`/`tryall`) versus first-class continuations for search controllers (Comet); exploration strategies as node management policies (evaluation + postponement rules) separated from tree topology; pragmatic issues: control transfer, mixed memory models, debugging support, control abstractions. : [[Constraints-in-Procedural-Concurrent-and-Rule-Based-Languages|Link]]
- **13.2 Concurrent Constraint Programming** — CCLP integrates concurrent logic programming with CLP; agents communicate via a shared constraint store using ask (entailment test) and tell (impose constraint); the CC language framework's abstract syntax ($\text{tell}(c)$, guarded choice $\sum_i \text{ask}(c_i)\to A_i$, parallel composition $A\|A$) with transition rules (Tell, Ask, Composition, Unfold) defining success/failure/deadlock; Oz and AKL integrate CCP with CLP-style problem solving; explicit concurrency control via threads; ports (AKL) and names (Oz) solve many-to-one communication (streams alone only support one-to-many); mutable state via cells; first-class procedures/objects; distributed programming via network transparency. : [[Constraints-in-Procedural-Concurrent-and-Rule-Based-Languages|Link]]
- **13.2.5 Encapsulation and Search** — computation spaces encapsulate speculative computation so failure doesn't affect other computations; stability (Janson & Haridi, AKL) generalizes entailment to determine when a computation is no longer speculative; deep guards allow arbitrary statements (not just constraints) as guards; first-class computation spaces (NewSpace, Ask, Access, Clone, Commit, Inject) let programmers implement arbitrary search engines and combinators (e.g. depth-first all-solutions search coded directly in Oz). : [[Constraint-Logic-Programming|Link]]
- **13.3 Rule-Based Languages (CHR)** — CHR distinguishes built-in (pre-defined) constraints from CHR (user-defined) constraints defined by rules; three rule forms: simplification ($H \Leftrightarrow C \mid B$), propagation ($H \Rightarrow C \mid B$), simpagation ($H \setminus H' \Leftrightarrow C \mid B$); operational semantics via Simplify/Propagate transition steps; declarative (logical) semantics reads $H\Leftrightarrow C\mid B$ as $\forall(C\to(H\leftrightarrow\exists \bar y B))$ and $H\Rightarrow C\mid B$ as $\forall(C\to(H\to\exists\bar y B))$; CHR is Turing-complete (via CHR machines); key program properties: termination, confluence (decidable via joinability of critical pairs from overlapping minimal states), completion (adding rules to restore confluence), and operational equivalence (decidable for terminating confluent programs); example solvers presented in CHR: Boolean constraints, minimum, linear equations (Gaussian-elimination style), finite-domain interval/enumeration propagators, N-queens, and a lexicographic-order global constraint. : [[Constraints-in-Procedural-Concurrent-and-Rule-Based-Languages|Link]]
- **13.4 Challenges and Opportunities** — cooperative/hybrid solvers (LP/IP + CP), orthogonal computation models (constraint-based local search vs. domain filtering), and orthogonal concerns (debugging/tracing/explanation tools at the modeling level).

### Chapter 15: Operations Research Methods in Constraint Programming (pp. 527–570)

**Summary:** This chapter surveys how OR techniques — linear/mixed-integer programming relaxations, cutting planes, Lagrangean relaxation, dynamic programming, branch-and-price, and Benders decomposition — have been imported into constraint programming to strengthen domain filtering, provide search guidance, and enable hybrid decomposition schemes that solve problems neither CP nor OR alone handles efficiently, with numerous documented speedups (Table 15.1). : [[Integration-of-Constraint-Programming-and-Operations-Research|Link]]

- **15.10 Branch-and-Price Methods** — column generation: solve a restricted LP master problem, use reduced costs to find promising new columns via a subproblem, repeat; illustrated on the generalized assignment problem (subproblem = 0-1 knapsack); applications include airline crew rostering, vehicle routing, timetabling. : [[Integration-of-Constraint-Programming-and-Operations-Research|Link1]], [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link2]]

- **15.12 Toward Integration of CP and OR** — double modeling (parallel CP and MILP models exchanging information) vs. deeper unification via shared concepts (logical inference, filtering-as-cutting-planes, conditional constraints, duality, search-infer-and-relax); hybrid solvers: ECLiPSe, OPL Studio, Mosel, SCIP, SIMPL. : [[Integration-of-Constraint-Programming-and-Operations-Research|Link]]

### Chapter 16: Continuous and Interval Constraints (pp. 571–606)

**Summary:** This chapter surveys interval constraint programming, the propagation-based approach to solving nonlinear continuous constraints (equations/inequalities over the reals) that generalizes finite-domain consistency techniques to interval domains, covering interval arithmetic, hull/box/strong (kB) consistency, hybridizations with symbolic algebra and numerical analysis (Newton's method, linear/nonlinear interval solvers), extensions to quantified continuous constraints, and applications/software. : [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link]]

- **16.1 From Discrete to Continuous Constraints** — an interval box $I_x = I_1\times\cdots\times I_n$; relational (generalized) arc consistency over the reals is generally uncomputable, so it is *weakened* to hull consistency: the smallest enclosing interval (interval hull) satisfying domain-reduction rules combining interval arithmetic and constraint inversion; propagation is convergent since reduction rules are contracting and monotonic; precision $\varepsilon$ and bisection-based splitting (bisect the largest domain) drive the search tree to terminal nodes; soundness of enclosing an actual solution can follow from fixed-point theorems (Brouwer, Miranda). : [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link]]
- **16.2 The Branch-and-Reduce Framework** — general algorithm (Table 16.1): $\text{Choose}$ a box, $\text{Reduce}$ it, $\text{Branch}$ to update the covering, $\text{Revise}$ (simplify/bound) — specialized to isolated-solution solving, continuum solving (inner-box approximation), quantified formula solving, and global constrained optimization (reliable bounding + existence proofs). : [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link]]
- **16.3 Consistency Techniques** — interval arithmetic defines set-extended operations ($[a,b]+[c,d]=[a+b,c+d]$, etc.) with outward rounding for soundness; an interval function $F$ is an interval extension of $f$ if $\{f(x):x\in I\}\subseteq F(I)$; hull consistency computed via constraint decomposition into a tree of unary operations and the HC4revise bottom-up/top-down propagation algorithm; box consistency (Definition via Eq. 16.2) relaxes hull consistency into a refutation test over $F$, solved by bisection search, and specifically fixes the "multiple occurrence" elimination problem that hull consistency mishandles (Example 16.8/16.11); strong (kB) consistency ($k\ge 3$) recursively strengthens by fixing one variable at a time and testing $(k{-}1)B$-consistency of the rest, addressing the "locality" problem across multiple constraints (Example 16.12–16.13); acceleration techniques include combining hull/box consistency by variable-occurrence count (BC4 algorithm), weakened dichotomous search, cycle-aware operator scheduling, and Aitken's $\Delta^2$-style bound acceleration. : [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link]]
- **16.4 Numerical Operators** — the (univariate) interval Newton method: $I_{k+1} := I_k \cap (x_k - F(x_k)/F'(I_k))$, converging quadratically near a root and providing existence proofs when $N(I_k,x_k)\subseteq I_k$; solving interval linear systems (NP-hard in general) via the interval Gauss-Seidel method, extended division, transversal choice, and preconditioning; solving nonlinear systems via the multivariate interval Newton method (linearizing via interval Jacobian $J_f$) and reformulation-linearization (introducing new variables for quadratic terms with linear envelope constraints). : [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link]]
- **16.5 Hybrid Techniques** — combining symbolic algorithms (Gaussian elimination, Gröbner bases, expression sharing/DAG representation) with consistency techniques to overcome the locality problem; combining symbolic rewriting (Horner forms, Bernstein polynomials, Taylor models, affine arithmetic) with interval methods to reduce the "overestimation problem" (dependency problem) of naive interval evaluation; combining interval methods (Newton) with consistency techniques (box consistency) as in Numerica's master algorithm. : [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link]]
- **16.6 First Order Constraints** — quantified continuous constraints (e.g. "guaranteed tuning problem" from control theory: $S=\{x\in I:\forall p\in J\, f(x,p)\ge 0\}$); general problem is undecidable with transcendental functions, doubly exponential for polynomials (cylindrical algebraic decomposition, Collins); sub-pavings (covers of a relation classified as inside/outside/boundary boxes) as the CP-relevant computational tool; interval inclusion tests for universal quantifiers; modal/directed (Kaucher) interval arithmetic and the *-semantic extension/theorem linking modal interval computations to proving first-order formulas like $\forall x\in I\,\exists y\in J\, f(x,y)=0$. : [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link1]], [[Temporal-Constraint-Satisfaction|Link2]]
- **16.7 Applications and Software Packages** — engineering design (design platform CE), robotics (workspace computation, Gough-Stewart platform, protein structure), automatic control (parameter/state estimation), computer graphics (camera control); software: interval arithmetic libraries (Boost, Gaol, MPFI), Intlab/C-XSC, constraint solvers (CLP(BNR), ILOG Solver, Prolog IV, Eclipse, RealPaver, ICOS), and global optimization tools (Numerica, GlobSol, BARON). : [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Hull consistency, box consistency, and kB consistency form a hierarchy addressing different failure modes of naive interval propagation: multiple occurrences of a *single* variable within one constraint versus interactions *across* multiple constraints. Using Examples 16.8/16.11 (hull vs. box on $x+x=0$) and 16.12/16.13 (3B on $x+y=0 \land x-y=0$), explain why no amount of strengthening within a single constraint (box consistency) can fix the second failure mode, and why 3B consistency's cost (testing sub-systems at every bound) is the price for fixing it.
2. The chapter states that solving continuous constraints over the reals is uncomputable in general, yet interval methods are described as "complete" and sometimes "sound." Reconcile these claims: what exactly is guaranteed (no solutions lost) versus not guaranteed (termination at exact solutions, or a box provably containing a unique solution), and what role do existence theorems (Brouwer/Miranda) and the interval Newton operator's contraction test $N(I_k,x_k)\subseteq I_k$ play in upgrading completeness to soundness?
3. Modal interval analysis (Section 16.6) reduces proving a quantified formula like $\forall x\in I\,\exists y\in J\,f(x,y)=0$ to computing the *-semantic extension $f^*(\dot I)$ and checking $f^*(\dot I)\subseteq \dot J$. What computational advantage does this reduction offer over directly searching sub-pavings with interval inclusion tests, and why does the chapter present both approaches rather than treating modal intervals as a strict improvement?

### Chapter 17: Constraints over Structured Domains (pp. 605–638)

**Summary:** Gervet surveys the extension of constraint programming beyond finite integer/real/Boolean domains to "structured" objects — strings, sets, multisets, relations, graphs, maps, lattices and ontologies — tracing the field from Laurière's ALICE and CHIP through finite set-interval solvers (Conjunto and peers) to modern global set constraints, ROBDD-based solvers, and higher-level relation/graph languages. The chapter matters because many NP-complete combinatorial problems (bin-packing, set partitioning, combinatorial design, network design) have a natural high-level formulation in terms of such structured objects, and representing them directly (rather than flattening to 0-1 variables) preserves problem structure and improves both modeling conciseness and propagation. : [[Constraints-over-Structured-Domains|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **17.1 History and Applications** — structured object (an object involving more than one element in a specific setting, e.g. a set, relation, graph); ALICE (Laurière, 1976) pioneered constrained structured objects (functions/relations with properties like injection, bijection); set constraints (Heintze–Jaffar, 1990: a distinct, program-analysis notion of $s_1 \subseteq s_2$ over sets of trees, unrelated to CSP-style set variables); finite set domain CSP (Puget and Gervet, independently, 1992–94): set variables range over set intervals and set constraints are solved via consistency techniques.
- **17.2 Constraints over Regular and Constructed Sets** — regular set (a finite set of strings generated from a finite alphabet $\Sigma$); CLP($\Sigma^*$) membership constraint $x \text{ in } e$ (e.g. $A \text{ in } (X.^{\prime\prime}ab^{\prime\prime}.Y)$ forces substring "ab"); finite automata representation of regular languages (avoids infinite enumeration; used in NASA planner); {log} / CLP(SET) (axiomatized set theory built from a `with` constructor, sound and complete but NP-hard unification, used for theorem proving); CLPS (three-sorted logic for sets/multisets/sequences of finite depth, satisfiability is NP-complete, solved via semantical-consistency rules + AC-3 + reduction to integer linear programming). : [[Constraints-over-Structured-Domains|Link]]
- **17.3 Constraints over Finite Set Intervals** — set interval $[glb, lub]$ (approximates a set domain by its greatest lower bound of definite elements and least upper bound including possible elements, under $\subseteq$); convex closure operator $\overset{\sim}{conv}(x) = [\bigcap_{a_i \in x} a_i, \bigcup_{a_i \in x} a_i]$ (ensures unions/intersections of set intervals remain intervals); graduation (a function $f$ from a set to $\mathbb{Z}$ measuring a term, e.g. cardinality or weight, used to state arithmetic constraints on sets); Bound Consistency (BC) for set constraints (lub/glb of each set variable equal the union/intersection of all values appearing in valid assignments); inference rules I1–I5 (deterministic rewrite rules pruning glb/lub and graduation bounds to enforce BC, analogous to interval-constraint relaxation). : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link1]], [[Constraints-in-Procedural-Concurrent-and-Rule-Based-Languages|Link2]]
- **17.3.6 Multiset Domains** — multiset/bag (a set allowing repeated elements); occurrence function $occ(m,s)$ (number of occurrences of $m$ in $s$; defines multiset union as max, intersection as min, difference as truncated subtraction); occurrence representation is strictly more expressive than the bound representation, and both are incomparable to the FD-list (cardinality) representation.
- **17.4 Influential Extensions to Subset Bound Solvers** — Cardinal (Azevedo): strengthens cardinality inference bidirectionally (e.g. from $|s_1|=2$ can deduce $|s_3|\le 2$ for $s_3=s_2\setminus s_1$), motivated by digital circuit diagnosis; lexicographic (lex) bounds $\langle inf, sup\rangle$ (a total order $\preceq$ on sets of naturals that extends $\subseteq$, Theorem 17.15: $s_1\subseteq s_2 \Rightarrow s_1\preceq s_2$; captures cardinality restrictions subset bounds cannot, at the cost of losing precise element-inclusion reasoning); ROBDD set domains (Lagoon–Stuckey: represent the *full* (unapproximated) set domain via a Reduced Ordered Binary Decision Diagram over the characteristic-function Boolean encoding); global set constraints atmost1/distinct/disjoint/partition (n-ary constraints on fixed-cardinality sets; enforcing BC on atmost1 and on fixed-cardinality disjoint/partition is NP-hard when decomposed into binary constraints, but polynomial global algorithms exist via an extension of Hall's theorem or via a dual reformulation using the Global Cardinality Constraint, GCC).
- **17.5 Constraints over Maps, Relations and Graphs** — relation variable (in Conjunto, represented via successor sets $s = \{y \in a \mid (x,y)\in r\}$ rather than an explicit Cartesian-product set, to avoid combinatorial blow-up); funct/inj/surj constraints on relations (defined via cardinality and disjointness of successor sets); CP(Graph) (graph domain $g=(sn,sa)$ ordered by $\subseteq$ on nodes and arcs, with kernel constraints Arcs, Nodes, ArcNode from which complex graph constraints like SubGraph and InNeighbors are derived); CP(Graph+Map) (extends to maps where the domain is a variable, not a ground set). : [[Backtracking-Search|Link1]], [[Constraints-over-Structured-Domains|Link2]]
- **17.6 Constraints over Lattices and Hierarchical Trees** — clp(L) (Fernández–Hill: a generic interval-constraint framework for any lattice-structured domain, finite or infinite); order-sorted domains and ontology domains (class-taxonomy/hierarchy-based constraint domains motivated by product/service configuration).
- **17.7–17.9 Implementation, Applications, Further Topics** — comparative time complexity of set operations across representations (sorted list, 0-1 array, hash table, ROBDD); application highlights include the Kirkman schoolgirl problem solved via set model + symmetry breaking, and the SONET network-design problem.

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the subset-bound (interval) approximation of a set domain lose exactly the information needed for cardinality-sensitive problems (e.g. combinatorial designs), and why do lexicographic bounds address this at the price of losing precise inclusion/exclusion reasoning for individual elements — motivating the later hybrid subset+lex domain?
2. What is the structural reason that decomposing the disjoint/partition constraints into binary intersection constraints preserves BC only when set cardinalities are unconstrained, but loses it (and becomes NP-hard to fix) once cardinalities are fixed — and why does the dual GCC-based reformulation restore tractability?
3. In what sense is the 0-1 (Boolean/ILP) encoding of a subset of a known set "structure-losing" compared to the set-interval/Conjunto representation, given that both are ultimately grounded in the same characteristic function $f(y_i)=1 \iff i\in s$?

### Chapter 18: Randomness and Structure (pp. 639–664)

**Summary:** Gomes and Walsh survey the use of randomly generated problems in constraint satisfaction and satisfiability research — as benchmarks, as a lens on problem hardness (phase transitions, backbones, backdoors), and as inspiration for algorithm design (randomized restarts) — while cautioning that naive random generators can be "flawed" (trivially easy) or fail to capture the structure of real-world instances, motivating structured-random hybrids like quasigroup completion and morphing. : [[Randomness-and-Phase-Transitions|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **18.1 Random Constraint Satisfaction** — Models A–D (four schemes for generating a random CSP $\langle n,m,p_1,p_2\rangle$ differing in how the constraint graph and incompatible value pairs are chosen); phase transition (a sharp shift from almost-surely-satisfiable to almost-surely-unsatisfiable as constrainedness increases, coinciding with a peak in search cost); constrainedness $\kappa = 1 - \frac{\log_2(\langle Sol\rangle)}{N}$ (Gent et al.'s unifying parameter; $\kappa<1$ under-constrained/easy-SAT, $\kappa>1$ over-constrained/easy-UNSAT, $\kappa\approx 1$ critically constrained); finite-size scaling ($prob(Sol>0) = f(\frac{\kappa-\kappa_c}{\kappa_c}N^{1/\nu})$, borrowed from statistical mechanics, describing how the transition sharpens with problem size); flawed variable/problem (a variable all of whose values are unsupported, making the problem trivially solvable by arc-consistency; occurs almost surely in naive Models A–D above a threshold tightness, motivating "flawless" generation schemes such as Model E). : [[Randomness-and-Phase-Transitions|Link]]
- **18.2 Random Satisfiability** — random $k$-SAT (fixed ratio $m/n$ of $k$-clauses to variables; sharp phase transition, e.g. $m/n\approx4.26$ for 3-SAT, proven at $2^k\log2 - O(k)$ asymptotically); backbone (fraction of variables fixed to the same value across all solutions, or across all max-satisfying assignments if unsatisfiable; discontinuous jump at the 3-SAT transition vs. smooth variation for 2-SAT, correlating with first- vs. second-order phase transitions); 2+p-SAT (interpolates between poly-time 2-SAT and NP-hard 3-SAT by mixing clause arities; empirically polynomial for $p<0.4$, hard beyond, tracking a backbone discontinuity); hidden-assignment / q-hidden generators (methods for producing only-satisfiable random SAT instances without biasing them toward being easy for local search). : [[Randomness-and-Phase-Transitions|Link]]
- **18.3 Random Problems with Structure** — Quasigroup Completion Problem (QCP) (complete a partial $n\times n$ Latin square; NP-complete; models scheduling/timetabling/wavelength assignment; phase transition governs hardness); Quasigroup with Holes (QWH) ("punches holes" into a full Latin square, guaranteeing satisfiability while still exhibiting a backbone-driven hardness transition); morphing (Gent et al.'s general technique mixing random and structured problem instances, showing that a little structure in a random problem — or vice versa — can be enough to defeat search heuristics). : [[Randomness-and-Phase-Transitions|Link]]
- **18.4 Runtime Variability** — fat-tailed / heavy-tailed distributions (kurtosis $\mu_4/\mu_2^2 > 3$ for fat-tailed; heavy-tailed defined by Pareto decay $1-F(x)\sim Cx^{-\alpha}$, with infinite variance for $1<\alpha<2$); backdoor set (Definition 18.1: given a poly-time "sub-solver" satisfying trichotomy/efficiency/trivial-solvability/self-reducibility, a nonempty variable subset $S$ such that some assignment to $S$ lets the sub-solver solve the rest; weak vs. strong backdoors; cutsets are a special case of backdoors based purely on graph topology); rapid randomization and restarts (RRR) (exploits heavy-tailed runtime distributions by repeatedly restarting a randomized backtracking procedure with an increasing cutoff, e.g. Luby's universal schedule $1,1,2,1,1,2,4,\ldots$). : [[Randomness-and-Phase-Transitions|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does constrainedness $\kappa\approx1$ correlate with peak search hardness across so many different NP-complete problem classes, and in what precise sense is this an empirical/approximate theory rather than an exact one (as the chapter notes, exact resolution-complexity results are far harder to obtain)?
2. What is the logical relationship (if any) between a problem's backbone and its backdoor set, and why does the chapter emphasize that these two concepts — despite both being "small sets of critical variables" — are not formally connected and can even be disjoint?
3. Why do naive random CSP models (A–D) risk generating trivially-flawed instances that are not "intrinsically hard" despite being combinatorially large, and why is fixing this (e.g. via Model E or flawless variants) still insufficient to guarantee hardness (per Gao and Culberson's path-consistency counterexample)?

### Chapter 19: Temporal CSPs (pp. 665–692)

**Summary:** Koubarakis surveys constraint-based formalisms for representing and reasoning about time — from Allen's qualitative interval algebra and Vilain–Kautz's point algebra through metric (difference-constraint) frameworks and their qualitative-metric hybrids — together with the efficient algorithms developed for each, and culminates in the "indefinite constraint database" scheme that extends temporal CSPs with first-order querying (via quantifier elimination) so that temporal and non-temporal information can be combined in one query language.

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **19.1 Preliminaries** — time-based approach to temporal representation (time is a set of points/intervals; contrasted with change-based approaches like situation/event calculus); definite vs. indefinite temporal information (a fact's time is fully known vs. under-specified via a qualitative/metric/mixed relationship to another time); $i$-consistency, strong $i$-consistency, global consistency/decomposability (standard CSP notions restated for temporal networks); minimal set of constraints (every pairwise instantiation consistent with the constraints on those two variables extends to a full solution — equivalent to a minimal network for binary constraints).
- **19.2.1 Qualitative Temporal Reasoning** — interval algebra IA (Allen's 13 mutually exclusive interval relations — before, meets, overlaps, during, starts, finishes, equals, and inverses — closed under intersection, complement, composition; the $2^{13}$ disjunctive relations form an algebra); point algebra PA (Vilain–Kautz: relates time points via $<,>,=$; convex point algebra CPA excludes $\ne$); Ord-Horn subclass $\mathcal H$ (Nebel–Bürckert's maximal tractable subclass of IA, 868 of the $2^{13}$ relations, expressible via Horn constraints on endpoints); dichotomy theorem (Krokhin–Jeavons–Jonsson: exactly 18 maximal tractable subclasses of IA exist; everything else is NP-complete); qualitative algebra QA (Meiri: unifies point-point, point-interval, and interval-interval qualitative relations). : [[Temporal-Constraint-Satisfaction|Link]]
- **19.2.2 Metric Temporal Reasoning** — disjunctive binary difference (DBD) constraint ($a_1\le x_i-x_j\le b_1 \vee \cdots$; NP-complete in general); binary difference (BD) constraint (single-disjunct case, solvable and minimal-network-computable in $O(n^3)$ via all-pairs shortest paths on the distance graph; path consistency = global consistency here); $n$-ary disjunctive difference (NDD) constraint (disjuncts may involve different variable pairs, strictly more expressive than DBD; NP-complete); Horn-disjunctive linear (HDL) constraints (Koubarakis / Jonsson–Bäckström: disjunctions with at most one inequality among disequations; deciding consistency is in PTIME because disequations can be handled independently). : [[Temporal-Constraint-Satisfaction|Link]]
- **19.2.3 Qualitative and Metric Combined** — binary mixed temporal constraint networks (Meiri; combines QA with DBD) and the IA+BD framework of Kautz–Ladkin; Krokhin et al.'s IA+HDL framework admits a full dichotomy theorem for tractability. : [[Temporal-Constraint-Satisfaction|Link]]
- **19.3 Efficient Algorithms** — TimeGraph I/II (Schubert–Gerevini: represent PA networks as chains with a meta-graph for cross-chain reasoning, giving near-constant-time reasoning within chains); backtracking with path-consistency preprocessing for IA (Ladkin–Reinfeld and successors); incremental algorithms for BD/DBD/NDD (avoid recomputation from scratch after a single constraint update, important for planning/scheduling); SAT-based solvers (TSAT, TSAT++, Epilitis) translate difference-constraint satisfiability into propositional SAT. : [[Global-Constraints|Link]]
- **19.4–19.6 Expressive Queries and Constraint Databases** — modal (certainty $\Box$ / possibility $\Diamond$) queries and aggregation queries (Van Beek: ask what is necessarily/possibly true of named events); indefinite constraint database (Koubarakis: a formula combining a "constraint store" CSP over Skolem constants with predicate-completion equivalences linking database predicates to those constants, representing partial/indefinite temporal knowledge alongside relational facts); quantifier elimination (Definition 19.12: a theory $Th$ admits it if every formula is provably equivalent to a disjunction of conjunctions of constraints; query evaluation over indefinite constraint databases is shown to reduce exactly to quantifier elimination in the underlying first-order temporal language, e.g. via Fourier's variable-elimination algorithm for LIN-constraints).

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does strong 4-consistency suffice to compute the minimal network for PA/PIA networks but not to achieve full global consistency (which requires strong 5-consistency, per Koubarakis) — what does this gap reveal about the difference between "minimality" and "decomposability" as consistency notions?
2. What is the essential reason path consistency is exactly equivalent to global consistency for BD constraints (via all-pairs shortest paths) but only a necessary, non-sufficient condition once the constraints become disjunctive (DBD/NDD), where enforcing it can even blow up the number of intervals exponentially?
3. In what precise sense does casting "query evaluation" as "quantifier elimination" over $Th(M_{L\cup EQ})$ unify Van Beek's modal/aggregation queries and LATER's hypothetical queries into a single computational problem — and why does this reduction require the constraint language $L$ itself to admit quantifier elimination as a prerequisite?

### Chapter 20: Distributed Constraint Programming (pp. 699–728)

**Summary:** Faltings surveys techniques for constraint satisfaction and optimization when variables, constraints, or the domain/tuple information itself are distributed among autonomous agents — motivated by problems (meeting scheduling, supply chains, sensor networks, e-commerce configuration) that are unbounded, privacy-sensitive, dynamic, or too costly to centralize — covering synchronous and asynchronous distributed search/optimization, distributed local search, open constraint programming, and issues of incentive-compatibility and privacy. : [[Distributed-Constraint-Programming|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **20.1 Definitions** — Distributed CSP (DisCSP) $\langle X,D,C,A\rangle$ (each variable is controlled by an agent that knows its domain and all its constraints); distributed constraint optimization problem (extends DisCSP by letting constraint cost functions map to $\mathbb{R}^+$, minimizing summed cost); open constraint satisfaction problem (OCSP) (a partially ordered, possibly unbounded sequence of CSP instances where only variable *domains* monotonically grow, $CSP(i)\prec CSP(j)$; a solution consistent at instance $i$ remains a solution at every later instance — Lemma 20.3); open constraint optimization problem (OCOP) (adds cost/weight functions on domain values rather than constraints, so costs need not be fully known upfront).
- **20.2 Distributed Search** — synchronous backtracking (partial assignments passed agent-to-agent; supports forward checking, dynamic variable ordering, backjumping, but exploits no parallelism); Asynchronous Backtracking (ABT) (Yokoo et al.: agents run in parallel with a fixed priority order; each agent maintains an agentview of higher-priority values and a nogoods store; adjust-value picks the least-cost value and, if none is consistent, sends a nogood — a resolvent of constraints proving the current agentview allows no solution — to the lowest-priority agent in that view); ABT-opt (generalizes ABT to optimization by having cost functions $r$ return real values instead of 0/1, and propagating lower-bound nogoods with an exact/inexact flag for termination detection); DFS tree ordering (ordering agents along a depth-first spanning tree of the constraint graph so that nogoods never overlap on the same variable via two different chains, guaranteeing exact lower-bound propagation — the basis of the ADOPT algorithm). : [[Distributed-Constraint-Programming|Link]]
- **20.3 Improvements and Variants** — asynchronous aggregation search (AAS) (Silaghi et al.: agents control constraints rather than variables, working on a dual problem, aggregating equivalent value combinations into single messages); asynchronous weak-commitment search (AWC) (dynamically re-prioritizes agents after each backtrack to focus search on the hardest subproblem, at the cost of exponential nogood storage for completeness); DPOP (distributed dynamic-programming algorithm based on bucket elimination along a DFS/pseudotree: children send UTIL messages upward summarizing optimal cost per parent-value combination, parents send VALUE messages downward; message count grows linearly with problem size but message size is exponential in induced width); Optimal Asynchronous Partial Overlay (OptAPO) (dynamically centralizes especially hard subproblems via mediating agents).
- **20.4 Distributed Local Search** — distributed hill-climbing with coordination (Algorithm 20.2: neighbors exchange values then proposed improvements; an agent only changes value if no neighbor proposes a bigger improvement, preventing simultaneous conflicting moves; termination counters $tc1$/$tc2$ track propagation distance); breakout algorithm (increases constraint weights on violated constraints to escape local minima; provably incomplete — Fig. 20.7 shows a graph-coloring instance on which it cycles forever). : [[Distributed-Constraint-Programming|Link]]
- **20.5 Open Constraint Programming** — feasibility of open CSP follows because adding domain values never invalidates an existing consistent solution (Lemma 20.3); open constraint optimization is feasible only if queries return best (most preferred / lowest cost) values first, else optimality cannot be certified without exhausting domains; the fo-opt algorithm (an $A^*$-style best-first search over partial domain knowledge that queries new values only for variables in the first violated constraint, minimizing the number of costly domain queries). : [[Distributed-Constraint-Programming|Link]]
- **20.6 Further Issues** — incentive-compatibility / Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) tax mechanism (each agent pays the marginal cost its constraints impose on the rest of the system, making truthful constraint reporting individually rational — the unique general mechanism achieving both incentive-compatibility and individual rationality); secure/cryptographic DisCSP (Yokoo: encrypted constraint matrices plus random value permutation and full-search-space exploration ensure no agent learns anything beyond the final consistent assignment). : [[Distributed-Constraint-Programming|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does ordering agents along a DFS tree (rather than an arbitrary priority order) guarantee that nogoods never double-count the same lower-priority variable's cost, and why is this property essential for ABT-opt/ADOPT to compute *exact* rather than merely lower-bound solutions?
2. In open constraint optimization, why is the monotonicity assumption (queries return best values first) not just a convenience but a strict *necessity* for provable optimality — what breaks in Faltings's example (Figure 20.8) if this assumption is dropped?
3. The VCG tax argument shows truth-telling is individually rational for soft constraints — why does the same argument explicitly fail to extend to hard constraints, and what does this imply about combining incentive-compatible mechanisms with real-world DisCSP applications that mix hard and soft constraints?

### Chapter 21: Uncertainty and Change (pp. 731–755)

**Summary:** Brown and Miguel survey extensions of constraint programming to problems that violate CP's two core assumptions — crisp, complete problem descriptions and static problems — organizing the field into uncertain problems needing a single solution (fuzzy CSPs, mixed/uncertain/probabilistic/stochastic CSPs) and dynamically changing problems needing multiple solution stages (pure reaction via local repair, prepared reaction via recorded oracles/explanations, and proactive prediction via supersolutions, stochastic/branching CSP, and controllability of temporal problems). : [[Uncertainty-and-Change-in-Constraint-Problems|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **21.1–21.2 Background** — finite-domain CSP $\langle X,D,C\rangle$ restated as baseline; running example, the Course Scheduling Problem (9 variables $x_{ij}$ for day/session-type counts, used throughout to illustrate each formalism).
- **21.3.1 Fuzzy Problems** — fuzzy CSP (a constraint is a fuzzy relation assigning each tuple a satisfaction degree in $[0,1]$; conjunction of constraints = minimum of degrees; hard constraints are the special case of degrees restricted to $\{0,1\}$); this captures graded preferences like "about three sessions" rather than crisp cutoffs.
- **21.3.2 Problems with Possible Realisations** — Mixed CSP (Fargier et al.: variables split into controlled decision variables and uncontrollable parameters set externally; a pure decision solves all realisations at once — NP-complete to decide existence even for independent parameters; a conditional decision maps each realisation to its own assignment); Uncertain CSP (Yorke-Smith–Gervet: uncertainty in constraint coefficients themselves; seeks the certainty closure — all solutions across possible realisations — or a covering set / most robust solution within it). : [[Uncertainty-and-Change-in-Constraint-Problems|Link]]
- **21.3.3 Probabilistic and Stochastic CSPs** — probabilistic CSP (two variants: probability distributions over constraint-coefficient realisations, solved via soft-constraint frameworks like valued/semiring CSP; or distributions over uncontrollable-parameter values, seeking the pure decision of maximal solving probability); 1-stage stochastic CSP ($\theta$-satisfiable if some pure decision solves the problem with probability $>\theta$; shown $NP^{PP}$-complete); multi-stage stochastic CSP (Walsh: alternating phases of decision-variable assignment and parameter revelation; solution is a decision tree; general case is PSPACE-complete); Branching CSP (models unbounded, uncertain arrival of new variables/constraints via a probabilistic arrival tree; solution is a policy maximizing expected utility, related to but generally intractable as an MDP). : [[Uncertainty-and-Change-in-Constraint-Problems|Link]]
- **21.4.1 Pure Reaction** — Local Repair / min-conflicts (Minton et al.: backtracking or hill-climbing guided by minimizing new constraint violations relative to the previous solution); Local Changes (Verfaillie–Schiex-style algorithm partitioning variables into fixed ($X_1$), tentatively-assigned ($X_2$), and unassigned ($X_3$) sets, recursively repairing only the variables implicated in violated constraints); minimal perturbation (explicit stability objective measuring distance from the previous solution, rather than relying on repair-search side effects to produce stability). : [[Uncertainty-and-Change-in-Constraint-Problems|Link]]
- **21.4.2 Prepared Reaction** — oracles (Van Hentenryck–Provost: record the search path to a prior solution and reuse it to prune the search tree for a related, differently-constrained problem, provided the oracle problem is less constrained than the current one); explanations (Jussien: subsets of constraints justifying solver events such as value removals; support constraint retraction — a case standard arc-consistency algorithms handle poorly — by tentatively reinstating values whose justification was retracted, as in DnAC-4/DnAC-6/PaLM). : [[Uncertainty-and-Change-in-Constraint-Problems|Link]]
- **21.4.3 Predicting Changes** — supersolutions ($(a,b)$-supersolution: a solution that, if any $a$ value assignments are lost, can be repaired by reassigning those plus at most $b$ other variables; NP-complete to find for fixed $a$; extended to weighted $(\alpha,\beta)$-supersolutions incorporating loss probability and repair cost); Simple Temporal Problems with uncertainty — strong/weak/dynamic controllability (Vidal–Fargier: a schedule is strongly controllable if one fixed decision works regardless of uncontrollable timepoints, weakly controllable if some decision exists per realisation, dynamically controllable if an online policy exists depending only on observed timepoints; strong and dynamic controllability are in P, weak controllability is in co-NP). : [[Uncertainty-and-Change-in-Constraint-Problems|Link]]
- **21.5 Pseudo-dynamic Formalisms** — conditional CSP (Mittal–Falkenhainer: the whole problem is known statically, but subparts are activated/deactivated by other variables' assignments — distinct from genuinely dynamic CSPs despite historical naming); Open CSP (OCSP, Faltings–Macho-Gonzalez: variables/constraints are fixed but domains/tuples are discovered incrementally by querying information sources; o-search / fo-search algorithms interleave querying and solving to minimize expensive queries). : [[Uncertainty-and-Change-in-Constraint-Problems|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. What is the precise structural distinction between a *dynamic* CSP (a genuine sequence of changing problems) and the several "pseudo-dynamic" formalisms — conditional CSP, Open CSP — that are often confused with it by name, and why does the chapter insist on keeping them separate?
2. Why does finding an $(a,b)$-supersolution become NP-complete even for a fixed small $a$ (e.g. $a=1$), and what does the shift from "any (1,0)-supersolution" to "the most robust solution when none exists" reveal about the tradeoff between guaranteed robustness and best-effort robustness?
3. Local repair (min-conflicts, Local Changes) achieves stability only as a side effect of minimizing search effort, whereas minimal-perturbation and supersolution approaches make stability/robustness an explicit objective — what are the practical circumstances under which the chapter suggests one approach dominates the other, and does either provide optimality guarantees the other lacks?

### Chapter 22: Constraint-Based Scheduling and Planning (pp. 761–800)

**Summary:** This chapter surveys how Constraint Programming (CP) models and solves scheduling problems (allocating scarce resources to activities over time) and, more briefly, planning problems (where the set of activities itself is not fixed in advance), arguing that CP's success in this area comes from combining Operations Research's efficient algorithms (embedded in global constraints) with Artificial Intelligence's flexible, declarative modeling paradigm. : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **22.1 CP Models for Scheduling** — activity variables $start(A_i), end(A_i), proc(A_i)$ with $proc(A_i)=end(A_i)-start(A_i)$; non-preemptive vs. preemptive (set-variable $set(A_i)$ or 0-1 variables $X(A_i,t)$) vs. elastic scheduling (energy $E(A_i,R)$); resource constraint $\sum_i E(A_i,t,R) \le cap(R)$; disjunctive (unary) vs. cumulative resources; temporal constraints of the form $x-y\le d$; extensions: alternative resources (constructive disjunction over fictive activities $A_i^u$), setup times/costs $setup(A_i,A_j,R_u)$, breakable activities and calendars (productivity profile), optional/unperformed activities, state resources, reservoirs; objective functions — makespan $C_{max}$, total weighted flow time $\sum w_iC_i$, tardiness $T_i=\max(0,C_i-\delta_i)$, weighted late jobs $\sum w_iU_i$; dichotomizing search for optimal $criterion$. : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]
- **22.2 CP Models for Planning** — STRIPS representation (add-list/delete-list, mutex actions/propositions); Graphplan's planning graph compiled into a CSP (variables = propositions per step, values = establishing actions plus dummy $\bot$); plan-space search (e.g. IxTeT formalism) representing a partial plan as tasks (events, hold-assertions, resource use) refined via non-established conditions, possible conflicts, and possible resource conflicts until complete and valid. : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]
- **22.3 Constraint Propagation for Resource Constraints** — disjunctive constraint propagation (arc-B-consistency on $[end(A_i)\le start(A_j)]\vee[end(A_j)\le start(A_i)]$); edge-finding (deducing that an activity must/can/cannot execute first or last in a set $\Omega$, using $r_\Omega, d_\Omega, p_\Omega$); not-first/not-last rules; timetable constraint (maintaining $\sum cap(A_i)\le cap(R)$ per time point); energy reasoning / Left-Shift-Right-Shift required energy $W_{Sh}(A_i,t_1,t_2)$; precedence-graph-based conjunctive reasoning — energy precedence constraint and the balance constraint for reservoirs (upper bound $L^<_{max}(x)$ on reservoir level, used to find dead ends, new capacity bounds, new time bounds, new precedence relations). : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]
- **22.4 Constraint Propagation on Optimization Criteria** — weighted number of late activities $\sum w_iU_i$ via a relaxed preemptive lower bound (MIP/LP relaxation using sets $S(t_1,t_2)$, $P(t_1,t_2)$); sum of setup times/costs via a routing-problem relaxation with route optimization constraint (Assignment Problem relaxation, reduced costs $\bar c_{ij}$) and a precedence graph constraint (five closure rules over "surely/possibly contribute" activities). : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]
- **22.5 Heuristic Search** — branching by ordering activities (rather than instantiating times) for both unary and cumulative resources; regular criteria (monotonic in end times, admit dominance pruning) vs. sequence-dependent criteria (e.g. setup costs) vs. irregular criteria (e.g. work-in-process time); hybridization with Local Search (Caseau–Laburthe repair/shuffle moves, Le Pape–Baptiste's edge-finding + Jackson derivation + limited discrepancy search) and with Mixed Integer Programming (cooperative optimization algorithms combining CP-derived initial solutions with CPLEX RINS/guided-dives). : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]
- **22.6 Conclusions** — identifies remaining challenges: good default/out-of-the-box search, scheduling/planning under uncertainty (reactive rescheduling vs. proactive robust scheduling), modeling of industrial side constraints, and generic lower-bounding techniques for arbitrary optimization criteria.

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does incorporating OR algorithms *inside global constraints* (rather than replacing CP's search-and-propagation architecture with an OR algorithm) preserve CP's modeling flexibility while gaining OR's efficiency — and where does this trade-off break down (e.g., for irregular criteria like work-in-process time)?
2. What is the essential difference between the edge-finding rules and the "not-first/not-last" rules, and why do both remain necessary even though they seem to derive similar kinds of orderings?
3. In what sense does the balance constraint for reservoirs perform strictly more powerful propagation than a naive timetable-style approach, and why can it not be directly transplanted to planning problems without extension?

### Chapter 23: Vehicle Routing (pp. 801–836)

**Summary:** This chapter surveys the Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) — constructing minimum-cost delivery routes for a fleet of vehicles — reviewing both classical Operations Research heuristics and Constraint Programming formulations, and showing how CP's expressiveness makes it especially valuable for handling the enormous variety of real-world side constraints that arise in industrial routing software. : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **23.1 The Vehicle Routing Problem** — CVRP (customers with demand $r_i$, vehicle capacity $Q$, minimizing $\sum c_{ij}$); extensions: heterogeneous fleet, multiple resources, VRP with Time Windows (VRPTW, time window $[a_i,b_i]$, NP-hard even for feasibility), open VRP, multi-depot VRP; side constraints (vehicle/customer incompatibility, driver preferences, precedence, split deliveries); related problems — TSP, TSPTW, Pickup-and-Delivery Problems (PDP), Dial-a-Ride, VRP with backhauls. : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]
- **23.2 Operations Research Approaches** — 3-index ILP formulation (variables $x_{ijk}$, subtour-elimination constraints of exponential size); construction heuristics (sequential vs. parallel insertion, seed customers, Clarke–Wright "savings" method $S_{ij}=c_{i0}+c_{0j}-c_{ij}$, sweep heuristic); local search operators — 2-opt, 3-opt, Or-opt, relocate, exchange, cross, $\lambda$-exchange, GENI exchange, ejection chains; meta-heuristics — simulated annealing, tabu search, genetic algorithms, variable neighborhood search; exact methods — column generation, Lagrangian relaxation, branch-and-cut. : [[Integration-of-Constraint-Programming-and-Operations-Research|Link]]
- **23.3 Constraint Programming Approaches** — path-constraint model with predecessor/successor variables $p_i, s_i$ and coherence (element) constraints; vehicle variable $v_i$; quantity-of-goods path constraint (bounds consistency on $q_i$); time path constraint with time windows; NoCycle constraint (chains tracked via $b_i, e_i$, $O(1)$ propagation per bound); connectivity pruning (reachable sets $R_k^F, R_k^B$); alternative formulation with before/after set variables $B_i, A_i$; lower bounds and cost-based propagation (regret-based bound $d_{LBR}$, Hungarian-algorithm reduced-cost propagation $d_H+\bar c_{i,j}>G \Rightarrow s_i\ne j$). : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]
- **23.4 Constraint Programming in Search** — the chronological-backtracking obstacle to combining CP with local search; CP as a rule-checker (De Backer et al.); Local Search within a CP framework via serial-insertion/block-deletion operators, notably Large Neighbourhood Search (LNS) and Limited Discrepancy Search (LDS); Pesant–Gendreau's branch-and-bound exploration of large neighbourhoods (e.g., orientation-preserving 3-opt via indices $I,J,K$). : [[Backtracking-Search|Link1]], [[Constraint-Logic-Programming|Link2]], [[Distributed-Constraint-Programming|Link3]], [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link4]]
- **23.5 CP as a Subproblem Solver** — set covering/partitioning with column generation (routes as columns, prize-collecting TSP subproblem); Lagrangian relaxation (dualizing the "visit each customer once" constraints, decomposing into per-vehicle prize-collecting TSPs). : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]
- **23.6 CP-VRP in the Real World** — catalog of idiosyncratic real-world constraints (contracted driver minimum time, meal breaks, subcontracting, linear-route constraints, cross-docking, trailer change-over, resource-limited docks, complex overtime rates, truck inventory tracking); dynamic operation (visits added/removed in real time, traffic incidents, breakdowns); commercial routing software ecosystem. : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why is standard chronological backtracking fundamentally incompatible with local-search-style repair moves in a CP-based VRP solver, and what are the two general strategies (rule-checking vs. operator-insulated search) developed to reconcile them?
2. How does redundant modeling — maintaining both predecessor variables $p_i$ and successor variables $s_i$ for the same routes — yield propagation that neither variable set alone would achieve?
3. The chapter argues CP's chief advantage over classical OR is the ability to add arbitrary side constraints "at very low cost" to a common core model — what does the core path/time/quantity model actually buy you, and what class of real-world constraints (e.g., cross-docking, trailer change-over) still strains this framework?

### Chapter 24: Configuration (pp. 837–874)

**Summary:** This chapter presents configuration — composing a customized system from generic catalog components under compatibility, requirement, and resource constraints — as a distinctive CP application domain whose dynamic, taxonomy-driven nature (unknown numbers of parts, inherited attributes, evolving constraint networks) required extending classical CSP with conditional and generative constraint models, plus new tasks like interactive consistency maintenance, conflict explanation, and preference-based search. : [[Applications-Configuration-Networks-and-Bioinformatics|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **24.1 What Is Configuration?** — configuration task (catalog + user requirements/preferences $\to$ configuration or failure explanation); primitive vs. composite components; functional vs. technical characteristics; historical approaches — rule-based (R1/XCON, suffering from poor modularity), model-based (description logic, resource-based reasoning, CSP-based, SAT, answer-set programming, knowledge compilation via BDDs/automata); modeling challenges (product catalogs, dynamic type-as-variable knowledge representation, universally quantified resource constraints on open domains, preference models); problem-solving challenges (top-down refinement, controlled component generation, user scalability, explanations, preference elicitation). : [[Applications-Configuration-Networks-and-Bioinformatics|Link]]
- **24.2 Configuration Knowledge** — catalog knowledge (technical types $T$, concrete/leaf types $L$, attributes $A$, catalog constraint linking type to attribute domains); structural knowledge — partonomies (part-of hierarchy from bill-of-materials) and taxonomies (functional/technical/concrete type layers), ports (partonomic vs. connection), part/port/property views ($parts^*, ports^*, props$); configuration constraints — compatibility constraints ($\exists (v_1,\dots,v_n)\in R$), requirement constraints ($\forall (v_1,v_2)\in R: a_1(x)=v_1\Rightarrow a_2(x)=v_2$), resource constraints (balancing produced $\ge$ consumed amounts, generalizable to min/max/average/set-union aggregation). : [[Applications-Configuration-Networks-and-Bioinformatics|Link]]
- **24.3 Constraint Models for Configuration** — functional/configuration space and functional completeness; Boolean models for option selection (variables $f_i, t_j$; implications, disjunctions, negative clauses); cardinality models for shopping lists (integer count variables $\#f_i,\#t_j$; linear resource constraints); flat CSP models for customizing fixed sets of components; Conditional CSPs for taxonomic reasoning (optional/activated variables, $active:a_j(o_i)$, universally quantified constraints over $instances(t)$); Conditional CSPs for simple partonomic reasoning (connection-ports as domain-restricted variables); Generative CSPs for complex (unbounded) partonomic reasoning (generative constraint $\#p(o)\ge i \Rightarrow p(o,i)\in instances(t)$, recursive set/resource aggregation over multi-valued ports). : [[Applications-Scheduling-Planning-and-Vehicle-Routing|Link1]], [[Randomness-and-Phase-Transitions|Link2]], [[Constraint-Logic-Programming|Link3]], [[Constraints-over-Structured-Domains|Link4]]
- **24.4 Problem Solving for Configuration** — interactive configuration via maintaining global consistency of functional-attribute domains (contrasted with cheaper arc consistency, which coincides with global consistency only when the constraint network is acyclic); explanation and relaxation tasks (minimal conflict / maximal relaxation, solved by the QuickXplain divide-and-conquer algorithm); solution search respecting generation order and top-down refinement, avoiding under-charged components and over-fulfilled functions via an ordered decision protocol (functional attributes $\to$ type specialization $\to$ technical attributes $\to$ port cardinalities $\to$ connections); satisfying user preferences via preference programming and diversified Pareto-optimal solution sets. : [[Applications-Configuration-Networks-and-Bioinformatics|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why do classical (static) CSP techniques fail to capture configuration knowledge, and in what precise sense do Conditional CSPs and Generative CSPs each solve a different piece of this problem (bounded optional structure vs. genuinely unbounded part generation)?
2. The chapter insists that configurators must sometimes "make wrong decisions in order to discover that they are wrong" (e.g., specializing to an incompatible type to inherit its constraints) — how does this contradict, and how does it get reconciled with, the classical CP principle of eliminating inconsistent choices a priori via propagation?
3. How does the QuickXplain algorithm achieve a minimal conflict via recursive divide-and-conquer without testing every subset, and why do naive truth-maintenance-based explanations tend to be far from minimal (especially with resource constraints)?

### Chapter 25: Constraint Applications in Networks (pp. 875–904)

**Summary:** This chapter surveys real-world network applications of CP across electrical power distribution, water/oil pipeline networks, and — in greatest depth — data (telecommunication/IP) networks, covering application placement, path placement, multi-path protection, bandwidth-on-demand scheduling, network design, IGP metric optimization, and resilience analysis, concluding that hybrid CP/OR methods (Lagrangian relaxation, column generation, Benders decomposition, local search) generally outperform pure finite-domain CP on these large, cost-driven, combinatorial problems. : [[Applications-Configuration-Networks-and-Bioinformatics|Link1]], [[Backtracking-Search|Link2]], [[Constraint-Propagation-and-Local-Consistency|Link3]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **25.1 Electricity Networks** — Ohm's Law ($V=I\cdot\Omega$), Kirchhoff's Current and Voltage Laws; power distribution network configuration as maintaining a cycle-free forest via switches while respecting station/line capacity and minimizing resistive loss; the PlaNets system (rational + finite-domain CHIP solvers) and later BDD-based reconfiguration.
- **25.2 Water (Oil) Networks** — flow conservation via Kirchhoff's current law generalized to networks with storage (reservoirs/water towers), variable flow rates, leaky pipes (broken flow conservation), and non-linear water/crude mixing; the CLOCWISe and FORWARD C systems.
- **25.3 Data Networks** — application placement as a capacitated warehouse-location-style ILP (variables $A_{jk}, U_{ij}^k$); path placement in three model families — link-based (variables $X_{de}$, path constraint via flow conservation, capacity constraint $\sum bw(d)X_{de}\le cap(e)$), path-based (variables $Y_i^d$, column generation, blocking islands for feasibility-only CSPs), node-based (successor variables $S_k^d$, cycle and cumulative global constraints); demand acceptance vs. traffic placement objectives; hybrid solvers (Lagrangian relaxation/CP via path or knapsack decomposition with cost-based filtering, Probe Backtracking Search, local search/CP hybrids); multiple paths with link/node/SRLG-disjoint primary-secondary protection; Bandwidth on Demand (time-indexed capacity constraint akin to `cumulative`); Network Design and capacity planning (design-alternative variables $W_{ie}$, ROCOCO benchmarks, ILP/CP/column-generation comparisons); IGP Metric Optimization (edge-weight variables $W_e$, unique-shortest-path constraints, branch-and-price, tabu search with $2^{-i}$ weight tricks, set-constraint solvers); Flow/Resilience Analysis (under-constrained demand-matrix reconstruction vs. directly bounding link traffic under failure scenarios); Bandwidth Protection (local per-node detours via nested min-cost-flow/capacity optimization, Benders-style decomposition). : [[Applications-Configuration-Networks-and-Bioinformatics|Link]]
- **25.4 Conclusion** — classical finite-domain CP alone is "rather limited" for large cost-optimization network problems; LP/Lagrangian relaxation reasons about cost bounds much better than finite-domain propagation; open question of whether new global constraints (e.g., `tree`, path constraints) can hide this algorithmic complexity behind declarative abstractions.

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the chapter conclude that classical finite-domain CP is comparatively weak for large-scale network cost-optimization, while hybrids with Lagrangian relaxation, column generation, and local search perform much better — what specifically does LP/Lagrangian reasoning provide that finite-domain propagation does not?
2. In the link-based path-placement model, what is the essential structural difference between the capacity constraint for a *primary* path (Eq. 25.21) and the capacity constraint required for a *secondary* (failure-protection) path (Eq. 25.23), and why does the latter blow up the constraint count so dramatically?
3. How does the "blocking island" technique let the path-based VRP-style model avoid ever materializing each demand's full domain of possible paths, and what does this reveal about implied/forward-checking constraint models more generally?

### Chapter 26: Bioinformatics and Constraints (pp. 905–944)

**Summary:** This chapter introduces bioinformatics to computer scientists and surveys where constraint techniques have been (or could be) applied across the "central dogma" — sequence problems (physical mapping, sequence/structure alignment, pattern discovery, phylogenetics), structure problems (protein structure prediction on lattice models, docking, structural motif matching/discovery), and function problems (metabolic/regulatory pathway analysis, microarray-driven network inference). : [[Continuous-and-Interval-Constraints|Link1]], [[Global-Constraints|Link2]], [[Soft-Constraints-and-Preferences|Link3]], [[Integration-of-Constraint-Programming-and-Operations-Research|Link4]]

- **26.4 Sequence Related Problems** — physical mapping (probe/clone hybridization matrix $A=(a_{ij})$, consecutive-ones property, NP-complete under error, ILP with betweenness constraints from end-probes); pairwise sequence alignment (Definition of alignment and alignment distance $D(a,b)$, dynamic-programming recursion for $D_{i,j}$, parametric alignment as a constraint problem); multiple sequence alignment (sum-of-pairs score, Kececioglu's Complete Maximum-Weight Trace formalization over an alignment graph, trace = realizable non-crossing edge set, ILP/ pseudo-Boolean ($x_k+x_l\le 1$ for conflicting edges) formulations, extended alignment graph with the strict partial order $\prec^*$ characterizing valid multi-sequence traces); protein threading (core model $(m,\vec c,\vec\lambda,\vec l^{min})$ with core regions $C^i$, threading as a mapping preserving loop-length bounds); pattern discovery (Eidhammer et al.'s constraint-based structure description language distinguishing sequential vs. structural patterns with correlation constraints; systems MilPat, SMaRTFinder, Staden's language, GENLANG); phylogenetic trees (distance-matrix reconstruction e.g. UPGMA, vs. parsimony-based tree construction minimizing total edge cost).
