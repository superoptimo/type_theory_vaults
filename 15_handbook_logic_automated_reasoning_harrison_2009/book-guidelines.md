# Handbook of Practical Logic and Automated Reasoning — Guidelines

## Header

**Title:** Handbook of Practical Logic and Automated Reasoning
**Author(s):** John Harrison
**Publication:** Cambridge University Press, 2009

**Brief Summary:**
This book is a self-contained, broad-based introduction to mathematical logic and automated theorem proving, unusual for developing the theory and the implementation in lockstep: every concept, from propositional satisfiability through first-order resolution, equational rewriting, decidable theories, interactive LCF-style proof, and the classical limitative theorems, is accompanied by working OCaml code the reader can run and extend. Its organizing idea is that formalizing reasoning on a computer forces a level of precision that benefits the reader even where automation is not the end goal, and that pure logic and automated deduction illuminate each other when taught together.

**Intent of the Author:**
Harrison wrote the book as a "one-stop" introduction to automated reasoning for readers with no prior logic background, aiming to present traditional mathematical-logic results (compactness, Löwenheim–Skolem, completeness, Gödel's theorems) in a concrete, algorithmic style backed by real, experimentable implementations, rather than through purely abstract set-theoretic exposition.

## Chapter Summaries

### Chapter 1: Introduction (pp. 1–24)

**Summary:** This chapter motivates the mechanization of logical reasoning historically (from Leibniz's *calculemus* dream through Boole's algebra of logic) and then pivots to practical foundations — the syntax/semantics distinction and object-language/metalanguage jargon — before giving a hands-on OCaml walkthrough of representing, simplifying, parsing, and prettyprinting symbolic expressions, setting up the programming style used throughout the book. : [[Automated-First-Order-Theorem-Proving|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **1.1 What is logical reasoning?** — logically valid argument (one whose validity depends only on its general form, not the content of the terms), contraposition (from "if $P$ then $Q$" it follows that "if not $Q$ then not $P$", distinct from the invalid converse "if $Q$ then $P$"), Euclid's axiomatic style (asserting a small number of axioms and deducing consequences by purely logical reasoning) : [[Foundations-of-Symbolic-Logic-in-OCaml|Link1]], [[Equality-Reasoning|Link2]]
- **1.2 Calculemus!** — characteristica universalis (Leibniz's proposed universal language in which anything can be expressed), calculus ratiocinator (Leibniz's proposed calculus of reasoning for deciding truth of assertions in the characteristica)
- **1.4 Boole's algebra of logic** — proposition (an assertion making a declaration of fact, meaningfully either true or false), logical connectives (operations combining truth-values: unary "not"/negation, binary "and"/conjunction, "or"/disjunction), Boole's notation ($0$ = false, $1$ = true, $pq$ = "$p$ and $q$", $p+q$ = "$p$ or $q$"), idempotence law $p^2 = p$ (a Boolean law with no counterpart in ordinary arithmetic), inclusive vs. exclusive "or" (the book adopts the inclusive reading throughout, following Jevons), logical form (two arguments have the same form if both are instances of the same formal expression under consistent variable substitution) : [[Foundations-of-Symbolic-Logic-in-OCaml|Link]]
- **1.5 Syntax and semantics** — syntax (rules governing the grammatical formation of expressions, considered apart from meaning), semantics (the systematic assignment of meanings to grammatical expressions), interpretation (the map from expressions to their meanings), metalanguage / object language (a metalanguage is used to talk about a distinct object language; correspondingly metalogic reasons about an object logic), metatheorem (a theorem about a formal logical system, as opposed to a theorem derived inside it), metamathematics (metalogical reasoning applied to formalized mathematical proofs), abstract syntax (the tree-structured representation of an expression reflecting its grammatical structure), concrete syntax (the linear, human-written representation of an expression), abstract syntax tree/AST (a tree representation used as the internal form of a language), parsing (translating concrete syntax to abstract syntax) and prettyprinting (translating abstract syntax back to concrete syntax) : [[First-Order-Logic-Syntax-and-Semantics|Link1]], [[Foundations-of-Symbolic-Logic-in-OCaml|Link2]]
- **1.6 Symbolic computation and OCaml** — symbolic computation (manipulation of symbolic/structured data rather than pure numeric calculation, exemplified by computer algebra systems), OCaml `expression` datatype (`Var of string | Const of int | Add of expression * expression | Mul of expression * expression` representing algebraic AST nodes), term rewriting/simplification via pattern matching (`simplify1` applies one rewrite step such as `Add(Const 0, x) -> x`; `simplify` recursively applies it bottom-up through the tree) : [[Foundations-of-Symbolic-Logic-in-OCaml|Link]]
- **1.7 Parsing** — lexical analysis/scanning (decomposing an input character stream into tokens), token (a maximal run of alphanumeric or symbolic characters, or a single punctuation character), recursive descent parsing (a parsing technique using mutually recursive functions, one per grammar category, mirroring the BNF grammar), BNF/Backus–Naur form (a notation for describing grammars, here used for `expression`, `product`, `atom`), left- vs. right-recursive grammar (determines left- vs. right-associativity of the parsed operators)
- **1.8 Prettyprinting** — precedence-aware printing (an extra "outer precedence" argument to `string_of_exp` determines whether parentheses are needed around a subexpression, based on comparing the subexpression's operator precedence to the surrounding context)

**Key Questions:**
1. In what precise sense does an argument's "logical form" make it valid independent of content, and why does making a hidden assumption explicit (as with "all Greeks are mortal") convert an invalid argument into a valid one?
2. Why does Harrison insist on the sharp separation between syntax (expressions) and semantics (meanings), and what practical problems in symbolic computation does collapsing that distinction (as ordinary mathematical practice often does) create?
3. Why is right-recursive descent parsing natural for producing right-associative parse trees, and what specific difficulty would a naively left-recursive grammar cause for a recursive-descent implementation?

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the material/truth-functional reading of implication ($p \Rightarrow q$ is false only when $p$ is true and $q$ is false) turn out to be the only consistent choice once any truth-functional semantics is adopted, despite conflicting so sharply with everyday causal readings of "if... then"?
2. DNF-via-truth-tables and DNF-via-distribution both risk exponential blowup, and NNF itself can double in size at each equivalence — what property of definitional CNF (Tseitin's construction) lets it sidestep this blowup, and what is given up (equisatisfiability instead of logical equivalence) to achieve it?
3. DP, DPLL, and Stålmarck's method all decide propositional satisfiability, yet rest on different core mechanisms (resolution, splitting-with-unit-propagation, and the dilemma rule with saturation levels respectively) — how does each exploit "locally simple" reasoning to avoid exhaustively enumerating valuations, and why might one be preferable to another on a given class of formulas (e.g. adder circuits vs. random 3-CNF)?

### Chapter 4: Equality (pp. 235–307)

**Summary:** This chapter treats equality not as an arbitrary interpreted predicate but as a relation that should mean genuine identity, developing both general-purpose techniques (equality axioms, Brand transformations) and specialized decision procedures and calculi (congruence closure, rewriting, Knuth–Bendix completion, paramodulation) for reasoning efficiently about equational facts in first-order logic and automated theorem proving. : [[Equality-Reasoning|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **4.1 Equality axioms** — normal interpretation (equality predicate interpreted as true identity on the domain); $\mathrm{eqaxioms}(\Delta)$ (equivalence + congruence formulas for all functions/predicates in $\Delta$); Theorem 4.1 ($\Delta$ has a normal model iff $\Delta \cup \mathrm{eqaxioms}(\Delta)$ has a model); `equalitize` (OCaml preprocessing step reducing validity with equality to ordinary first-order validity of $\mathrm{eqaxiom}(p) \Rightarrow p$) : [[Equality-Reasoning|Link]]
- **4.2 Categoricity and elementary equivalence** — isomorphism between interpretations; categorical / $\kappa$-categorical set of formulas; elementary equivalence; equalitarian versions of compactness, downward and upward Löwenheim–Skolem theorems; nonstandard models (e.g., via compactness argument adding an "infinite" constant) : [[Equality-Reasoning|Link]]
- **4.3 Equational logic and completeness theorems** — Birkhoff's proof rules (AXIOM, INST, REFL, SYM, TRANS, CONG) for provability $\Delta \vdash s = t$; Birkhoff's theorem (soundness and completeness of these rules for equational consequence, Theorem 4.7); soundness/completeness vs. refutation completeness (as with resolution); the intrinsic difficulty of finding equational proofs despite the "tame" theory : [[Equality-Reasoning|Link]]
- **4.4 Congruence closure** — congruence (equivalence relation closed under function congruence); congruence closure of a relation; Theorem 4.8 (equivalence of semantic entailment, congruence, restricted Birkhoff proof, and provability for ground equations); `emerge`/union-find implementation with predecessor function `pfn`; decision procedure `ccvalid`/`ccsatisfiable` for universal formulas with only equality; Ackermann reduction (eliminating functions via new variables, further reducible to propositional tautology checking) : [[Equality-Reasoning|Link]]
- **4.5 Rewriting** — rewriting (directional use of an equation to replace a subterm); canonical/convergent rewrite system (decides $E \models s=t$ by reducing to normal forms and comparing); abstract reduction relation, termination (strongly normalizing/noetherian), normal form; diamond property, confluence, weak confluence, joinability ($x \downarrow y$); Newman's lemma (Theorem 4.9: terminating + weakly confluent $\Rightarrow$ confluent); Church–Rosser property (equivalent to confluence, Theorem 4.10); canonical reduction relation (terminating and confluent); narrowing (contrasted with rewriting, allows instantiating the term being rewritten) : [[Algebraic-Decision-Procedures|Link1]], [[Equality-Reasoning|Link2]]
- **4.6 Termination orderings** — rewrite order (transitive, irreflexive, closed under instantiation and congruence); reduction order (terminating rewrite order); measure-based orders and their limitations for associative/distributive laws; lexicographic path order (LPO), parametrized by a function-symbol precedence/weighting; simplification order (has the subterm property, automatically terminating by Dershowitz's theorem) : [[Equality-Reasoning|Link]]
- **4.7 Knuth–Bendix completion** — local confluence and its decidability via critical pairs; critical pair (Definition 4.22, via most general unifiers of overlapping left-hand sides); Theorem 4.24/Corollary 4.25 (a (terminating) TRS is (locally) confluent iff all critical pairs are joinable); completion procedure `complete` (orienting normalized critical pairs into new rewrite rules, deferring non-orientable ones); interreduction (removing redundant/simplifiable equations, Métivier's result); associative–commutative (AC) unification/matching; ordered rewriting and unfailing completion (Bachmair–Dershowitz–Plaisted), which yields a complete equational proof procedure even without finding a canonical system : [[Equality-Reasoning|Link]]
- **4.8 Equality elimination** — predicate formulation of equational theories (e.g., group law via ternary predicate $P(x,y,z)$ for $x\cdot y = z$); equivalence elimination / "killer transformations" (Theorem 4.29, replacing $R(s,t)$ by $R^*[s,t]$ to drop equivalence axioms); Brand's S-modification (eliminating symmetry), T-modification (eliminating transitivity), and E-modification (flattening, eliminating congruence axioms); flat formula; Theorem 4.30 (flat quantifier-free $P \wedge E \wedge C$ satisfiable iff $P \wedge E$ satisfiable); the overall Brand transformation and `bmeson` : [[Equality-Reasoning|Link]]
- **4.9 Paramodulation** — demodulation (using unit equations as simplifying rewrite rules; incomplete); paramodulation inference rule (resolution-like rule combining an equation and a literal via unification into a subterm); refutation completeness of paramodulation with functional reflexive axioms (Theorem 4.31, simulating hyperresolution with equality axioms); simple reflexivity sufficing (Brand's result) and restrictions such as disallowing paramodulation into variables; the superposition method as a further refinement unifying equational and logical inference

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does replacing an arbitrary interpreted equality predicate with $\mathrm{eqaxioms}(\Delta)$ let a theorem prover for plain first-order logic handle equality soundly, and what is lost in efficiency compared to a dedicated equality-reasoning procedure like congruence closure?
2. Newman's lemma shows termination plus weak (local) confluence implies full confluence — why does this let Knuth–Bendix completion get away with checking only finitely many critical pairs rather than all possible pairs of equal terms?
3. What is the essential difference between congruence closure (deciding ground equational validity) and paramodulation (a general inference rule for first-order logic with equality), and why does the latter need functional reflexivity axioms or restrictions (e.g. no paramodulation into variables) to remain refutation complete?

### Chapter 5: Decidable problems (pp. 308–463)

**Summary:** Although first-order validity is undecidable in general (Church–Turing, proved in Chapter 7), many restricted classes of formulas and many theories of practical interest (linear arithmetic, real/complex algebra, rings, geometry) do admit decision procedures; the chapter develops a toolkit — prefix restrictions, the finite/small model property, and especially quantifier elimination — culminating in Gröbner bases, Wu's method for geometry, and the Nelson–Oppen/Shostak methods for combining decision procedures for different theories. : [[Combining-Decision-Procedures|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **5.1 The decision problem** — three related problems (confirm valid, confirm invalid, decide both); restricting formula syntax or restricting to a class of models as the two general routes to decidability. : [[Decidable-Theories-and-Quantifier-Elimination|Link]]
- **5.2 The AE fragment** — Herbrand's theorem and ground-instance bounds, AE subset ($\forall\ldots\exists\ldots$ prenex form) and dually the EA subset, `aedecide` (Skolemize, bound ground instances by constants, test propositional unsatisfiability of their conjunction). : [[Decidable-Theories-and-Quantifier-Elimination|Link]]
- **5.3 Miniscoping and the monadic fragment** — miniscoping (pushing quantifiers inward, converse of PNF) via `separate`/`pushquant`/`miniscope`; monadic formulas (only unary predicates, no functions); Wang's procedure `wang` (Wang 1960) extending `aedecide` via miniscoping. : [[Decidable-Theories-and-Quantifier-Elimination|Link]]
- **5.4 Syllogisms** — Aristotelian premiss forms A/E/I/O and the four figures; syllogisms as formulas in the monadic fragment, decidable via `aedecide`; discrepancy between the 15 syllogisms valid without existential import and the traditional 24 valid ones once "some S exists" hypotheses are added.
- **5.5 The finite model property** — finite model property for validity/satisfiability (Definition 5.1); Harrop's theorem (Theorem 5.2) that FMP formulas are decidable by interleaving proof search with finite-model enumeration (`decide_finite`, `limited_meson`, `decide_fmp`); small model property (Theorem 5.3, monadic formulas decidable at model size $2^k$); Bernays–Schönfinkel bound for AE; Ackermann's $\forall^n\exists\forall^m$ and Gödel's $\forall^n\exists\exists\forall^m$ prefix classes with FMP; undecidability of $\exists\exists\exists\forall$/$\exists\forall\exists$ prefixes (Surányi; Kahr–Moore–Wang); effect of adding equality (Ramsey's theorem, Goldfarb's refutation of Gödel's claim, Ackermann's equality result, Scott/Mortimer two-variable fragment). : [[Decidable-Theories-and-Quantifier-Elimination|Link]]
- **5.6 Quantifier elimination** — theory, axiomatizable/finitely axiomatizable, consistent, complete, decidable (Theorem 5.5: complete iff all models elementarily equivalent); quantifier elimination defined (reduction to a $T$-equivalent quantifier-free formula); `lift_qelim` architecture reducing full QE to eliminating $\exists x.\ \alpha_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha_n$; dense linear orders without endpoints (DLO) as worked example (`dlobasic`, Langford 1927), showing $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ are elementarily equivalent in this language. : [[Decidable-Theories-and-Quantifier-Elimination|Link]]
- **5.7 Presburger arithmetic** — linear integer arithmetic with divisibility predicates $D_k$; canonical linear term form; Cooper's algorithm (`minusinf`/$P_{-\infty}$, B-sets, `unitycoeff`, `cooper`) as an optimized alternative to Presburger's original method; natural-number version via relativization; Skolem arithmetic (multiplicative analogue); undecidability once both addition and multiplication (or general divisibility) are present; complexity (doubly exponential lower bound, Fischer–Rabin). : [[Decidable-Theories-and-Quantifier-Elimination|Link]]
- **5.8 The complex numbers** — canonical (Horner-form) polynomials; degree, root, splitting theorems culminating in algebraic closure of $\mathbb{C}$; pseudo-division; sign contexts and case-splitting; quantifier elimination for algebraically closed fields (`cqelim`/`complex_qelim`) via the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. : [[Decidable-Theories-and-Quantifier-Elimination|Link]]
- **5.9 The real numbers** — Tarski's original real quantifier elimination (generalizing Sturm's theorem), simplified by Seidenberg; sign matrices and the recursive sign-determination algorithm (`inferpsign`, `condense`, `inferisign`, `matrix`) underlying `real_qelim`; real closed fields; cylindrical algebraic decomposition (CAD) as the practical state of the art; Fourier–Motzkin elimination and linear programming for the purely linear fragment; doubly-exponential complexity in general (Davenport–Heintz). : [[Decidable-Theories-and-Quantifier-Elimination|Link]]
- **5.10 Rings, ideals and word problems** — (uniform/free) word problem for a class of algebras; ring axioms and characteristic; polynomial rings as finitely-supported coefficient functions with a monomial ordering; ideal $\mathrm{Id}_R\langle p_1,\ldots,p_n\rangle$; word problem for rings/torsion-free rings/integral domains reduced to ideal membership over $\mathbb{Z}$/$\mathbb{Q}$ (the (weak/strong) Nullstellensatz); field, field of fractions; Rabinowitsch trick (turning $\neg(q=0)$ into $\exists z.\ 1-qz=0$); monoid, monoid ring, and reduction of the abelian-group/monoid word problem to ideal membership. : [[Algebraic-Decision-Procedures|Link]]
- **5.11 Gröbner bases** — polynomial reduction as a rewriting relation, confluence via Newman's lemma; S-polynomials (syzygies) as the analogue of Knuth–Bendix critical pairs; Gröbner basis defined as a confluent generating set for an ideal; Buchberger's algorithm (completion-style, guaranteed to terminate by Dickson's lemma); `grobner_decide` combining Rabinowitsch trick with Gröbner bases as a fast alternative to real quantifier elimination for algebraic identities. : [[Algebraic-Decision-Procedures|Link]]
- **5.12 Geometric theorem proving** — coordinatizing geometric predicates (collinear, perpendicular, midpoint, etc.) as polynomial equations; quadrance and spread as square-root-free substitutes for length/angle; invariance under affine transformations; Wu's complex-coordinates trick (proving real geometric theorems via the more tractable algebra over $\mathbb{C}$); Wu's method (triangular form via pseudo-division, generating non-degeneracy conditions automatically); worked examples Simson's theorem and Pappus's theorem. : [[Algebraic-Decision-Procedures|Link]]
- **5.13 Combining decision procedures** — the "alien term" problem when extending a decidable theory; undecidability from adding even one predicate/function symbol (real+integer-hood, Presburger+squaring); Craig's interpolation theorem (and Robinson's consistency theorem) proved constructively via propositional interpolation lifted through Herbrand's theorem and Skolemization; Nelson–Oppen combination method for theories with disjoint signatures, using homogenization/purification, stably infinite theories, and (to avoid enumerating all equality arrangements) convex theories; Shostak's method (canonizer + solver) as a more efficient but less general alternative; modern SMT (lazy vs. eager, delayed theory combination). : [[Combining-Decision-Procedures|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. The chapter repeatedly gets decidability from either (a) a syntactic quantifier-prefix restriction or (b) an explicit quantifier-elimination algorithm — why do these two routes coincide for the AE fragment but diverge sharply for Presburger arithmetic (which needs infinitely many divisibility predicates to admit elimination at all)?
2. The Ackermann/Gödel prefix classes ($\forall^n\exists\forall^m$, $\forall^n\exists\exists\forall^m$) have the finite model property while the seemingly similar $\exists\forall\exists$ prefix does not — what does the strict-order counterexample reveal about why the finite model property fails exactly there, and why doesn't its failure automatically imply undecidability?
3. Wu's method and Tarski/Seidenberg real quantifier elimination both decide geometric theorems, but Wu's approach silently produces "sufficient, not necessary" non-degeneracy conditions via triangularization — in what sense is this a genuinely different (weaker but more efficient) notion of "proof" than a true quantifier-elimination equivalence, and how does the complex-coordinates trick let Gröbner-basis methods substitute for it?
4. Nelson–Oppen combination relies on stable infiniteness and (for efficiency) convexity to avoid enumerating all equality arrangements between shared variables — why does convexity fail for both linear integer arithmetic and real arithmetic with multiplication, and what does the resulting need for case-splitting cost in practice?

### Chapter 6: Interactive theorem proving (pp. 464–525)

**Summary:** The chapter turns from fully automatic proof search to interactive theorem proving, motivated by the limits of automation for real mathematics, and builds an LCF-style prover for first-order logic in OCaml: a Hilbert-style axiomatic core is implemented as an abstract type of theorems whose only constructors are sound inference rules, derived rules are layered on top to give propositional and first-order automation (tableaux by inference), and finally a tactic/goal framework supports both procedural and Mizar-inspired declarative proof styles. : [[Interactive-Theorem-Proving-and-the-LCF-Approach|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **6.1 Human-oriented methods** — human-oriented/heuristic theorem proving (programs designed to reason like a human, e.g. Newell and Simon's Logic Theorist, Gelerntner's geometry prover, Bledsoe's analysis heuristics, Boyer–Moore's NQTHM), contrasted with systematic algorithmic methods that ultimately proved more effective; proof planning (Bundy's reconstruction of NQTHM's methods).
- **6.2 Interactive provers and proof checkers** — semi-automated mathematics / SAM family (combining automatic logic routines with human-guided control), proof checker (AUTOMATH, Mizar: checks a supplied proof/proof sketch rather than searching automatically), Mizar's notion of an "obvious" inference (a fixed, precisely defined class of steps the system will accept without further justification), the LCF approach (two key ideas: (1) the prover is implemented inside an interactive general-purpose programming language so users can freely program new proof procedures, and (2) an abstract type `thm` of proven theorems whose only constructors are the approved primitive inference rules, guaranteeing soundness by construction), ML (meta-language, the language created for LCF, ancestor of OCaml). : [[Interactive-Theorem-Proving-and-the-LCF-Approach|Link]]
- **6.3 Proof systems for first-order logic** — proof system / provability relation $\Gamma \vdash p$ (defined inductively by permissible proof steps), sequent $\Gamma \to p$ (meaning "if all of $\Gamma$ hold then $p$ holds"), Hilbert (Frege) systems (axiom schemas plus very few inference rules), natural deduction (introduction/elimination rule for each connective, e.g. implication-introduction and -elimination, or-introduction/-elimination), sequent calculus (Gentzen: only introduction rules but both left and right versions), cut rule and the Hauptsatz (cut elimination: any proof with cut can be transformed to a cut-free one), cut-free proof as the basis of syntax-directed proof search (semantic tableaux, the inverse method). : [[Automated-First-Order-Theorem-Proving|Link1]], [[First-Order-Logic-Syntax-and-Semantics|Link2]], [[Propositional-Logic|Link3]]
- **6.4 LCF implementation of first-order logic** — the chosen Hilbert-style axiom system for first-order logic with equality, built from just $\{\bot,\Rightarrow,\forall\}$ plus definitional axioms for the other connectives, with two proper inference rules: modus ponens and generalization; Theorem 6.1 (soundness: $\vdash p$ implies $\models p$); OCaml `Proofsystem` module signature exposing `thm` as an abstract type with `modusponens`, `gen`, one function per axiom schema, and `concl`; `occurs_in` / `free_in` helper functions implementing the side conditions $x \notin \mathrm{FV}(p)$ and $x \notin \mathrm{FVT}(t)$ without an explicit free-variable-set representation. : [[Automated-First-Order-Theorem-Proving|Link1]], [[First-Order-Logic-Syntax-and-Semantics|Link2]]
- **6.5 Propositional derived rules** — derived rule / derived inference rule (an OCaml function built by composing primitive rules, e.g. `imp_refl` proving $\vdash p \Rightarrow p$, `imp_trans`, `imp_swap`, `iff_imp1`/`iff_imp2`, `imp_antisym`, ex falso quodlibet, contraposition, conjunction/negation properties); the technique of expressing propositional connectives via $\{\Rightarrow,\bot\}$ and treating $\neg p$ as $p \Rightarrow \bot$ so that only a small core of rules is needed. : [[Interactive-Theorem-Proving-and-the-LCF-Approach|Link]]
- **6.6 Proving tautologies by inference** — `lcfptab`, an inference-producing reimplementation of the propositional tableau procedure that, instead of merely asserting a contradiction, actually derives the theorem $p_1 \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow p_n \Rightarrow l_1 \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow l_m \Rightarrow \bot$; `eliminate_connective`/`expand_connective` (rewriting defined connectives to $\{\Rightarrow,\bot\}$ form via inference); `lcftaut` (proves any propositional tautology by refuting its negation and applying double-negation elimination). : [[Algebraic-Decision-Procedures|Link]]
- **6.7 First-order derived rules** — specialization (deriving $\vdash \forall x.\,P[x] \Rightarrow P[t]$ from more primitive congruence and existence axioms, following Tarski's approach), equality properties (`eq_sym`, `eq_trans`) derived from congruence, `icongruence` (derives $s=t \Rightarrow u[s]=u[t]$ by structural recursion), alpha-conversion (renaming a bound variable, needed because naive substitution into quantified formulas can capture variables), `isubst`/`subalpha`/`subspec` (recursive congruence rule for formulas, handling the quantifier case via a fresh variable to avoid capture), `ispec`/`spec` (the final derived specialization rule). : [[Interactive-Theorem-Proving-and-the-LCF-Approach|Link]]
- **6.8 First-order proof by inference** — lifting the full first-order tableau procedure (Section 3.10) to produce inference-justified theorems (`lcftab`/`lcfrefute`/`lcffol`); the key difficulty that instantiations and Skolem terms are not known until a refutation is found, solved by passing theorem-producing functions (parameterized by the eventual instantiation and Skolemization formula) through the continuation rather than theorems themselves; dynamic Skolemization and the resulting Skolem hypothesis $P[t_1,\ldots,t_n,f(t_1,\ldots,t_n)] \Rightarrow \forall y.\,P[t_1,\ldots,t_n,y]$, which is not logically valid but is retained as an extra antecedent and later eliminated; elimination of Skolem hypotheses using the drinker's principle (`elim_skolemvar`); Theorem 6.2 (completeness of the primitive rules for first-order logic without equality, realized constructively by `lcffol`); generalized provability from assumptions $\Gamma \vdash p$; Theorem 6.3 ($\Gamma \vdash p$ iff $\Gamma \models p$); Corollary 6.4 (the deduction theorem, $\Gamma \vdash \forall(p) \Rightarrow q$ iff $\Gamma \cup \{p\} \vdash q$). : [[Interactive-Theorem-Proving-and-the-LCF-Approach|Link]]
- **6.9 Interactive proof styles** — goal (a conclusion formula plus labelled hypotheses, corresponding to a theorem $p_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge p_n \Rightarrow q$) and the `goals` type (a list of goals plus a justification function mapping subgoal theorems to the parent theorem); tactic (a function `goals -> goals` that refines a goal, reversing a natural-deduction rule, e.g. `conj_intro_tac`, `forall_intro_tac`, `exists_intro_tac`, `imp_intro_tac`, `exists_elim_tac`, `disj_elim_tac`); tacticals (combinators on tactics analogous to imperative control constructs); procedural proof (a tactic sequence, imperative and hard to read without replaying it) versus declarative proof (Mizar-inspired style stating what is proved at each step, via `note`/`have`/`assume`/`fix`/`consider`/`take`/`cases`/`conclude`/`our thesis`/`qed`, with step-bridging automation constrained by explicitly cited hypotheses and block-structured local variables/assumptions); the finding–checking (proof search vs. proof verification) separation as a strategy for LCF efficiency; reflection principles / metatheoretic extensibility (extending a trusted core only with code proven correct using the existing system) as an alternative to full LCF-style implementation. : [[Propositional-Satisfiability-Algorithms|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does the LCF approach place the burden of soundness entirely on the *type discipline* (an abstract type `thm` with a fixed, small set of constructors) rather than on trusting a large body of proof-search code — and what specific move in Section 6.4 (deriving substitution instead of taking it as primitive) reflects that same philosophy?
2. In the first-order tableau-by-inference construction (Section 6.8), why can't Skolemization be soundly "undone" after the fact by direct inference, and what is the logical trick (retaining Skolem hypotheses as extra antecedents, then eliminating them via the drinker's principle) that makes proof reconstruction possible anyway?
3. What exactly distinguishes "procedural" (tactic-script) proof from "declarative" (Mizar-style) proof in this chapter's framework, given that both are ultimately implemented as functions transforming the same `goals` type — is the difference one of semantics/soundness or purely of programming style and readability?

### Chapter 7: Limitations (pp. 526–592)

**Summary:** This chapter turns from positive automation results to the inherent limits of formal proof and computation, proving Tarski's undefinability of truth, Gödel's first and second incompleteness theorems, and Church's theorem on the undecidability of first-order logic (with a constructive Robinson-arithmetic proof procedure), before surveying further limitative results and reflecting on what makes reasoning "logical." : [[Combining-Decision-Procedures|Link]]

**Key Definitions & Concepts by Section:**
- **7.1 Hilbert's programme** — constructive vs. nonconstructive proof (a proof is nonconstructive if it establishes existence via excluded middle or double-negation elimination without exhibiting a witness), intuitionism (Brouwer's rejection of the law of the excluded middle and principle of contradiction for infinite/undecided propositions), classical logic (traditional logic, so named in contrast to intuitionistic logic), metamathematics (treating formal proofs as concrete finite objects amenable to mathematical study), Hilbert's programme (justify infinitary/nonconstructive mathematics by proving, via finitary proof theory, that its concrete consequences are sound) : [[Limits-of-Automated-Reasoning|Link]]
- **7.2 Tarski's theorem on the undefinability of truth** — definable relation/set/function (an $n$-ary relation $R$ on domain $D$ is definable in language $L$ and interpretation $M$ if some formula with $n$ free variables picks out exactly $R$; a function is definable if its graph is), Gödel numbering (an injective mapping $\ulcorner p \urcorner = g(p)$ from formulas/terms to natural numbers, arithmetization of syntax), pairing function ($\langle x,y\rangle = (x+y)^2+x+1$, injective, used to encode finite lists), primitive recursive function (defined via $f(0)=a$, $f(S(n))=g(n,f(n))$ for definable $g$; shown definable in arithmetic), diagonalization / quasi-diagonalization (`diag`, `qdiag` — replacing occurrences of a variable/placeholder by a quoted copy of the whole string/formula, the mechanism for self-reference), fixpoint (diagonal) lemma (Lemma 7.3: for any arithmetical formula $P[x]$ there is a sentence $\phi$ with $\phi \Leftrightarrow P[\phi]$ true in $\mathbb{N}$), Tarski's theorem (Theorem 7.4: no formula in the language of arithmetic defines the set of Gödel numbers of true formulas) : [[Limits-of-Automated-Reasoning|Link]]
- **7.3 Incompleteness of axiom systems** — arithmetization of provability ($Pr_A(n)$, a definable — in fact $\Sigma_1$ — predicate expressing "$n$ is the Gödel number of a formula provable from $A$"), semantical incompleteness (some true formula is not provable) vs. unsoundness (some provable formula is not true), Theorem 7.5 / Corollary 7.6 (a weak form of Gödel's first incompleteness theorem: for any definable sound axiom set $A$, $Cn(A)$ is incomplete), Peano arithmetic $PA$ (basic axioms for successor, addition, multiplication, plus the induction schema, shown to be definable) : [[Limits-of-Automated-Reasoning|Link]]
- **7.4 Gödel's incompleteness theorem** — Gödel sentence $G$ (a self-referential sentence satisfying $G \Leftrightarrow \neg Pr_A(G)$, "asserts its own unprovability"), $\Delta_0$ formula (Definition 7.7: all quantifiers bounded, hence decidable by direct evaluation), $\Sigma_1$/$\Pi_1$/$\Sigma_n$/$\Pi_n$ formulas (classified by outermost pattern of unbounded quantifier alternations, existential/universal respectively), $\Sigma_1$-soundness / $\Pi_1$-soundness (aka 1-consistency: all provable $\Sigma_1$/$\Pi_1$ sentences are true), $\Sigma_1$-completeness / $\Pi_1$-completeness (all true $\Sigma_1$/$\Pi_1$ sentences are provable), $\omega$-consistency (a stronger, historically standard alternative hypothesis to $\Sigma_1$-soundness), Theorem 7.8 / Corollary 7.9–7.10 (sharp forms of Gödel's first incompleteness theorem: a consistent, $\Sigma_1$-definable, $\Sigma_1$-complete axiom set is incomplete, exhibiting the Gödel sentence explicitly) : [[Limits-of-Automated-Reasoning|Link]]
- **7.5 Definability and decidability** — Entscheidungsproblem (Hilbert's decision problem for first-order validity), Church–Turing thesis (identification of "effectively calculable" with Church's/Turing's formal notions), Turing machine (finite-state device operating on an unbounded tape, formalized in OCaml via `symbol`, `direction`, `tape`, `config`, and a `run` function), Turing-machine computability (Definition 7.11: total/partial function computable if some fixed program computes it), recursively enumerable (r.e.) / semicomputable / semidecidable set (Definition 7.12: domain of a partial computable function), the equivalence "a set is r.e. iff it is $\Sigma_1$-definable" (the key link between computability and definability), $\Delta_1$ (decidable: both $\Sigma_1$- and $\Pi_1$-definable; Theorem 7.13 characterizes decidable sets via r.e. set and its complement both being r.e.) : [[Limits-of-Automated-Reasoning|Link]]
- **7.6 Church's theorem** — Robinson arithmetic $Q$ (a finite, weak set of axioms for successor/addition/multiplication/order, proved $\Sigma_1$-complete via an explicit derived-rule proof procedure `sigma_prove`), Theorem 7.14 (removing finitely many axioms from an undecidable theory keeps it undecidable), Church's theorem (Theorem 7.15: the set of first-order logical validities, even restricted to the language of arithmetic, is not recursive/decidable) : [[Limits-of-Automated-Reasoning|Link]]
- **7.7 Further limitative results** — Goldbach conjecture and Fermat's last theorem as $\Pi_1$-sentences (illustrating the connection between Hilbert's programme and $\Pi_1$-truths), Gödel's second incompleteness theorem (a sufficiently strong consistent theory $A$ cannot prove its own consistency statement $Con(A) =_{def} \neg Pr_A(\bot)$), Löb's derivability conditions (three formal properties of $Pr_A$ sufficient to prove the second incompleteness theorem), Löb's theorem (if $\vdash Pr_A(S) \Rightarrow S$ then $\vdash S$), reflection principle (an added axiom, like a consistency statement, expressing trust in a system from "outside" it), Hilbert's tenth problem (decidability of Diophantine equation solvability; resolved negatively via the MRDP theorem that every r.e. relation is Diophantine, i.e. definable by a purely existentially-quantified polynomial equation), reduction class (a syntactic class of formulas to which validity of arbitrary formulas can be computably reduced, e.g. the $\exists^*\forall^n$ prefix class), Rosser construction (Rosser's sentence $R$, strengthening Gödel's theorem to require only simple consistency rather than $\Sigma_1$-soundness), essential undecidability (every consistent extension of the theory is undecidable) and strong undecidability (every compatible theory is undecidable) : [[Limits-of-Automated-Reasoning|Link]]
- **7.8 Retrospective: the nature of logic** — formal checkability of proofs as arguably the defining property of "logical" reasoning; discussion of whether first-order validity captures informal distinctions like analytic/synthetic or necessary/contingent : [[Limits-of-Automated-Reasoning|Link]]

**Key Questions:**
1. Why does Tarski's theorem (undefinability of truth) already force a choice between semantical incompleteness and unsoundness for any definable, sound axiomatization of arithmetic — before Gödel's sharper, syntactic construction is even introduced?
2. What is the precise role of the distinction between $\Sigma_1$-soundness (1-consistency) and mere consistency in Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, and how does the Rosser construction manage to weaken this hypothesis back down to simple consistency?
3. In what sense does Gödel's second incompleteness theorem actually refute Hilbert's programme, and in what sense does it leave room (via things like reflection principles or relative consistency proofs) for partial realizations of it?
