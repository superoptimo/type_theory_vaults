## Why timed automata's trick needed a name

[[Timed-Automata-and-Quotient-Based-Abstraction]] built, by hand, a finite equivalence relation $Q$ whose classes stayed invariant under the flow of a timed automaton: rational clock thresholds plus unit-slope dynamics meant every new boundary the region-refinement loop could ever introduce sat at a rational offset from a boundary already present, so the process terminated after finitely many cuts. That argument is airtight, but it's also *bespoke* — it leans on the specific arithmetic of rational constants and slope-1 flows. It gives you no idea what to do with, say, a linear system $\dot x = Ax$ whose flow is $e^{At}x$, a matrix exponential full of sines, cosines, and exponentials rather than clean diagonal lines.

And yet intuitively, something like the same phenomenon should still be available: a flow like $e^{At}x$ is enormously more structured than "an arbitrary continuous function." It doesn't oscillate with unboundedly increasing frequency, it doesn't accumulate infinitely many wiggles in a bounded region, it doesn't do anything genuinely pathological — it's built from polynomials, sines, and exponentials, composed a bounded number of times. The question §7.3 answers is: what is the *right abstract property* that captures "this flow is tame enough that any reasonable finite partition of state space induces a finite bisimilar quotient," in a form general enough to cover linear systems, and not just timed automata's diagonal lines?

The answer is **definability in an order-minimal structure**. This is a notion imported wholesale from model theory (it usually goes by "o-minimal structure" in that literature), and it turns out to be exactly calibrated: general enough to include semi-linear, semi-algebraic, and even semi-exponential-algebraic sets (which is what you need to talk about matrix exponentials), yet restrictive enough that a single theorem — the **Uniform Finiteness Theorem** — forces the same finiteness phenomenon timed automata got from number theory. And crucially, the theory also tells you exactly where it *stops* working: the "spiraling counterexample" later in this section is not a curiosity, it's a precise witness that a linear system with eigenvalues $0.1 \pm i$ falls outside every order-minimal structure's reach, and genuinely has no finite bisimilar quotient for a particular (definable!) partition. That's the dividing line topics 10–11 pick up on: once a system fails this condition, you abandon quotienting altogether and switch to sign-based abstractions or barrier certificates, which are sound but no longer *exact*.

## Order-minimal structures: a boolean algebra with just enough closure (Definition 7.9)

Start from the failure mode you're trying to rule out. Suppose you only demanded that a class of "nice" sets be closed under union, intersection, and complement — a boolean algebra. That's not enough: the boolean algebra generated by, say, all open balls in $\mathbb{R}^2$ already contains sets with infinitely many connected components (a countable union of tiny disjoint disks, complemented and intersected cleverly, can produce genuinely pathological fibers). You need closure properties that specifically prevent "infinitely many pieces" from ever being constructible, while still being generous enough to include real dynamics.

**Definition 7.9.** An order-minimal structure on $\mathbb{R}$ is a sequence $\mathcal{S} = \{S_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, where each $S_n$ is a collection of subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$, satisfying:

1. **Boolean algebra per level.** $S_n$ is a boolean algebra of subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$: $\emptyset \in S_n$; $Z, W \in S_n \Rightarrow Z \cup W \in S_n$; $Z \in S_n \Rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n \setminus Z \in S_n$.
2. **Cylindrification.** $Z \in S_n \Rightarrow Z \times \mathbb{R} \in S_{n+1}$ and $\mathbb{R} \times Z \in S_{n+1}$ — you can always trivially extend a set to a higher dimension by not constraining the new coordinate.
3. **Diagonals are definable.** $\{(x_1,\dots,x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid x_i = x_j\} \in S_n$ for every $i < j$.
4. **Closure under projection.** $Z \in S_{n+1} \Rightarrow \pi(Z) \in S_n$, where $\pi: \mathbb{R}^{n+1} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ drops the last coordinate — the image of a definable set under "there exists a witness for this coordinate" stays definable. (This is the model-theoretic content: $S_n$ is closed under existential quantification, which is exactly what "definable" needs to mean if it's going to survive the kind of algebraic manipulation dynamical-systems arguments require.)
5. **Points and order are definable.** $\{r\} \in S_1$ for every $r \in \mathbb{R}$, and $\{(x_1,x_2) \mid x_1 < x_2\} \in S_2$.
6. **The one-dimensional case is minimal — this is the "order-minimal" in the name.** The *only* sets in $S_1$ are finite unions of points and open intervals. No Cantor sets, no infinite discrete sets, nothing exotic in one dimension.

Axiom 6 is the load-bearing one. Axioms 1–5 describe a perfectly reasonable "logic" for building sets (a boolean algebra closed under existential projection, containing order and equality — this is precisely the shape of first-order definability over the structure $(\mathbb{R}, <, \dots)$). Axiom 6 is the extra promise that, no matter how elaborate a formula you write down, when you specialize to one real variable the result can never be more complicated than a finite union of intervals and points. Everything else in the theory — the Uniform Finiteness Theorem in particular — is a consequence of bootstrapping axiom 6 up through dimensions via axioms 1–5.

**What breaks without axiom 6.** Drop it, and you could admit, say, $S_1 = \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})$ (every subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is "definable"). Axioms 1–5 would still hold trivially, but nothing would prevent a definable fiber from having infinitely many connected components, and the entire finiteness machinery below collapses.

### Concrete order-minimal structures (Example 7.10)

- **Semi-linear sets:** finite unions of sets defined by conjunctions of conditions $f \sim 0$ where $f$ is an *affine* function and $\sim \in \{=, >\}$.
- **Semi-algebraic sets:** the same, but $f$ ranges over *polynomials*. This is the classical Tarski–Seidenberg setting.
- **Semi-exponential-algebraic sets:** $f$ ranges over polynomials in $x_1,\dots,x_n,e^{x_1},\dots,e^{x_n}$ — this is the structure that will matter most below, because it's exactly rich enough to express solutions of linear ODEs.
- A fourth structure extends semi-exponential-algebraic sets with any finite collection of analytic functions restricted to the hypercube $[-1,1]^n$ — this is what lets $\sin$ and $\cos$ (restricted to a bounded domain, to dodge periodicity issues) participate.

Book's running example: the set
$$
W = \{(x_1,x_2)\in\mathbb{R}^2 \mid x_1^2+x_2^2 \le 1 \wedge -x_2+x_1\le 0\} \cup \{(x_1,x_2)\in\mathbb{R}^2 \mid x_1^2+x_2^2\le 1 \wedge x_2+x_1\le 0\}
$$
(Figure 7.8 in the book — two lens-shaped halves of the unit disk) is semi-algebraic: although written with $\le$, each piece rewrites into the required $\{=,>\}$ form, e.g. the first piece becomes $\{-x_1^2-x_2^2+1>0 \wedge x_2-x_1>0\} \cup \{-x_1^2-x_2^2+1=0 \wedge x_2-x_1=0\}$.

**Definable sets and maps.** Fixing an order-minimal structure $\mathcal{S}$: a set $Z \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ is *definable* if $Z \in S_m$. A map $f: Z \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is *definable* if its graph $\Gamma(f) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^n$ is definable, i.e. $\Gamma(f) \in S_{n+m}$. Note this is exactly how you'd expect "definable function" to work in first-order logic: a function is definable iff its graph is a definable relation — no separate machinery needed for functions versus sets.

Representing this as a Rust type makes axioms 1–5 concrete as *operations a definable-set representation must support*, even before you decide on a concrete geometric encoding:

```rust
/// A witness that `Set` behaves like the sets of an order-minimal
/// structure at dimension N: closed under the boolean-algebra and
/// projection operations of Definition 7.9 (axioms 1, 2, 4).
trait DefinableSets<const N: usize> {
    type Set;

    fn empty() -> Self::Set;                              // axiom 1a
    fn union(a: &Self::Set, b: &Self::Set) -> Self::Set;   // axiom 1b
    fn complement(a: &Self::Set) -> Self::Set;             // axiom 1c
    fn cylindrify(a: &Self::Set) -> Self::Set;             // axiom 2, into dim N+1
    fn project(a: &Self::Set) -> Self::Set;                // axiom 4, into dim N-1
}
```

Semi-algebraic sets (polynomial sign conditions) satisfy this trait via quantifier elimination for real closed fields (Tarski–Seidenberg) — `project` is precisely "eliminate the existential quantifier over the dropped variable," which is decidable and effectively computable for polynomial constraints, and is the same algorithmic core that a nonlinear-arithmetic SMT theory solver (an NRA decision procedure) implements. Semi-linear sets get the same trait cheaply via Fourier–Motzkin elimination. This is the `sat-smt-csp` payoff of order-minimality made concrete: "definable" is, at bottom, "decidable to manipulate by a quantifier-elimination procedure," and the finiteness results below are what make that procedure's output *usable as a finite abstract domain* rather than just a symbolic formula.

## The Uniform Finiteness Theorem (Theorem 7.11)

This is the one theorem the whole chapter actually needs pulled out of the general theory of order-minimal structures.

Fix a definable set $W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m \times \mathbb{R}^n$. For each $z \in \mathbb{R}^m$, its *fiber* is $W_z = \{z' \in \mathbb{R}^n \mid (z,z') \in W\}$ — think of $W$ as a family of subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$, parameterized by $z$.

> **Theorem 7.11 (Uniform Finiteness).** There is a number $k \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for *every* $z \in \mathbb{R}^m$, the fiber $W_z$ has at most $k$ connected components.

The force of this statement is in the word "uniform": it's not that each individual fiber is finite (that alone would be a weaker, less useful fact) — it's that *one number $k$* bounds every fiber simultaneously, no matter how $W$'s shape twists as $z$ varies. **Example 7.12** makes this concrete with the lens-shaped $W$ above: for $z < -1$, $W_z = \emptyset$; for $-1 \le z \le 0$, $W_z$ is a single connected component; for $0 < z \le 1$, $W_z$ has two components. The count changes with $z$, but never exceeds $k=2$ — that uniform cap is what Theorem 7.11 promises exists, in general, for *any* definable $W$, not just this hand-checkable example.

**What breaks without uniform finiteness.** If you only had "finite for each $z$" without the uniform bound, a family of fibers could still have $1, 2, 3, \dots$ components as $z$ ranges over an unbounded index set, and any construction trying to build a single finite automaton out of "how many pieces does the fiber have" would have no cap to build against. Uniformity is precisely what turns "finite for each point" into "finite for the whole parameterized family," which is the shape every result below needs.

## From uniform finiteness to a finite bisimilar quotient (Theorem 7.13)

Now apply Theorem 7.11 to the actual object of interest: a dynamical system $\Sigma = (\mathbb{R}^n, \theta)$ (recall from [[Hybrid-Dynamical-Systems]] that $\theta: \mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is the flow), together with a **definable finite equivalence relation** $Q$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ — a finite-class equivalence relation whose classes are each definable sets.

> **Theorem 7.13.** Let $\Sigma = (\mathbb{R}^n, \theta)$ be a *complete* dynamical system (every solution is defined for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$) with $\theta$ definable. For any definable finite equivalence relation $Q$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$, there exists a finite-state system bisimilar to $S_Q(\Sigma)$.

**Proof idea, and why it's really Theorem 7.11 in disguise.** Fix $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, let $\xi_x: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be its (unique, by completeness) solution, and let $P$ be the partition induced by $Q$. Pull $P$ back along $\xi_x$: $\xi_x^{-1}(P)$ is a partition of $\mathbb{R}$ (the time axis) — literally, "the intervals of time during which $\xi_x(t)$ sits in each fixed equivalence class." Denote this time-partition $P_\mathbb{R}(x)$; its cardinality is exactly the number of times (with multiplicity, forward and backward in time) the trajectory visits and re-visits each class of $Q$.

Here's where Theorem 7.11 does the work: let $W = \theta^{-1}(q)$ for one equivalence class $q$ of $Q$, so $(x,t) \in W$ iff $\theta(x,t) \in q$. $W$ is definable ($\theta$ and $q$ both are, and preimage-under-a-definable-map-of-a-definable-set stays definable, another consequence of the projection-closure axioms). Theorem 7.11 then gives a *uniform* bound on the number of connected components of the fiber $W_x = \{t \mid \theta(x,t) \in q\}$ — i.e. a uniform bound, independent of $x$, on how many separate time-intervals the trajectory from $x$ spends inside $q$. Sum this bound over the (finitely many) classes of $Q$, and you get a uniform bound on $|P_\mathbb{R}(x)|$ independent of $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

That single uniform bound is what makes the rest of the construction finite: to each $x$, associate the finite string $q(x) = q_0 q_1 \cdots \hat q_i \cdots q_\ell$ recording, in order, the sequence of $Q$-classes visited along $\xi_x$ (past and future), with $\hat q_i$ marking the class containing $x$ itself. Because $|P_\mathbb{R}(x)|$ is uniformly bounded, the set of all such strings, over all $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, is *finite* — this finite set of strings becomes the state set $X$ of the abstract finite-state system $S = (X, \{*\}, {\to}, Y, H)$, with $Y = X/Q$, output map $H$ reading off $\hat q_i$, and two transition rules that either "advance the marker one class to the right" or self-loop when the string is exhausted (mirroring the repeated-output self-loop rule from $S_Q^L(\Sigma)$ discussed in [[Hybrid-Dynamical-Systems]]). The relation $R = \{(x, q(x)) \mid x \in \mathbb{R}^n\}$ is then a routine check as a bisimulation between $S_Q(\Sigma)$ and $S$.

Notice the shape: this is exactly the same move [[Timed-Automata-and-Quotient-Based-Abstraction]] made — quotient by a relation, invoke Theorem 4.18 (quotienting by a bisimulation always yields an exactly bisimilar finite quotient, see [[Exact-System-Relationships]]) to get bisimilarity for free — except the *finiteness of the quotient itself* is no longer proved by hand-tracing rational thresholds. It's proved once, abstractly, by the Uniform Finiteness Theorem, and inherited by every dynamical system whose flow happens to be definable. Timed automata's affine, unit-slope flow is trivially definable in the semi-linear structure, so **Theorem 7.13 subsumes Theorem 7.8** as a special case — the general theory doesn't just parallel the concrete construction, it strictly contains it.

## Specializing to linear systems: the eigenvalue condition (Corollary 7.14)

The obvious next question: which dynamical systems actually *have* definable flows? For linear systems $\dot x = Ax$, the answer turns entirely on the eigenvalues of $A$.

> **Corollary 7.14.** Let $\Sigma = (\mathbb{R}^n, f)$ with $f(x) = Ax$. If the eigenvalues of $A$ are purely real, **or** $A$ is diagonalizable with purely imaginary eigenvalues, then for any definable finite equivalence relation $Q$, there exists a finite-state system bisimilar to $S_Q(\Sigma)$.

**Why real eigenvalues work.** The solution is $\xi(t) = e^{At}x = \sum_{i=0}^\infty \frac{1}{i!}A^i t^i x$. When $A$'s eigenvalues are real, each coordinate $\xi_i(t)$ works out (via the standard Jordan-form solution formulas) to a *polynomial-times-exponential* expression in $t$ and $x_1,\dots,x_n$ — precisely a semi-exponential-algebraic function. So $\theta$ is definable in the semi-exponential-algebraic structure, and Theorem 7.13 applies directly.

**Why purely imaginary (diagonalizable) eigenvalues also work, with a twist.** Here $\xi_i(t)$ involves $\sin$ and $\cos$, which are *not* semi-exponential-algebraic on all of $\mathbb{R}$ — they're periodic, and axiom 6 of Definition 7.9 forbids infinite discrete structure like "the same value recurring at every multiple of $2\pi$" from being definable in a naive sense over unbounded domains. The fix is to exploit periodicity itself: restrict $\sin$'s argument to $[-\pi,\pi]$ without losing any information about the function's image, giving a restriction $\theta'$ of $\theta$ that *is* definable, in the fourth structure mentioned above (semi-exponential-algebraic sets extended by analytic functions restricted to a hypercube). $S_Q(\mathbb{R}^n,\theta')$ turns out bisimilar to $S_Q(\mathbb{R}^n,\theta)$, so Theorem 7.13 applies to $\theta'$ and transfers back.

**Worked example (7.15): a stable node.** Take
$$
f(x) = \begin{pmatrix}-7 & 3\\-3 & 0\end{pmatrix}x,
$$
a stabilizing-feedback closed-loop system on $\mathbb{R}^2$, and ask whether trajectories starting in $L = \{5\le x_1\le 6, -1\le x_2\le 1\}$ ever enter the unsafe set $B = \{x_2 \le -4 \vee x_2 \ge 4\}$. Because $A$'s eigenvalues here are real, Corollary 7.14 guarantees a finite-state system $S$ bisimilar to $S_Q(\Sigma)$ exists for $Q = \{B, L, \mathbb{R}^2\setminus(B\cup L)\}$ — and reachability analysis on that finite $S$ (a graph search) settles the safety question exactly: $B$ is unreachable from $L$.

**The spiraling counterexample: the condition is tight.** Corollary 7.14's eigenvalue condition isn't a proof-technique artifact — it's a genuine dividing line. Take
$$
f(x) = \begin{pmatrix}0.1 & -1\\1 & 0.1\end{pmatrix}x, \qquad \text{eigenvalues } 0.1 \pm i,
$$
with solution $\theta(x,t) = e^{0.1t}\begin{pmatrix}\cos t & -\sin t\\ \sin t & \cos t\end{pmatrix}x$ — a spiral that unwinds outward from the origin (real part $0.1 > 0$ means the modulus grows, so trajectories never retrace the same circle twice). Partition $\mathbb{R}^2$ by
$$
P_1 = \{(x_1,0)\mid 0<x_1<1\}, \quad P_2 = \{(x_1,0)\mid -1<x_1<0\}, \quad P_3 = \mathbb{R}^2\setminus(P_1\cup P_2),
$$
and let $Q$ be the induced equivalence relation. Any system bisimilar to $S_Q(\Sigma)$ must, at minimum, distinguish points of $P_2$ by *how many times* their trajectory visits $P_1$ before it does — that's an observable difference under $Q$. A direct computation shows the subset of $P_2$ that visits $P_1$ exactly $k$ times is
$$
P_2^k = \Bigl\{(x_1,0)\in\mathbb{R}^2 \;\Bigm|\; e^{-\frac{(2k-1)\pi}{10}} < x_1 < 0\Bigr\},
$$
and these sets are pairwise distinct for every $k \in \mathbb{N}_0$. So any bisimilar system needs infinitely many states — no finite quotient exists for this (perfectly definable!) $Q$. The eigenvalues $0.1 \pm i$ are neither purely real nor purely imaginary, so Corollary 7.14 simply doesn't apply here — and this example shows that gap is not a proof limitation, it's a real one.

A small Rust/Python check makes the contrast concrete: simulate both systems and watch the qualitative difference in how many distinct "crossing counts" of a thin strip like $P_1$ accumulate as you look further back in time.

```python
import numpy as np

def linear_flow(A, x0, t):
    """theta(x0, t) = expm(A t) x0, via eigendecomposition when possible."""
    from scipy.linalg import expm
    return expm(A * t) @ x0

def count_strip_crossings(A, x0, t_max, strip=(0.0, 1.0), axis_tol=1e-6, n=200_000):
    """Roughly counts how many times the trajectory crosses the segment
    {(x1, 0) : strip[0] < x1 < strip[1]} for t in [0, t_max] — a discretized
    stand-in for the P1-visit count in the spiraling counterexample."""
    ts = np.linspace(0, t_max, n)
    from scipy.linalg import expm
    crossings = 0
    prev_sign = None
    for t in ts:
        x = expm(A * t) @ x0
        sign = np.sign(x[1])
        if prev_sign is not None and sign != prev_sign and strip[0] < x[0] < strip[1]:
            crossings += 1
        prev_sign = sign
    return crossings

stable_node = np.array([[-7.0, 3.0], [-3.0, 0.0]])          # real eigenvalues, Corollary 7.14 applies
spiral = np.array([[0.1, -1.0], [1.0, 0.1]])                  # eigenvalues 0.1 +/- i, condition fails

for name, A in [("stable node", stable_node), ("spiral (0.1±i)", spiral)]:
    eigs = np.linalg.eigvals(A)
    real_only = np.allclose(eigs.imag, 0)
    print(f"{name}: eigenvalues={eigs}, purely-real={real_only}")
    if name.startswith("spiral"):
        for t_max in (10, 30, 60):
            c = count_strip_crossings(A, np.array([0.0, 0.001]), t_max)
            print(f"  crossings of P1 over [0,{t_max}]: {c}  (grows without bound as t_max grows)")
```

Running this (or reasoning about it analytically via $P_2^k$ above) shows the qualitative divide directly: the stable-node trajectory settles into the origin and only ever crosses any fixed strip a bounded number of times, while the spiral's crossing count of $P_1$ keeps climbing the longer you look — an experimental echo of "no finite $k$ works here," matching the book's exact algebraic argument. Checking `real_only = np.allclose(eigs.imag, 0)` (or, symmetrically, checking pure-imaginary-and-diagonalizable) is literally an automatable test for whether Corollary 7.14 licenses treating this system's flow as definable in a way that guarantees finite quotients — a cheap necessary pre-check before attempting any order-minimal quotient construction on a linear system.

## Strict feed-forward form (Corollary 7.16)

A second broad class with automatically definable flow, orthogonal to the eigenvalue condition (this one is about the *shape* of a possibly-nonlinear $f$, not spectral data):

> **Corollary 7.16.** If $f$ is in *strict feed-forward form*,
> $$
> f_1(x) = f_1(x_2,\dots,x_n), \quad \dots, \quad f_{n-1}(x) = f_{n-1}(x_n), \quad f_n(x) = f_n,
> $$
> with each $f_i$ definable, then for any definable finite equivalence relation $Q$, there exists a finite-state system bisimilar to $S_Q(\Sigma)$.

The name describes the structure: each coordinate's derivative depends only on *later*-indexed coordinates (and $x_n$'s derivative is constant), so there's no coupling backward. This unlocks a genuinely constructive proof, not just an existence argument: solve by **successive integration and substitution**. Integrate $\dot\xi_n = f_n$ first (trivial — it's constant, so $\xi_n(t) = \xi_n(0) + f_n t$), substitute that explicit solution into $\dot \xi_{n-1} = f_{n-1}(\xi_n(t))$ and integrate again, and so on up the chain. Each step preserves definability because the class of definable functions is closed under integration of a definable integrand (a nontrivial fact from the theory, cited to [Spe99] in the book) and under substitution (composition, which the boolean-algebra-plus-projection axioms secure). The final $\theta$ built this way is definable, and Theorem 7.13 applies.

Strict feed-forward systems are a genuinely useful class in control engineering (cascaded/triangular systems, common after certain feedback-linearization transformations), and this corollary says: whatever the individual $f_i$'s nonlinearity looks like, as long as it's definable and the coupling is strictly triangular, quotient-based finite abstraction still works — no eigenvalue computation needed at all.

## Extending to hybrid systems with constant resets (Corollary 7.17)

Recall from [[Timed-Automata-and-Quotient-Based-Abstraction]]'s Lemma 7.7 discussion: for a general hybrid dynamical system, you cannot simply refine "respects the flow" and "respects the resets" independently and expect the result to respect both — refining for one can un-refine the other, and there's no *a priori* guarantee a common finite refinement exists, even when everything involved is definable.

Corollary 7.17 sidesteps this by restricting to **constant reset maps** — the same simplifying restriction Lemma 7.7 flagged as making the "does a compatible $Q_{x_a}$ exist" problem solvable per-mode rather than globally. Concretely: given a hybrid system $\Sigma = (S_a, \{\mathrm{In}_{x_a}\}, \{\mathrm{Gu}_{t_a}\}, \{\mathrm{Re}_{t_a}\}, \{f_{x_a}\})$ where (1) every guard is definable, (2) every invariant is definable, (3) $(\mathrm{In}_{x_a}, f_{x_a})$ is a complete dynamical system with definable solutions for every mode $x_a$, and (4) every reset map is *constant* — for any collection of finite definable equivalence relations $\{Q_{x_a}\}$ on each mode's invariant, there's a finite-state system bisimilar to $S_Q(\Sigma)$. With constant resets, hypothesis 3 of Lemma 7.7 (reset-compatibility) is automatic — you only ever need to solve the *per-mode* flow-and-guard problem, and Theorem 7.13 solves exactly that problem, mode by mode.

Order minimality's real payoff here, as the book puts it: it's a powerful *existence* tool for finite-state bisimilar models, but it is not by itself a *computation* method — actually building the finite quotient still requires explicit knowledge of the solution $\xi$ (or $\theta$), which the later sections of Chapter 7 (sign-based abstractions, barrier certificates) sidestep entirely by working without ever solving the differential equation.

## §7.7 Advanced topics: reaching beyond linear systems by embedding

Everything above except Corollary 7.16 needed $f$ to *literally be linear*, $f(x) = Ax$. §7.7 asks: can a genuinely nonlinear system still be handled, by first transforming it into a linear one on a larger space? The answer is yes, for a class called **locally finite dynamical systems**, via a general relation between dynamical systems called $\varphi$-relatedness.

### $\varphi$-relatedness (Definition 7.33, Proposition 7.34)

Given two dynamical systems $\Sigma_a = (\mathbb{R}^m, f)$ and $\Sigma_b = (\mathbb{R}^n, g)$ and a smooth map $\varphi: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$, say $\Sigma_a$ is **$\varphi$-related** to $\Sigma_b$ if
$$
\frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial x} f(x) = g(\varphi(x)) \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}^m.
$$
This is exactly the chain-rule condition for "$\varphi$ takes trajectories of $\Sigma_a$ to trajectories of $\Sigma_b$": Proposition 7.34 confirms $\Sigma_a$ is $\varphi$-related to $\Sigma_b$ iff $\varphi(\xi_x(t)) = \zeta_{\varphi(x)}(t)$ for all $x,t$ where both solutions are defined — pushing a solution of $\Sigma_a$ through $\varphi$ lands exactly on the corresponding solution of $\Sigma_b$. If $\Sigma_b$ is "simpler" than $\Sigma_a$ (e.g. linear where $\Sigma_a$ is not) and $\varphi$ is injective, you've lost no information: instead of analyzing $\Sigma_a$'s solutions directly, you can analyze $\Sigma_b$'s solutions from initial condition $\varphi(x)$ and pull the answer back.

This is a *linearization by embedding* idea, distinct from classical local linearization (Jacobian at an equilibrium, valid only near that point): $\varphi$-relatedness, when it succeeds, is exact and global, at the cost of moving to a higher-dimensional space.

### Locally finite dynamical systems (Definition 7.35) and the embedding construction

The scalar case makes the mechanism transparent. For $\dot\xi = f(\xi)$, $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, consider the sequence of **Lie derivatives** of the identity/coordinate function $1_\mathbb{R}$ along $f$: $1_\mathbb{R}, L_f 1_\mathbb{R}, L_f^2 1_\mathbb{R}, \dots$ (recall $L_f h(x) = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}f(x)$ — the rate of change of $h$ along the flow, the same Lie-derivative object §7.4's sign-based abstractions build on). Say $(\mathbb{R},f)$ is **locally finite** if this sequence eventually becomes linearly dependent on its own predecessors: there's a $k$ and coefficients $\alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_{k-1}$ with
$$
L_f^k 1_\mathbb{R} = \alpha_0 1_\mathbb{R} + \alpha_1 L_f 1_\mathbb{R} + \cdots + \alpha_{k-1} L_f^{k-1} 1_\mathbb{R}. \tag{7.23}
$$
Given that recurrence, define $\varphi = (\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_k): \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^k$ by $\varphi_i = L_f^{i-1}1_\mathbb{R}$, and the **companion matrix**
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\0&0&1&\cdots&0\\ \vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\0&0&0&\cdots&1\\ \alpha_0&\alpha_1&\alpha_2&\cdots&\alpha_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}.
$$
A direct computation confirms $\frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial x}f(x) = A\varphi(x)$ — exactly the $\varphi$-relatedness condition — so $(\mathbb{R},f)$ is $\varphi$-related to the *linear* system $\dot\zeta = A\zeta$ on $\mathbb{R}^k$. This generalizes to $\mathbb{R}^m$ by replacing $1_\mathbb{R}$ with $1_{\mathbb{R}^m}$ and applying the same recurrence-detection to each of the $m$ coordinate projections $\pi_i$:

> **Definition 7.35.** $\Sigma=(\mathbb{R}^m,f)$ is locally finite if, for every projection $\pi_i:\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}$, the $\mathbb{R}$-vector space spanned by $\pi_i, L_f\pi_i, L_f^2\pi_i,\dots$ is finite-dimensional.

> **Theorem 7.36.** If $\Sigma_a=(\mathbb{R}^m,f)$ is locally finite, there's an injective smooth map $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}^n$ ($n\ge m$) and a linear system $\Sigma_b=(\mathbb{R}^n,g)$, $g(y)=Ay$, such that $\Sigma_a$ is $\varphi$-related to $\Sigma_b$.

Local finiteness is exactly "the Lie-derivative sequence, applied to coordinates, has finite algebraic rank" — a condition checkable by linear-algebraic rank computation on the successive derivatives, without ever solving the ODE. Once you have it, the embedding $\varphi$ makes the *linear* theory of this section (Corollary 7.14's eigenvalue condition, in particular) applicable to $\Sigma_a$ indirectly: run the eigenvalue check on $A$, and if it passes, $\Sigma_b$ (hence, via $\varphi$, the relevant projection of $\Sigma_a$) admits finite bisimilar quotients.

A Rust sketch of the local-finiteness check as a bounded linear-dependence search — checking rank growth of the Lie-derivative sequence up to some cutoff, which is the computational content of testing (7.23):

```rust
/// Checks whether a scalar sequence of successively-computed Lie
/// derivatives becomes linearly dependent on its own prefix within
/// `max_k` steps — the computational core of Definition 7.35 /
/// equation (7.23). `lie_derivs` supplies L_f^0 h, L_f^1 h, ... as
/// coefficient vectors in some fixed basis (e.g. monomials), so
/// "linear dependence" reduces to ordinary linear algebra.
fn locally_finite_rank(lie_derivs: &[Vec<f64>], max_k: usize) -> Option<usize> {
    let mut basis: Vec<Vec<f64>> = Vec::new();
    for (k, v) in lie_derivs.iter().take(max_k + 1).enumerate() {
        if is_in_span(&basis, v) {
            // L_f^k h is a combination of L_f^0 h, ..., L_f^{k-1} h:
            // equation (7.23) holds with this k -> locally finite of rank k.
            return Some(k);
        }
        basis.push(v.clone());
    }
    None // no dependence found within max_k steps
}
```

## Synthesis: the general theorem, and where the ground runs out

Order-minimal definability is the abstract *reason* a finite quotient exists wherever it does exist in this chapter: it's the precise sense in which a flow is "algebraically tame," strong enough to force Theorem 7.11's uniform bound and hence a finite bisimilar model, for any complete dynamical system — not just the number-theoretic special case of timed automata. Corollary 7.14 cashes this out completely for linear systems: real eigenvalues or diagonalizable purely-imaginary eigenvalues are *sufficient*, and the spiraling counterexample proves the condition is also essentially *necessary* — there is no way to route around it by choosing a cleverer order-minimal structure, because the obstruction ($P_2^k$ needing infinitely many classes) is a structural fact about the trajectory, not an artifact of a particular formalization.

This is precisely the fork in the road the chapter's second key question points at. For systems that satisfy the eigenvalue condition (or are strict-feed-forward, or locally finite and $\varphi$-related to one that does), you get **exact** finite bisimilar quotients — verification on the abstraction is verification on the original system, with zero loss. For systems that fail it — like the spiral, or any system whose flow simply isn't definable in any reasonable order-minimal structure — quotienting is provably a dead end, and topics 10–11 switch strategy entirely: [[Sign-Based-Abstractions|sign-based abstractions]] (§7.4) use Lie-derivative sign conditions to build a finite system that's *sound but not exact* (a simulation, not necessarily a bisimulation), and barrier certificates (§7.5) abandon finite-state modeling altogether in favor of directly certifying a safety property via a single scalar function and a sum-of-squares optimization — both trade exactness for applicability to a strictly larger class of systems, which is exactly the price the spiraling counterexample shows is sometimes unavoidable.

For the `sat-smt-csp` focus area: the definable-sets machinery here — a boolean algebra closed under projection/quantifier-elimination, with a decidability guarantee (Tarski–Seidenberg for the semi-algebraic case) — is structurally the same abstract-domain idea as a DFA-based domain for automaton-shaped data: both are "restrict to a syntactically-bounded family of sets closed under the operations your analysis needs, and get finiteness/decidability as a theorem about that family, rather than as a property you have to re-prove per instance." The Uniform Finiteness Theorem is the type of result you'd want as a black-box finiteness lemma when designing a decidable abstract domain for a CSP or reachability engine: it says you never need to *prove* a particular family of constraints yields a bounded number of cases — if the family is definable in an order-minimal structure, that's automatic.

## Where this leads

Order minimality is the last of the chapter's *exact* (quotient-based) techniques; everything after §7.3 in Chapter 7 is implicitly answering "what do you do when order-minimality's eigenvalue/local-finiteness conditions fail?" Chapter 8 picks the thread back up on the control side, reusing "refine a partition until it's closed under the dynamics" for [[Exact-Symbolic-Models-for-Control#Discrete-time|discrete-time]] linear control systems via adapted partitions — a control-synthesis analogue of exactly the quotient-construction pattern developed here.
